## Abstract

Protein coding variants span the gamut of structural and functional effects on proteins, and they have similarly heterogeneous effects on traits. Their functional effects can be predicted and categorized, particularly using machine learning models, and integrating these predictors benefits gene discovery. However, it remains unclear how well known structural features and predicted functional scores explain the heterogeneous effects of protein coding variants on human complex traits. We introduce the Kernel Allelic Series Model (KASM), which integrates multiple functional annotations to form a kernel describing how allelic effects within a gene covary. We apply it to exome data from 26 traits and 786,871 individuals from UK Biobank and All of Us, integrating 35 annotations describing protein structure and predicting allelic effects. We estimate that rare missense heritability is at least 1.27±0.09% on average, 1.83±0.11% under our most flexible model, exceeding pLoF heritability (0.87±0.05%) and far exceeding missense burden heritability as previously estimated (0.54±0.04%). AlphaMissense is the single most enriched missense predictor, but a composite score doubles its enrichment. A series of structural features is enriched for heritability, from amino acid chemical features and structural motifs to residue contacts and protein-protein interaction interfaces. Examining pairs of variants, we find that most missense effects are modestly correlated, but an exception is when residues are close in 3D structure but distant in 1D sequence; such pairs also covary across species. Finally, we analyze individual genes and identify gene-specific enrichments, like a 95-fold enrichment among PIEZO1 hydrogen bond residues, that nominate functional hypotheses.


## Introduction

Rare protein-coding variants are a major driver of gene discovery for common diseases and complex traits 1 , 2 , motivating the study of their functional effects. These effects are diverse, especially for missense variants, affecting protein stability 3 , protein-protein interactions 4 , post-translational modifications 5 , splicing 6 , and dosage 7 . Heterogeneity presents a statistical challenge: alleles with the most deleterious effects are extremely rare, and their rarity seemingly necessitates aggregation, yet effect-size heterogeneity dilutes aggregated burden statistics 8 , 9 . For this reason, the total contribution of missense variants to complex-trait heritability is unclear, and the interpretation of gene-aggregated missense associations is clouded by a lack of insight into the functional effects of individual alleles.

An opportunity exists to address this challenge by leveraging recent progress in machine learning and experimental approaches to protein structure and missense variant effect prediction. Such approaches both predict general deleteriousness 10 – 17 and characterize specific molecular features: the position of an altered amino acid within a protein 18 , its effect on protein folding stability 19 , its effect on splicing 6 and post-translational modifications 20 , and its interaction with other proteins and molecules 21 , 22 . Such annotations have been used to distinguish benign from damaging variants within known disease genes 23 – 25 and to identify new gene-trait associations 26 – 28 , but the opportunity extends much further. Within a gene, the pattern of functional and phenotypic effects across variants—its allelic series—can distinguish between hypotheses for the mechanism of an association 29 , 30 . Across the exome, the pattern defines protein coding functional architecture: what mechanisms are most prevalent; what aspects of protein biology are broadly relevant to traits; and against this background, what genes stand out individually. In common-variant association studies, a key approach has been to integrate association data with epigenomic and gene expression annotations, both at individual loci 31 – 33 and across the genome 34 , 35 . For rare protein-coding variants, an analogous approach would integrate functional annotations describing how variants affect proteins, both exome-wide and within specific genes.

A functionally informed approach could also recover rare coding heritability missed by existing estimators. Burden Heritability Regression (BHR) 9 estimates the component of rare coding heritability captured by prespecified burden statistics, but it does not account for within-gene heterogeneity. GREML-LDMS avoids gene-level aggregation, but its estimates become unreliable at minor allele frequencies below around 0.01% 36 , 37 . One challenge is that residual population stratification can bias heritability estimates 37 ; another is that ultra-rare variants are carried by too few individuals for their contributions to be resolved without some amount of aggregation. A more flexible approach would allow variant effects which are neither identical nor independent, aggregating the effects of functionally similar alleles to the extent supported by the data.

Here, we introduce the Kernel Allelic Series Model (KASM), under which the effects of different alleles within the same gene covary according to their functional features. We integrate rare-variant association statistics from UK Biobank 38 and All of Us 39 with structural and functional annotations, including machine learning-derived predictions of variant effects and protein structure. We substantially revise previously published lower bounds on rare coding heritability 9 , identify enriched structural and functional features, quantify effect-size heterogeneity, and apply the same statistical approach to individual trait-associated genes.


## Results


## Methods overview

The premise of the Kernel Allelic Series Model (KASM) is that functionally similar alleles within the same gene, as predicted by relevant annotations, should have similar phenotypic effects. Mathematically, effect-size similarity is encoded by a kernel, or covariance matrix, which is calculated from the annotation data using a function fitted to the genetic data. Its diagonal elements describe the predicted magnitude of each effect; its off-diagonal elements describe how the effects of two variants covary. This covariance might be larger for similarly annotated alleles. For missense variants, these annotations could include deleteriousness scores 10 , 40 , 41 , allele frequency, and various features of amino acid substitutions, like whether they cause a change in polarity or disrupt a molecular interaction 18 , 23 ( Methods ). The relevance of these annotations is learned from the data. This approach generalizes the burden-effect model, under which variant effects are perfectly correlated. A general kernel allows variant effects to be perfectly correlated, partially correlated, or independent, to the extent supported by the data.

Mathematically, KASM features an unobserved true effect-size covariance kernel, Σ, a model specifying some space of working kernels, K , and a distance metric which quantifies whether some kernel K ∈ K well approximates Σ ( Methods ). For the same Σ, different choices of K give rise to different distance-minimizing kernels K * ∈ K . The estimand is K *, not Σ, so the choice of model K affects both the estimator and the estimand. We consider a series of models incorporating different functional features, each defining different components of rare coding heritability (see analyses below).

KASM quantifies the kernel heritability , defined as the fraction of phenotypic variance explained by variant effects aligned with a specified kernel space ( Methods ). When the kernel is rank one, the kernel heritability reduces to the burden heritability. More generally, for an appropriate space of kernels, the kernel heritability is a lower bound on the total SNP-heritability ( Methods ). The kernel heritability enrichment of a subset of variants is defined as the fraction of heritability divided by the fraction of rare-variant heterozygosity (not the fraction of variants). This denominator accounts for allele-frequency differences; variants within an enriched annotation have higher per-allele effect sizes ( Methods ). We quantify this enrichment primarily among missense variants, separately within exome-wide significant gene-trait associations (“significant genes”) and other “subthreshold genes”.

KASM implements random-effects and fixed-effects estimators, suitable for analyzing the set of subthreshold genes and individual significant genes, respectively. Both operate on variant association statistics, within-gene linkage disequilibrium matrices, and functional annotations. The random-effects estimator ( Methods ) is a fast, summary-level implementation of Haseman-Elston regression 42 , 43 . It features diagonal kernels which absorb confounding due to residual population stratification, analogous to the BHR intercept 9 . It models the effects of subthreshold genes as draws from a shared distribution and excludes significant genes, so that the estimate is not driven by a few large effects. The fixed-effects estimator ( Methods ) treats each significant gene’s variant effects as fixed quantities without distributional assumptions, similar to ref. 44 . This estimator provides the resolution to interrogate individual genes and improves statistical power in exome-wide analyses. For exome-wide heritability estimation, we combine random-effects and fixed-effects estimates to obtain a mixed-effects estimate. The random- and fixed-effects estimators account for distinct sources of uncertainty across genes and sampled individuals ( Methods ).


## Simulations

We evaluated KASM in simulations with variant-level functional heterogeneity, gene-level differences in selective constraint, linkage disequilibrium (LD) computed from All of Us data, and excess variance in association statistics representing residual population stratification ( Methods ). Variant effects were simulated from a kernel derived from real functional annotations, with excess biological effect heterogeneity not explained by these annotations, and summary statistics were simulated conditional on LD. Fixing this data-generating model, we computed both true and estimated kernel heritabilities under four KASM models (none of which is exactly the data-generating model): the burden model of Weiner et al. 9 , under which variants are partitioned into discrete categories and aggregated (“BHR-like”); a more flexible model featuring several continuous-valued missense annotations (“KASM Baseline”; see below); a model further allowing distinct effects across exons within each gene (“KASM Baseline x Exon”); and a model which adds a heritability component for independent variant effects (“KASM Baseline + Indep.”). The true kernel heritability was smallest under the BHR-like model and largest under the KASM Baseline + Indep. model.

KASM accurately estimated the kernel heritability across simulation settings ( Figure 1a , Supplementary Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 1 ). The mixed-effects estimator reduced standard errors relative to treating all genes as random effects ( Supplementary Figure 2 ). Ignoring LD within genes led to inflated heritability estimates ( Supplementary Figure 3 ). KASM also accurately estimated heritability enrichment for enriched, depleted, and null missense variant subsets ( Figure 1b , Supplementary Figure 4 , and Supplementary Table 2 ). Enrichment estimates were well calibrated in both significant genes and subthreshold genes, supporting the use of both estimators. In the same simulations, KASM also accurately estimated effect concordance across within-gene variant pairs for both missense and pLoF variants ( Supplementary Figure 5 ).

We additionally evaluated KASM using phenotypes simulated in the All of Us “ All by All ” data release with zero kernel heritability but nonzero common-variant heritability 39 . Estimates were near-zero, as expected, across models and ancestry groups ( Figure 1c and Supplementary Table 3 ). These individual-level simulations show that KASM avoids genetic confounding due to population structure and LD.


## Functionally informed kernel heritability

We applied KASM to exome association statistics for 16 UK Biobank traits and 19 All of Us traits (26 distinct, well-powered continuous traits), including up to 394,841 UK Biobank and 392,030 All of Us individuals ( Supplementary Table 4 ). We analyzed approximately 17K genes and 15 million rare coding variants (MAF < 0.1%; including singletons), classified as pLoF, missense, or synonymous variants. For missense variants, we compiled 15 quantitative deleteriousness scores, including PolyPhen-2 10 , AlphaMissense 14 , and ESM-1v 12 , which were only partially correlated ( Figure 2a and Supplementary Table 5 ); and 20 structural and functional annotations spanning primary sequence, secondary and tertiary structure, and protein-protein interaction interfaces ( Supplementary Table 6 ). We meta-analyzed kernel heritability estimates across continuous traits. We separately analyzed four UK Biobank binary disease traits and did not include them in subsequent analyses because of their low observed-scale heritability ( Methods and Supplementary Table 7 ).

We estimated kernel heritability under several models, producing a sequence of lower-bound estimates for rare missense heritability ( Figure 2b and Supplementary Table 7 ). Synonymous heritability was near zero (0.07±0.03%). Under a “naïve” burden model, which assigns one burden effect to all missense variants in each gene, the missense kernel or burden heritability was 0.26±0.02% (under this model, kernel and burden heritability are equivalent). Under a “BHR-like” model, which partitions missense variants by MAF and a binarized PolyPhen-2 score, the kernel or burden heritability was 0.54±0.04%, still smaller than the pLoF burden heritability (0.75±0.03%). Under a model containing three quantitative deleteriousness scores (AlphaMissense, ESM-1v, PolyPhen-2) stratified by MAF, termed “KASM Baseline”, the missense kernel heritability was 0.87±0.05%, slightly surpassing pLoF heritability. Stratification by MAF was important, as ultra-rare variants had larger per-allele effects than higher-frequency variants with similar predicted deleteriousness ( Supplementary Figure 6 ). Adding additional missense annotations had little effect ( Supplementary Figure 7 ), likely because these annotations were redundant or contained very few variants.

Next, to capture additional effect-size heterogeneity that is poorly predicted by deleteriousness scores, we fitted a “KASM Baseline x Exon” model, which allows effects to vary across exons, and “KASM Baseline + Indep.”, which allows variants to have independent effect-size residuals, particularly at higher allele frequencies ( Figure 2b , Supplementary Table 7 ). Missense kernel heritability increased from 0.87±0.05% under KASM Baseline to 1.27±0.09% under KASM Baseline x Exon and 1.83±0.11% under KASM Baseline + Indep. The increase under the exon model may reflect splicing-related effects or any other source of distance-dependent heterogeneity. The corresponding pLoF estimates were 0.75±0.03%, 0.87±0.05%, and 0.86±0.03%. Among these models, KASM Baseline yielded the strongest statistical evidence for nonzero kernel heritability ( P =7×10 −82 and 2×10 −152 for missense and pLoF variants), suggesting a useful balance between model flexibility and estimation precision, and is used as a default baseline model in subsequent sections ( Supplementary Table 7 ).

The KASM Baseline + Indep. model, which yields the highest kernel heritability, relies on additional assumptions in order to separate biological effects from confounding. Like the other models, it includes “overdispersion” kernels intended to absorb confounding effects, and these confounding effects are assumed to be uncorrelated ( Methods ). Unlike other models, it also features a biological kernel featuring uncorrelated effects, raising the question of identifiability. To distinguish confounding effects from independent biological effects, we assume that the former are equally strong for missense and synonymous variants at the same allele frequency ( Supplementary Figure 8 ), and moreover that they do not vary with gene-level selective constraint. These assumptions are plausible but not fully testable. An additional limitation of this analysis is that the independent-effects kernel is not frequency stratified; for this reason, the kernel heritability under this model remains a lower bound on the total missense heritability.

The additional missense heritability explained by the expanded models was concordant in both significant and subthreshold genes ( Supplementary Figure 9 ); for the KASM Baseline + Indep. model, this concordance is reassuring because the ratio of biological vs. confounding effects should be much higher within significant genes. Some specific gene-trait associations exhibited differences across models, including a pLoF association between TET2 and platelet count, for which heterogeneous pLoF effects probably reflect somatic clonal expansion 45 , 46 ( Supplementary Figure 10 ). As a negative control, synonymous heritability remained near zero under these models ( Supplementary Table 7 ).

Previous burden heritability estimates 9 were ~2x higher for pLoF variants than for missense variants. The primary difference in our analysis is that a more flexible model captures more missense heritability; when fitted to the same set of 16 UK Biobank traits, KASM “BHR-like” and BHR produced concordant missense burden heritability estimates ( Supplementary Figure 11 ). An additional difference is that for pLoF variants, KASM produced lower estimates than BHR, by about 11% ( Supplementary Figure 11 ). This difference is related to LD among pLoF indels, which are sometimes represented by multiple correlated variant records. KASM accounts for this using within-gene LD matrices, whereas the previous analysis 9 used a correction factor assuming that variants in different functional categories have similar amounts of LD. We verified that, without LD correction, KASM and BHR produced concordant pLoF heritability estimates ( Supplementary Figure 11 ).

We examined how rare coding effects vary with gene-level selective constraint estimated from pLoF variants in gnomAD 47 ( Figure 2c and Supplementary Table 8 ). pLoF variant effects were 8.7-fold enriched in the most constrained gene quintile. Missense variants in aggregate were only 1.8-fold enriched, apparently suggesting that pLoF variant constraint is weakly relevant to missense variant effects; however, AlphaMissense-prioritized missense variants were 6.9-fold enriched, approaching the pLoF enrichment ( Methods ). Without conditioning on deleteriousness, effect-size heterogeneity among variants combines with the effect of negative selection to dilute, or flatten 48 , the heritability enrichment of constrained genes: deleterious variants are depleted, and segregating variants are biased toward milder effects, reducing apparent differences between genes ( Supplementary Figure 12 ). A related phenomenon is that enrichment in constrained genes is greatly attenuated when measured in units of heritability per gene (we instead measure effect-size enrichment); Spence et al. 49 reported that constrained genes are unenriched for pLoF heritability per gene, but the discordance with our results is superficial.

We performed two secondary analyses. First, we assessed potential bias from residual population stratification aligned with functional annotations ( Methods ). We determined that their contribution was minimal for most traits (below 0.1% of phenotypic variance; Supplementary Figure 13 ), concordant with Weiner et al. 9 . Second, we added the genotype covariance matrix inverse as an overdispersion kernel, motivated by the theoretical possibility that the kernel heritability could exceed the total heritability (under conditions described in Methods ). This addition had little effect, supporting the interpretation of the kernel heritability as a lower bound on the total heritability ( Supplementary Figure 14 ).


## Benchmarking missense deleteriousness predictors

We next evaluated how well individual missense deleteriousness predictors prioritize trait-associated rare missense variants. We compared 15 predictors, including the three used in the KASM Baseline model (PolyPhen-2, ESM-1v, and AlphaMissense). For each predictor, we ranked missense variants within each gene and estimated heritability enrichment for variants in the top decile. This approach measures a predictor’s ability to distinguish variants with larger versus smaller phenotypic effects in the same gene, rather than prioritizing variants in genes with larger overall phenotypic effects ( Methods ).

Top-decile variants from every deleteriousness predictor were enriched for missense heritability in both significant and subthreshold genes ( Figure 3 and Supplementary Table 9 ). Predictors based on neural-network representations of protein sequence and structure, including AlphaMissense, MisFit-S and PrimateAI-3D, showed the strongest enrichment. AlphaMissense and MisFit-S additionally leverage human population variation through allele-frequency information for supervised training and allele-count-based estimates of selection, respectively. The stronger performance of neural-network-based predictors relative to other traditional and ensemble predictors is consistent with previous benchmarks using functional assays and clinical variant classifications 13 , 14 .

We then constructed a KASM composite score from MAF-stratified deleteriousness predictors, with weights estimated using an even-odd chromosome split to avoid overfitting ( Methods ). The composite score outperformed every individual predictor ( Figure 3 and Supplementary Table 9 ): its top-decile variants were 14.2±0.7-fold enriched in subthreshold genes and 8.7±0.2-fold enriched in significant genes, compared with 7.6-fold and 5.8-fold for AlphaMissense. Much of the improvement came from MAF stratification; the top composite decile prioritized variants with larger per-allele effect magnitudes and was therefore strongly depleted of higher-frequency rare variants ( Supplementary Figure 15 ). With a different definition of enrichment, prioritizing heritability per variant rather than effect size magnitude, an optimal composite score prioritizes higher-frequency variants instead ( Supplementary Table 9 and Methods ). The lower enrichment in significant genes partly reflected genes whose trait-associated variants were poorly prioritized by deleteriousness predictors. One example is the GPT -alanine aminotransferase (ALT) association (2.5±0.2-fold for AlphaMissense). GPT encodes the cytosolic ALT1; variants reducing GPT activity can strongly alter measured ALT without large fitness effects, which may make them difficult for general deleteriousness predictors to prioritize.

These results indicate that, among missense variants observed in population-scale exome data, trait-relevant deleterious missense variants are best prioritized by combining functional predictors with allele frequency information, consistent with improved power for gene discovery 26 , 28 . Allele frequencies are highly relevant when analyzing inherited variation, for which observed alleles are sampled in proportion to their population frequencies; by contrast, most de novo or experimentally generated mutations are absent from population reference datasets ( Discussion ).

We compared computational predictors with deep mutational scanning (DMS) assays, which experimentally measure molecular variant effects in specific contexts 50 . Four significant gene-trait associations had sufficient overlap between DMS measurements and observed exome variants. Within the common set of variants for each assay, we compared heritability enrichment for top-quartile variants prioritized by DMS or by computational predictors ( Methods ).

The relative performance of DMS and computational predictors varied across gene-trait associations ( Figure 3 , Supplementary Figure 16 and Supplementary Table 10 ). For FGFR3 and height, top-quartile variants from a kinase-domain activation assay 51 had enrichment comparable to AlphaMissense (3.2±0.3-fold vs. 3.7±0.5-fold), while accounting for more heritability because they had higher allele frequencies (71±6% vs. 41±5%). For GCK and HbA1c, a glucokinase activity assay 52 performed similarly to AlphaMissense, whereas AlphaMissense outperformed an LDL-uptake assay 53 for LDLR and LDL cholesterol. Results were similar for MC4R and BMI using an MC4R functional assay 54 . DMS assays therefore captured a component of effect-size heterogeneity within trait-associated allelic series, but apparently not to a greater degree than AlphaMissense, at least across four gene-trait pairs. Computational predictors have the advantage that they can be applied to any variant in any gene at little incremental cost, while experimental assays have the advantage that their specific readout can be mechanistically interpretable ( Discussion ).


## Structural and functional enrichments

We next asked where within proteins rare missense heritability is concentrated. Proteins are organized hierarchically, from the primary amino acid sequence to secondary structural motifs, tertiary folding within a single chain, and the formation of protein complexes via protein-protein interactions. We examined 20 structural and functional annotations spanning this hierarchy. Enrichment estimates were concordant between exome-wide significant and subthreshold genes, with smaller standard errors in significant genes; we report estimates from significant genes below ( Figure 4 and Supplementary Table 11 ).

At the level of the primary sequence, substitutions between amino acids with divergent physicochemical properties were enriched for missense heritability. Charge-reversing substitutions (negative-to-positive or positive-to-negative) showed 2.2±0.09-fold enrichment (explaining 9% of heritability vs. 4% of heterozygosity). Hydrophobicity-disrupting substitutions (hydrophobic-to-polar or polar-to-hydrophobic) showed 1.7±0.04-fold enrichment (20% vs. 11%), and large amino-acid volume changes (absolute volume change > 90Å 3 ) showed 1.5±0.06-fold enrichment (10% vs. 7%).

At the level of secondary structure, variants in regular structural elements were enriched, whereas variants in disordered regions were depleted. Variants in α-helices and β-sheets were 1.4±0.02-fold and 1.8±0.04-fold enriched, respectively. In contrast, variants in highly disordered regions were depleted, with a 0.5±0.02-fold enrichment, consistent with stronger effects in structurally rigid regions.

At the level of tertiary structure, buried residues and intrachain contacts were enriched, consistent with their potential to disrupt protein stability. Variants in buried residues (relative solvent accessibility < 0.25) showed 2.4±0.02-fold enrichment (59% of heritability from 24% of heterozygosity). Intrachain contacts were also enriched, including non-bonded contacts at 1.8±0.02-fold, hydrogen-bond residues at 1.9±0.03-fold, and salt bridges at 3.0±0.1-fold. Disulfide-bond residues showed particularly strong enrichment (5.3±0.5-fold), although they represented only 0.28% of heterozygosity.

The strongest enrichments occurred at protein-protein interaction interfaces. Residues forming interchain hydrogen bonds were enriched 4.9±0.2-fold. Interfaces supported by Protein Data Bank structures were enriched 2.5±0.09-fold, homology-inferred interfaces 2.7±0.3-fold, and the highest-confidence PIONEER-predicted interfaces 21 7.3±1.2-fold, although this category represented only 0.08% of heterozygosity.

Broadly, these results demonstrate that well-known structural features, many of which are also enriched for Mendelian disease mutations 21 , 23 , 25 , are generically relevant within trait-associated allelic series. This suggests that trait-associated missense alleles often have unexotic structural effects on the stability of proteins and especially protein complexes. When investigating missense associations within a gene, a logical starting point is to overlay structural features; whatever change in protein structure is phenotypically relevant, it likely aligns or at least correlates with some feature that is already well annotated.


## Context-dependent variant effects

We next tested whether certain missense annotations interact to amplify heritability enrichments. Specifically, we asked whether variants with higher-level structural features (e.g., hydrogen-bond residues) had markedly different enrichments depending on the particular amino acid substitution (e.g., whether the substitution was charge-reversing) ( Methods ). We analyzed the three primary-sequence substitution annotations from Figure 4 together with the 17 other, structural annotations and identified 29 annotation pairs for which the heritability enrichment of the structural annotation differed significantly depending on the chemical effect of the amino acid substitution ( Supplementary Figure 17 and Supplementary Table 12 ).

The strongest interaction involved charge-reversing substitutions at interchain hydrogen-bond residues, which had 27±2-fold heritability enrichment, compared with 3.9±0.2-fold for other substitutions at these residues ( Figure 5a ). Neither hydrophobicity-disrupting nor large-volume-changing substitutions showed a comparable interaction ( Supplementary Figure 17 ). Charge-reversing substitutions were also strongly enriched at intrachain hydrogen bonds (6.9±0.4-fold vs. 1.8±0.03-fold) and intrachain salt bridges (9.3±0.8-fold vs. 2.6±0.1-fold). At residues buried within protein monomers, all three primary-sequence substitution classes were enriched ( Supplementary Figure 17 ), suggesting that buried residues can perturb protein structure in a variety of ways; for example, volume-changing substitutions could cause cavity formation or overpacking ( Figure 5b ). Charge-reversing substitutions at buried H-bond residues were 13±1-fold enriched, compared with 2.7±0.05-fold for other substitutions ( Figure 5c ); such variants might be especially destabilizing due to an inability to form compensatory hydrogen bonds with water. Broadly, these estimates imply that missense effects depend upon the interaction between amino acid-level biochemical properties and higher-level structural features.

Missense variants can disrupt protein complexes either indirectly, by destabilizing the folded structure of a constituent protein, or directly, by altering a protein-protein interaction interface 4 , 55 . Both mechanisms are common among Mendelian disease mutations, which are enriched in buried residues and at protein-protein interfaces 4 , 25 . We annotated variants by whether their residues were buried in a protein complex and then compared interface and non-interface residues within this complex-buried variant set. Within complex-buried variants, interface residues had significantly higher enrichment than non-interface residues (6.0±0.2-fold vs. 3.7±0.1-fold; Figure 5d ). Thus, the enrichment we observed at protein interfaces is not explained by the generic enrichment of buried residues.


## Effect concordance of variant pairs

Missense variants at nearby residues may perturb the same structural or functional element and therefore produce more concordant trait effects than distant variants. For example, pathogenic missense variants are known to cluster in three dimensional structures of some proteins 56 – 58 , but whether spatial proximity predicts concordance in the direction and magnitude of complex-trait effects has been unclear. We defined effect concordance ( ρ ) as the ratio between mean pairwise causal-effect covariance and mean effect-size variance among selected variant pairs. We stratified variant pairs by three dimensional distance in AlphaFold-predicted structures 18 , 58 or by one dimensional coding-sequence distance, and estimated concordance using KASM Baseline + Indep. augmented with kernels encoding these pairs ( Methods ).

Across missense variant pairs, average concordance was ρ =0.34±0.01, consistent with a high degree of effect-size heterogeneity. 3D-proximal pairs (<6Å) had greater effect concordance than 3D-distant pairs (≥6Å; ρ =0.57±0.04 vs. 0.34±0.02; Figure 6 and Supplementary Table 13 ). This distance threshold roughly identifies residues that are close enough to make structural contact. Stratified by 1D distance, concordance was ρ= 0.50±0.03 for pairs separated by <15 nucleotides (nt) versus 0.33±0.01 for pairs separated by ≥90nt. As expected, pLoF variants showed high effect concordance across distance strata ( Figure 6 and Supplementary Table 13 ).

Notably, among 3D-proximal missense pairs (<6Å), variants that were distant in 1D (≥90nt; 1D-distant) had greater effect concordance than pairs that were also proximal in 1D (<15nt; 1D-proximal; ρ =0.80±0.08 vs. 0.48±0.04; P =9×10 −5 ; Figure 6 , Supplementary Figure 18 , and Supplementary Table 13 ). These 3D-proximal/1D-distant residue pairs are folded into close proximity and may interact to stabilize the fold, such that changing either residue disrupts the interaction or destabilizes the folded structure. Indeed, residues in 3D contact exhibit co-evolution across homologs, supporting the notion that their pairwise interaction determines their substitution effects 59 . We also observed elevated effect concordance among variants within the same secondary-structure element and between hydrogen-bonded residues ( Supplementary Figure 19 ). Concordance was higher among missense variant pairs in which both variants had high AlphaMissense scores, consistent with more concordant trait effects among variants predicted to be deleterious ( Supplementary Figure 20 ).

We next estimated effect concordance without any distance threshold among missense variants ( ρ MM ), among pLoF variants ( ρ LL ), and between missense and pLoF variants in the same genes ( ρ ML ), restricting to genes with at least two variants of each class ( Methods ). Concordance was substantially higher among pLoF variants ( ρ LL =0.91±0.03) than among missense variants ( ρ MM =0.38±0.02), whereas cross-class concordance was ρ ML =0.40±0.02. Their normalized ratio was 0.68±0.03, consistent with the previously reported burden genetic correlation 9 .Under a simple partial-loss-of-function model with a shared latent effect direction, r ML would equal one, even if missense effects are weaker and more heterogeneous. The lower observed correlation could reflect gain- or change-of-function mechanisms, or gene-to-gene variation in the relative effects of missense and pLoF variants ( Discussion ).


## Characterizing functional allelic series

Functional enrichments may vary across the allelic series of different genes, depending on their particular structure and function. We applied KASM to each of 299 significant gene-trait associations individually and tested for enrichment of 20 structural and functional annotations. We identified 215 significant gene-trait-annotation triplets spanning 79 gene-trait pairs and 19 annotations (one-sided Wald tests of enrichment greater than one; Bonferroni-corrected; Supplementary Table 14; Methods ). We examined the two largest enrichments, in PIEZO1 and FGFR3 , in detail using protein structures and functional assays.

Within PIEZO1 , a trimeric ion channel associated with HbA1c 60 , 61 , we identified a striking enrichment of HbA1c heritability at hydrogen-bond contacts connecting individual subunits of the channel ( Figure 7 and Supplementary Table 15 ). These contacts occur at two inter-subunit interfaces, the cytoplasmic anchor and the extracellular cap, where residues form hydrogen bonds with neighboring subunits 60 , 61 . They include only 0.6% of PIEZO1 missense variants (14 of 2,472; 0.8% of heterozygosity) yet explain 78% of the gene’s HbA1c heritability (95±3-fold enrichment, P = 5×10 −207 , burden Z=−27). 7 of 14 hydrogen-bond contact variants had nominally significant effects, all lowering HbA1c (Z<−3). They map to five positions spanning both interfaces: R2110, T2127 and K2169 in the cytoplasmic anchor, and L2277 and R2279 in the extracellular cap (the latter two are paired, forming a side chain-main chain hydrogen bond). By contrast, heterozygous pLoF variants had weaker effects in the opposite, HbA1c-increasing direction (burden Z = +3.1, P = 1.8×10 −3 ). These results are consistent with a gain-of-function mechanism in which weaker inter-subunit coupling slows channel inactivation, causing increased cation influx. This influx would cause red blood cell dehydration 62 – 64 , shorten red cell lifespan, and lower HbA1c 65 . Monti et al. 66 previously highlighted this association in a functionally informed analysis but did not identify the enrichment at hydrogen-bond residues.

Within FGFR3 , a transmembrane receptor that regulates skeletal growth 67 ( Figure 8a ), we identified strong enrichment of height heritability in the transmembrane domain. This domain contains only 3.0% of FGFR3 missense variants (16 of 524) but explains 44% of the gene’s missense height heritability (9.2±0.8-fold enrichment, P = 7.5×10 −27 ; Supplementary Table 16 ). The enrichment was driven primarily by G380R, the canonical achondroplasia-associated substitution in the FGFR3 transmembrane helix 68 , which promotes aberrant activation of the FGFR3 receptor tyrosine kinase 69 . We therefore tested whether the genetic effects on height tracked experimentally measured FGFR3 activation using a kinase-domain deep mutational scanning assay 51 , stratifying variants by their FGFR3 activation scores. Variants in the highest-activation-scoring quartile predominantly had negative effect estimates on height (33 of 39 variants; Figure 8b ) and were strongly enriched for height heritability relative to all FGFR3 missense variants (5.3±0.5-fold enrichment, P = 9.8×10 −17 ; Figure 8c ), whereas the lower-scoring quartiles showed no significant enrichment. This relationship was consistent across experimental conditions ( Supplementary Figure 21 ). This example shows how missense associations in different protein domains, here in conjunction with DMS data, can provide convergent mechanistic evidence.

We highlight two additional examples involving ion-binding sites ( Supplementary Figure 22 ). CASR , encoding the Ca 2+ -sensing receptor (CaSR), regulates calcium homeostasis 70 and is implicated in Mendelian disorders of both increased and decreased serum calcium through loss- and gain-of-function variants 71 , 72 . In our analysis, missense effects were enriched at Ca 2+ -binding residues: all five missense variants, mapping to four such residues, were associated with higher serum calcium levels, with effect sizes similar to pLoF variants (approximately 2 s.d.). These residues either coordinate Ca 2+ or couple Ca 2+ binding to receptor activation 73 , 74 . The direction of the effect is therefore consistent with partial loss of function that reduces CaSR sensitivity to extracellular Ca 2+ , raising the serum calcium set point 70 . IHH , encoding Indian hedgehog, a signaling protein required for growth-plate development 75 , was associated with height, with missense effects enriched at its conserved Zn 2+ -binding site. The two observed H145 variants (H145R and H145N) affect a residue that directly coordinates Zn 2+ within the IHH signaling domain 76 , and both were associated with shorter stature, also with effect sizes similar to pLoF variants (approximately −2 s.d.). A mutation at the homologous zinc-coordinating histidine in SHH was previously shown to substantially reduce zinc affinity, destabilize the folded signaling domain, and diminish signaling activity 77 . The H145 substitutions in IHH may similarly impair the stability and activity of the IHH signaling domain. These examples illustrate how integrating structural and functional features adds mechanistic resolution to pLoF burden associations: in both cases, missense alleles at specific functional sites recapitulate the pLoF effect and help to explain what trait-relevant function is lost.


## Discussion

Rare missense variants perturb proteins through diverse mechanisms, producing phenotypic effects that differ in magnitude and direction within the same gene. In clinical genetics, distinguishing pathogenic from benign mutations has been a longstanding goal, and recent progress has been driven by the development of machine learning algorithms for variant effect prediction 10 – 17 . These algorithms, in turn, were enabled by fundamental advances in algorithms to predict protein structure and function. We combined deleteriousness predictors with a range of functional features, many of them predicted computationally, to provide molecular explanations for how protein-altering variants affect human complex traits. With this approach, missense variants contributed more heritability than pLoF variants, their effects were concentrated in structurally and functionally important protein features, and mutations at 3D-proximal residues had highly concordant phenotypic effects. Within individual genes, enriched annotations pointed to plausible mechanisms. KASM therefore connects allelic heterogeneity to protein structure and function both exome-wide and within individual gene-trait associations.

A previous study 9 estimated a missense burden heritability of around 0.5% on average across traits, providing a lower bound on the total contribution of rare missense variants. KASM substantially improves this lower bound, from 0.54±0.04% under a BHR-like burden model to 1.27±0.09% under KASM Baseline x Exon and 1.83±0.11% for KASM with independent variant effects. The latter estimate is about 2x higher for missense versus pLoF variants.

These estimates were also larger than those reported in a recent whole-genome sequencing analysis in UK Biobank, which analyzed variants with MAF > 0.01% 36 . For height, that study estimated that coding variants with MAF of 0.01-1% explained 3.5% of phenotypic variance in height. We estimated that missense and pLoF variants with MAF < 0.001%, 0.001-0.01% and 0.01-0.1% explained 5.5%, 3.0%, and 1.9% of phenotypic variance in height, respectively; our estimate in the overlapping 0.01-0.1% range was consistent with the published estimate of 3.5% in the broader 0.01-1% range. These results suggest that rare coding variants account for a substantial fraction of heritability not captured by previous analyses. For height, our estimate for variants with MAF < 0.01% corresponds to approximately half the gap between WGS-based heritability of 70.9% and the reported pedigree-based heritability of 88.2% 36 .

Variant-effect predictors are commonly evaluated against clinical or experimental benchmarks that may not directly measure their ability to prioritize variants affecting complex traits 78 – 80 . KASM asks whether a predictor identifies variants with disproportionately large effects on complex traits. Unlike clinical pathogenicity benchmarks, which are concentrated in established Mendelian disease genes, KASM evaluates variants across the broader range of genes and traits in population-scale exome studies. It also complements DMS assays, whose molecular readouts are measured in specific experimental contexts and may not translate directly to organism-level phenotypes or disease risk. In our comparisons, computational predictors sometimes stratified trait effects more strongly than DMS, indicating that DMS performance is not necessarily gold-standard evidence for variant prioritization.

On the other hand, general-purpose deleteriousness predictors such as AlphaMissense do not provide mechanistic hypotheses, and interpretable functional annotations such as DMS scores complement such predictors by helping identify the molecular function of deleterious alleles. At FGFR3 , variant effects on height tracked experimentally measured kinase activation, supporting altered receptor signaling as a mechanism linking missense variation to the phenotype. At PIEZO1 , HbA1c heritability was concentrated among missense substitutions at inter-subunit hydrogen-bond contacts, supporting a mechanism involving gain of function. Exome-wide analyses demonstrate the same principle: for example, charge-reversing substitutions at interchain hydrogen bond residues have disproportionately large effects, implying that an important coding-variant mechanism is the disruption of quaternary structure.

Several existing methods integrate functional annotations to improve the power of rare variant association tests. Monti et al. 66 use functionally informed kernels to increase gene-level association power, motivated as we are by the principle that variants with similar functional annotations might have similar phenotypic effects, but a key difference is that our kernels are estimated from the data, while theirs are fixed. STAAR 81 uses a similar approach, combining the P values of multiple SKAT and annotation-weighted burden tests. DeepRVAT 27 does learn a data-dependent variant aggregation function, but the output of this function is a single score per gene, leading to a burden-like rather than kernel-like association test. Each of these approaches overlaps with ours methodologically, but their goal is different.

An unresolved question is how often missense variants have effects other than partial loss of function. For rare diseases, a substantial fraction of known causal alleles acts via gain of function 82 . We found that for complex traits, missense and pLoF effects in the same gene do have a positive correlation, but this correlation is weaker than expected under a narrow model of uniformly partial loss-of-function effects. Within pLoF-constrained genes, heritability enrichment is similar for pLoF alleles and AlphaMissense-prioritized missense alleles, consistent with those alleles causing partial loss-of-function; on the other hand, constrained genes could be similarly sensitive to both loss and gain of function 83 . Overall, we can rule out a narrow model in which missense variants produce weaker loss-of-function effects in a manner that is uniform across genes, but addressing the broader question remains challenging because existing annotations do not systematically identify gain-of-function effects.

More generally, a key limitation of KASM is that it relies upon existing annotations, which might not capture the functional perturbations through which variants influence traits. More accurate deleteriousness predictors 14 , 17 could yield higher heritability estimates, and annotations of specific molecular mechanisms, including ligand/protein binding 21 – 23 and post-translational regulation 5 , 84 , could provide new explanations for how variants influence traits.

We note several additional limitations. First, KASM fits an additive model among component kernels, without interactions except involving frequency bins. This approach is not expected to produce bias, but it potentially misses interesting interactions, and indeed, certain combinations of annotations do exhibit notable enrichments ( Figure 5 ). Second, our exome-wide analyses provide limited insight into heterogeneity among genes; for example, if some annotations were enriched specifically in some gene family, this enrichment would be diluted in our analysis. We do show that significant gene-trait pairs sometimes exhibit strong enrichments, pointing to gene-specific biology. Third, our analysis is limited to protein coding variants; regulatory variants would pose additional methodological challenges, including the problem of how to map variants to genes or otherwise group them into units. Despite these limitations, our results provide insight into the molecular processes through which protein variation influences human phenotypes.

## Inference for the Kernel Allelic Series Model

KASM operates on variant association statistics, variant-level functional annotations and gene-level constraint bins, and within-gene LD matrices. It applies separate random-effects and fixed-effects estimators for subthreshold and exome-wide significant gene-trait associations. The random-effects estimator models covariance shared across subthreshold genes and includes overdispersion components for residual inflation. The fixed-effects estimator conditions on each significant gene’s realized effects without imposing a distributional assumption. Compared with the random-effects model, an advantage of the fixed-effects estimator is that it provides gene-specific estimates; a disadvantage is that it does not include a correction for confounding. The rationale for combining both estimators is that excluding significant genes stabilizes the random-effects estimator, while excluding subthreshold genes limits the effect of confounding on the fixed-effects estimator. The same separation is used by BHR 9 and LDSC 86 ; a difference is that our fixed-effects estimator is more sophisticated.

## Alignment of stratification effects with functional annotations

Population stratification can bias our random effects estimator, despite the inclusion of overdispersion kernels, if its effects specifically align with functionally informed kernels. In particular, if minor alleles tend to associate with increased trait values, their effects are concordant along the burden direction. Minor allele-biased stratification can also affect BHR, and Weiner and Nadig et al. 9 showed that it arises in demographic simulations involving subpopulations of different sizes. Unlike biological effects, these stratification effects are expected to be equally correlated for any two minor alleles whether or not they belong to the same gene. Under this assumption, Weiner and Nadig et al. estimated the bias affecting synonymous variant burden heritability in UK Biobank traits to be around 0.01%-0.1% across traits. ## Exome-wide association studies and functional annotations

We analyzed rare coding association statistics from UK Biobank and All of Us, using variants with minor allele frequency < 10 −3 . The threshold was selected to minimize tagging the effects of variants outside each gene, following previous work 9 . For UK Biobank, we used Genebass summary statistics 38 ; for All of Us, we used All by All v8 Controlled Tier summary statistics 39 . The main joint analysis included 16 UK Biobank continuous traits and 19 All of Us continuous traits in each of the European-like (EUR-like), African-like (AFR-like), and admixed American-like (AMR-like) genetic-similarity groups. For each variant-trait pair, we extracted the marginal effect estimate, its standard error, allele frequency and sample size. Variants were harmonized by GRCh38 coordinate, reference and alternate alleles. We additionally analyzed four UK Biobank binary disease traits (diabetes, asthma, osteoarthritis, and cancer), estimating kernel heritability on the observed scale.

We classified rare coding variants into three broad consequence categories: predicted loss-of-function (pLoF), missense, and synonymous variants, as provided in Genebass 38 and All by All 39 .

We analyzed 15 quantitative scores spanning supervised and unsupervised deleteriousness prediction, evolutionary conservation, splicing, mutation rate, and protein language model-based annotations. These included PolyPhen-2 10 , AlphaMissense 14 , ESM-1v 12 , REVEL 88 , PrimateAI-3D 13 , MisFit-S 89 , SIFT 90 , CADD 11 , 91 , phyloP 92 , and popEVE 16 . Scores were matched by genomic coordinate and reference and alternate alleles. Details about sources and definitions for each score are reported in Supplementary Table 6 .

We annotated each missense substitution from its reference and alternate amino acids in the canonical UniProt sequence. Charge-reversing substitutions were Glu↔Lys substitutions, the only single-nucleotide changes between positively and negatively charged amino acids. Hydrophobicity-disrupting substitutions were changes between a hydrophobic (C, L, V, I, M, F, W) and a polar (R, K, E, D, Q, N) amino acid. Large volume-changing substitutions changed side-chain volume by more than 90 Å 3 (Grantham volumes 93 ).

We analyzed structural and functional annotations from NetSurfP 94 , PIONEER 21 , and the Genomics 2 Proteins (G2P) portal 95 . These included predicted solvent accessibility, secondary structure and intrinsic disorder, UniProt-annotated transmembrane regions and disulfide bonds 96 , PDB-supported intrachain and interchain contacts, and protein-protein interaction interfaces. Intrinsically disordered residues were defined as those in the highest within-gene quintile of NetSurfP-predicted disorder scores. We mapped residue-level annotations to variants using canonical protein coordinates derived from transcript consequence annotations.

We retrieved the HINT high-quality binary human interactome 97 (2024-06 release) and retained protein-protein interactions involving exome-wide significant genes that were supported by at least one PDB co-crystal chain pair. For each PDB chain pair, we retrieved the deposited mmCIF structure from RCSB 98 , mapped PDB residues to UniProt positions using per-residue SIFTS mappings 99 , and computed solvent-accessible surface area using FreeSASA 100 . For each gene, solvent accessibility was computed both for the isolated chain and for the two-chain complex. Residues were annotated as interface residues if they were solvent-exposed in the isolated chain (relative SASA ≥ 15%) and lost at least 1.0 Å 2 SASA upon complex formation. Residues were annotated as complex-buried if their relative solvent accessibility in the complex was < 25%.

For synonymous variants, we used five splicing, conservation, deleteriousness, and mutation-rate scores: phyloP 92 , GERP++ 101 , CADD 11 , Roulette mutation rate 102 , and SpliceAI 6 maximum delta score across acceptor gain, acceptor loss, donor gain and donor loss. Details about sources and definitions for each score are reported in Supplementary Table 6 .

We curated four DMS assays with sufficient overlap with rare missense variants in the exome association data in well-powered gene-trait associations: FGFR3-height, GCK-HbA1c, LDLR-LDL cholesterol, and MC4R-BMI. We compiled them from MaveDB (FGFR3 51 , LDLR 53 , 2026-02-06 quarterly Zenodo bulk release) 103 , ProteinGym (GCK 52 , v1.3) 104 , and the Octant MC4R DMS (github.com/octantbio/mc4r-dms 54 ). DMS target sequences were aligned to the canonical human UniProt proteome using MMseqs2 105 ; protein targets were aligned directly, whereas DNA targets were translated in the three forward reading frames before protein-level alignment. Substitutions were converted to all codon-compatible genomic SNVs, producing (chr:pos:ref:alt) variant IDs for overlap with exome association statistics.

Following ref. 9 , we partitioned genes by quintiles of the observed/expected number of unique pLoF variants in gnomAD 47 . The first quintile contains the most constrained genes and the last quintile the least. We estimated heritability enrichment across these quintiles separately for pLoF variants, all missense variants, and AlphaMissense-prioritized missense variants (the highest-scoring missense variants within each gene, retaining up to the number of pLoF variants in that gene).


## Functionally informed kernel heritability

We fit KASM models separately to pLoF, missense, and synonymous variants, using random-effects estimators for subthreshold genes, and fixed-effects estimators for significant genes. MAF-stratified models used bins of 0-10 −5 , 10 −5 -10 −4 , 10 −4 -10 −3 , with matching diagonal overdispersion kernels for the same bins. All models used five gnomAD pLoF observed/expected bins for gene-level scaling. Unless noted, pLoF and synonymous variants used one constant weight per MAF bin.

To estimate heritability under a conventional burden assumption, a single constant burden weight was assigned separately for pLoF and missense variants. The random-effects estimator modeled the corresponding rank-one burden kernel across subthreshold genes, and the fixed-effects estimator fitted the same one-dimensional burden score separately in each significant gene.

To approximate BHR’s functional and MAF stratification, missense variants were divided by the three MAF bins and binary PolyPhen-2 classification, yielding separate burden weights for damaging and benign variants within each MAF bin.

To model quantitative differences in missense variant deleteriousness, we used four burden weights: a constant weight, PolyPhen-2, ESM-1v, and AlphaMissense. We stratified each weight by the three MAF bins, yielding 12 burden weights.

KASM Baseline + Annotation

For a binary annotation, we added one annotation-specific weight per MAF bin. The subthreshold model included all burden and pairwise cross-burden kernels from the expanded weight set, and the significant-gene model included the same weights in the fixed-effects burden matrix.

KASM Baseline x Exon

Variants were assigned to coding exons using GENCODE v39 coding-sequence annotations 106 , and each exon was treated as an aggregation unit. Baseline weights were restricted to variants in each exon, and every exon inherited its parent gene’s constraint quintile. The random-effects model was fit across exon-level units in subthreshold genes. In significant genes, fixed-effects heritability was estimated for each exon and summed within each gene; gene significance was still defined by the gene-level burden test.

To estimate heritability not represented by the gene- or exon-level burden kernels, we implemented estimators that allowed a more flexible variant effect distribution. For subthreshold genes, we added an identity kernel for each of pLoF, missense and synonymous variant classes to model independent variant-effect variance not represented by the burden and cross-burden kernels. Because this kernel is partially collinear with MAF-stratified overdispersion kernels, we constrained the overdispersion coefficients to be shared across the pLoF, missense, and synonymous variants when analyzing each trait. This was supported by similar coefficients across classes ( Supplementary Figure 8 ). To further improve identifiability, we first fit KASM Baseline, fixed its gene-scaling coefficients, and then jointly estimated the burden-kernel, identity-kernel, and overdispersion coefficients. This procedure encodes the assumption that constrained genes are equally enriched for variant effects which are or are not aligned with functional annotations. For significant genes, the baseline burden model was replaced by the variant-level fixed-effects estimator.

To estimate effect concordance among proximal variants, we extended KASM Baseline + Indep. by adding one binary position kernel at a time (see above).

We simulated rare coding effects as the sum of gene-level burden, exon-level missense burden, independent missense and overdispersion effects, and generated the associational effect vector as . Conditional on and within-gene LD matrix R g , we simulated marginal variant association effects according to .

Heritable kernel coefficients, gene-scaling coefficients, and overdispersion coefficients were obtained by fitting “KASM Baseline + Indep.” to height summary statistics from EUR-like All of Us participants. Variant annotations, allele frequencies, sample sizes, and within-gene LD matrices were from All of Us EUR-like participants. To introduce model misspecification, we simulated gene-level sparsity that deviates from the KASM model; we randomly selected 1% of genes and multiplied their effect variance by 25, 50, or 100, then rescaled all causal effects to keep total heritability fixed.

Each simulation setting used 100 replicates. We fit BHR-like, KASM Baseline, KASM Baseline x Exon and KASM Baseline + Indep. models to estimate their corresponding heritability estimands. For enrichment, we evaluated AlphaMissense-ranked sets within each gene: the most deleterious variants, variants centered on the 10th percentile, the most benign variants, and a random set of the same size.

We also performed individual-level null simulations with common-variant effects but no rare coding effects in EUR-like, AFR-like, and AMR-like All of Us cohorts, as described in ref. 39 . The common-variant genetic component was sampled from a genetic relationship matrix and scaled to 50% heritability; independent environmental noise produced standardized continuous phenotypes. We calculated rare-variant association statistics from each phenotype and fit BHR-like, KASM Baseline, KASM Baseline x Exon and KASM Baseline + Indep. Five replicates were analyzed for each genetic-similarity group.


## Benchmarking missense deleteriousness predictors

For each of the 15 missense deleteriousness predictors, we ranked variants within each gene and defined the top decile as the variants predicted to be most deleterious. Variants in the top decile were analyzed using a binary annotation with KASM Baseline + Annotation, with one annotation-specific weight per MAF bin.

We constructed a KASM composite score from the fitted KASM Baseline covariance. For variant v in gene g , the score was the diagonal entry K g,vv , representing fitted per-allele effect variance. Coefficients were estimated in meta-analysis across traits. To prevent overfitting, separate models were trained on even- and odd-chromosome genes, and each model scored variants on the opposite chromosome set. We excluded each trait’s exome-wide significant genes in model fitting. Variants were ranked within genes, and the top decile was defined and evaluated with the same enrichment procedure as individual predictors. We also constructed an alternative composite score with 2 f v (1 − f v ) K g,vv , representing predicted per-variant heritability. For this analysis, we defined heritability enrichment as the fraction of missense heritability attributable to the selected variants divided by their fraction of missense variants, rather than their fraction of missense heterozygosity.

For the DMS benchmark, each gene-trait comparison was restricted to variants observed in the association data with complete DMS, PolyPhen-2, ESM-1v, and AlphaMissense scores. Within the shared variant set, each score was divided into quartiles, and quartile enrichment was estimated with the fixed-effects estimator.


## Structural and functional enrichment analyses

Each binary structural or functional annotation was tested separately with KASM Baseline + Annotation. Enrichment was estimated independently in significant and subthreshold genes and then meta-analyzed across traits. We tested whether enrichment differed from one with two-sided Wald tests, Bonferroni-corrected across the 20 annotations.


## Context-dependent missense variant effects

We performed context-dependent enrichment analyses for missense variants in exome-wide significant gene-trait associations using the fixed-effects KASM estimator. For each pair of a structural or functional context and a substitution class, we defined two binary annotations within the context: variants with (Yes) and without (No) the substitution class. Each annotation was analyzed separately as a binary annotation on top of the KASM Baseline model, and enrichment was meta-analyzed across traits.


## Effect concordance of variant pairs

To evaluate whether nearby coding variants have more concordant phenotypic effects, we constructed binary position kernels representing proximity in the primary sequence or three-dimensional protein structure. One-dimensional distance was defined as the absolute difference in coding-sequence position. For three-dimensional proximity, we used AlphaFold-predicted structures and GRCh38-to-UniProt residue mappings released by Nason et al. 58 . We calculated the minimum distance between side-chain heavy atoms of each residue pair, excluding glycine and residues with incomplete side chains. For proteins represented by multiple overlapping AlphaFold segments, we used the minimum distance across all segments containing both residues. Each binary relationship was fitted separately as a position kernel in the KASM Baseline+ Indep. + Position model, and effect concordance was estimated from the resulting covariance as described above. We performed several secondary analyses. We included an independent-effect kernel restricted to variants participating in the position kernel. We defined separate position kernels for pairs of residues within the same α-helix or β-strand at each residue offset from 0 to 6. For AlphaMissense-stratified concordance, variants with scores above each threshold (0.564, 0.8, or 0.9) were classified as high, and all other scored variants as other. We fitted separate position kernels for high-high, high-other, and other-other variant pairs.


## Characterizing functional allelic series

To characterize functionally defined allelic series within individual genes, we applied the fixed-effects estimator separately to each significant gene-trait association. We fit KASM Baseline + Annotation and tested enrichment greater than one with a one-sided Wald test.

For the PIEZO1 -HbA1c example, we used UK Biobank exome association statistics. The PIEZO1 trimer structure was obtained from the cryo-EM structure PDB 8YEZ. We queried PDBe PISA for assembly interface 1 and extracted hydrogen bonds connecting subunits. To estimate heritability enrichment, we built a binary annotation for missense variants at PISA-defined interchain hydrogen-bond residues and fit KASM Baseline + Annotation.

For the FGFR3 -height example, we used UK Biobank exome association statistics. We mapped FGFR3 missense variants to canonical protein coordinates and defined the transmembrane helix as UniProt residues 376-396 and the kinase domain as UniProt residues 472-761. For the kinase domain, we used the mapped FGFR3 DMS activation assay restricted to kinase-domain variants overlapping the exome association data and assigned these variants to DMS quartiles. To estimate the heritability enrichment for the transmembrane helix and the kinase-domain DMS quartiles, we partitioned FGFR3 missense variants into six non-overlapping subsets: transmembrane-helix variants, four DMS-scored kinase-domain quartiles, and all remaining missense variants. We then fit a single KASM Baseline fixed-effects model in which each Baseline burden weight was fitted separately within each subset and decomposed the gene’s heritability additively across subsets. The total heritability from this model and the total heterozygosity of all FGFR3 missense variants were used as denominators when estimating heritability enrichment for each subset.
