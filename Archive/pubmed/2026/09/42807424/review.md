## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (Abstract, Introduction, AI and genome editing section, Regulatory considerations, Discussion, Data availability, Author contributions, Conflict of interest, Generative AI statement, Publisher’s note, Acknowledgments)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific and regulatory claims as presented in the manuscript; no external literature verification performed
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors argue that AI-assisted crop improvement, including both conventional-like edits and de novo protein design, does not inherently challenge existing regulatory frameworks for biotech crops, and that current regulatory approaches remain appropriate for foreseeable outcomes.
- **Visible evidence base** Two soybean case studies (Xia et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025), one receptor engineering study (Ngou et al., 2025), and cited regulatory documents and policy literature
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** No figures, tables, or supplementary materials provided; no access to cited primary literature; no experimental data presented in the manuscript itself

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This Perspective presents a coherent and well-structured argument that AI-assisted crop improvement outcomes can be accommodated within existing regulatory frameworks. The authors draw on relevant examples and demonstrate familiarity with both the technical and regulatory landscape. However, the manuscript relies heavily on a small number of illustrative cases, and the central claim that regulatory status "should not change" is asserted rather than systematically demonstrated. The treatment of de novo protein design is largely speculative, as no crop-focused examples are provided. The regulatory analysis would benefit from deeper engagement with jurisdictions that have not adopted product-based approaches, and from a more critical examination of how AI-specific uncertainties might challenge existing assessment paradigms.

- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Regulatory scientists and policymakers working on biotech crop oversight, plant biotechnology researchers developing AI-guided breeding tools, and industry stakeholders seeking regulatory clarity for AI-assisted product development. The manuscript offers a useful synthesis of current AI applications and their regulatory implications, which is relevant for ongoing policy discussions in multiple jurisdictions.

- **Major strengths** The manuscript provides a clear conceptual framework distinguishing between conventional-like edits and de novo protein design, which is useful for regulatory analysis. The authors demonstrate practical familiarity with both the technical tools and the regulatory landscape, and the discussion of AI limitations and cross-cutting governance issues is balanced and measured. The examples chosen are recent and relevant.

- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence sufficiency
  - **Claim pointer** The authors contend that "the regulatory status of the potential outcomes should not change" for AI-assisted crop improvement, including de novo protein design.
  - **Evidence pointer** Section "Regulatory considerations for AI-guided crop improvement", location not provided
  - **Concern** The central claim that regulatory status should not change is supported primarily by assertion and by two soybean examples and one receptor engineering study. No systematic analysis is provided of how different regulatory jurisdictions would actually treat these outcomes, nor is there engagement with jurisdictions that have not adopted product-based regulatory approaches. The de novo protein design scenario is discussed without any concrete crop-focused example, making the regulatory conclusion speculative.
  - **Why it matters** The manuscript's primary contribution is its regulatory argument. If that argument is not rigorously supported, the manuscript does not fulfill its stated purpose. The claim that existing frameworks are adequate for de novo designed proteins requires more than assertion, particularly given that some jurisdictions may treat such products differently from conventional edits.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should either (a) provide a more detailed analysis of how specific regulatory frameworks (e.g., EU, US, Japan, Australia) would treat the described outcomes, including de novo protein design, or (b) explicitly limit their claim to the examples presented and acknowledge that broader regulatory conclusions require further analysis.

  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that "AI-assisted optimization of editing processes and outcomes could reduce unintended outcomes, such as off-target edits."
  - **Evidence pointer** Section "Regulatory considerations for AI-guided crop improvement", location not provided
  - **Concern** This claim is presented without supporting evidence or citation. While it is plausible that AI tools could improve editing precision, the manuscript does not provide any data or references demonstrating that this is currently the case in crop applications. The claim is also somewhat in tension with the earlier acknowledgment that AI tools have limitations and can produce flawed outputs.
  - **Why it matters** The claim is used to support the argument that AI tools are beneficial from a regulatory perspective. If this claim is not substantiated, it weakens the overall argument and may be seen as advocacy rather than analysis.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should either provide citations to studies demonstrating AI-mediated reduction of off-target edits in crops, or soften the claim to reflect that this is a potential benefit that requires further validation.

  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Scope and generalizability
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that "currently foreseeable outcomes do not inherently challenge established biotech regulatory frameworks."
  - **Evidence pointer** Section "Introduction", location not provided
  - **Concern** The manuscript focuses exclusively on protein design and editing outcomes. It does not address other AI applications in crop improvement, such as genomic selection, phenotype prediction, or AI-guided breeding decisions, which may raise different regulatory considerations. The claim of generalizability is therefore broader than the evidence presented.
  - **Why it matters** If the authors intend to make a general claim about AI-assisted crop improvement, they need to either broaden their analysis or narrow their claim. As written, the claim may overstate the scope of the analysis.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should either expand the analysis to cover a broader range of AI applications, or explicitly state that their conclusions apply only to the protein design and editing applications discussed.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that AI-assisted optimization reduces off-target edits is presented without supporting evidence or citation.
- The assertion that "currently foreseeable outcomes do not inherently challenge established biotech regulatory frameworks" is broader than the evidence presented, which covers only a limited set of protein design and editing applications.
- The statement that de novo designed proteins "remain within the scope of GMO regulatory provisions" is asserted without analysis of how specific jurisdictions would classify such products.
- The claim that AI tools "may further inform problem formulation approaches to risk assessment" is presented as a possibility but is not developed with specific examples or mechanisms.
