# Ligand-induced reshaping of the conformational free energy landscape drives dynamic restriction in Trypanosoma brucei alternative oxidase


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10822-026-00945-9.


## Introduction
  Human African Trypanosomiasis (HAT) is a disease caused by the kinetoplastid Trypanosoma brucei, which remains a significant public health threat in sub-Saharan Africa. Although the number of reported cases has declined from the intensive surveillance, the disease remains fatal if untreated where millions of lives can be at risk of infection [1]. Therapeutic solutions such as fexinidazole and eflornithine have improved patient outcomes, but there are some challenges related to complex administration regimens, stage-specificity, and the emergence of drug resistance that urge the continuous development of novel chemotypes [2, 3].

  The trypanosome alternative oxidase (AOX) has been recognized as a validated therapeutic target long ago. In the parasite, particularly in its bloodstream form, the uncoupli ng of mitochondrial respiration with oxidative phosphorylation occurs while the redox balance of the glycosomal NADH pool is maintained solely by AOX via the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle [4]. This physiological reliance is what makes the parasite and the mammalian host different. The mammalian host utilizes the cytochrome c oxidase pathway, thus offering a unique opportunity for selective inhibition with high therapeutic indices [4, 5]. AOX consists of a diiron interfacial membrane protein that plays a critical role in the reduction of oxygen to water. According to Shiba et al. [6], the crystallographic resolution of T. brucei AOX bound to inhibitors such as ascofuranone and ferulenol reveals a hydrophobic active site deeply buried within the membrane-binding surface [6] (Fig. 1). These structural insights have guided the design of synthetic analogues to maximize lipophilicity and bioavailability.   Nonetheless, these static crystal structures (e.g., PDB 5ZDP) can capture only a single, energetically minimized state within a frozen lattice, and because of this limitation, the dynamic conformational changes required for the entry of the bulky ubiquinol substrate or the solvent gating necessary for proton transfer are difficult to be further studied [8, 9]. Moreover, the efficacy of enzyme inhibitors is progressively known to not only rely on thermodynamic affinity (Kd), but also on the drug-target residence time (governed by the dissociation rate constant, koff), the lifespan of the drug-target complex [10]. While absolute koff and Kd values cannot be directly derived from microsecond-scale classical molecular dynamics, the experimentally observed picomolar affinity of ASCO strongly implies a highly prolonged residence time that correlates with the severe dynamic restriction observed in our models. In interfacial enzymes such as AOX, which lack classical “switch” motifs (for example, the DFG-in/out in kinases), functional regulation is likely controlled by the temporary reshaping of the conformational free energy landscape [10]. From a hypothesis by Tonge and Pan, high-affinity inhibitors function by kinetically trapping the enzyme in non-productive binding poses which effectively decouple it from the thermal fluctuations required for catalytic turnover [11, 12].

  In this work, we employed microsecond-scale molecular dynamics (MD) simulations combined with Markov State Modeling (MSM) to define the kinetic basis of AOX inhibition. In this way, it is possible to rigorously reconstruct the free energy landscape of the protein and quantify the occurrence of rare transition events [12, 13]. We also compared the conformational dynamics of the AOX bound with the substrate analogue ubiquinone-2 (UQ2) against systems bound with the specific inhibitors ascofuranone (ASCO) and ferulenol. From our analysis, we found that although the substrate-bound enzyme retained functional plasticity across multiple states, inhibitor binding caused a significant restriction of the free energy landscape into a single, constrained spatial variance. These results suggest that the major cause of the picomolar efficacy observed for ASCO is this “dynamic restriction.”


## Materials and methods

### Complex prediction and docking validation
  To compare the binding patterns of the substrate analogue UQ2 and the inhibitors ASCO and ferulenol within the catalytic cavity of T. brucei AOX, a dual-method approach was employed. Initial structural modeling of the apo-protein and preliminary complexes was generated using the Boltz-1 deep learning model. To validate these structural predictions and calculate binding affinities, classical molecular docking was subsequently performed using AutoDock Vina (v1.2.x) [14, 15]. Prior to docking, the protein structure was prepared using AutoDockTools (MGLTools). Preparation included the removal of heavy atoms without compromising active site geometry, the addition of polar hydrogens, and the assignment of protonation states at physiological pH, followed by the addition of Kollman partial charges and conversion to PDBQT format. Ligand geometry optimization was performed, after which Gasteiger charges were assigned, and all active torsions were defined. A search space was established around the ubiquinone-binding pocket, fully encompassing the lipophilic channel and the catalytic cavity. The docking grid box was centered at coordinates X = [7.67], Y = [− 3.331], Z = [2.87] with dimensions of [64 × 62 × 60] Å and a grid spacing of 0.375 Å. A standard flexible-ligand docking protocol was executed with an exhaustiveness of 16. The resulting poses were evaluated based on their AutoDock Vina scoring (kcal/mol), conformational clustering, and consistency with known interactions in the active site. Finally, the visual characteristics of hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts inside the catalytic pocket were systematically analyzed to validate the binding modes.


### Molecular dynamics simulations

#### System preparation
  The coordinates for the T. brucei AOX were obtained from the crystal structure of the ferulenol-bound complex (PDB ID: 5ZDP) [6] The initial spatial orientation of the integral membrane protein with respect to the lipid bilayer was calculated using the PPM server. In the case of the non-crystallographic systems, UQ2 and ASCO were first generated as initial protein-ligand complexes using Boltz-1, a deep learning-based biomolecular interaction model [14]. The structural alignment of the predicted complexes was performed to the 5ZDP reference to ensure a consistent orientation. The full simulation systems were subsequently constructed using the CHARMM-GUI Membrane Builder, embedding the protein in a mixed lipid bilayer mimicking the inner mitochondrial membrane (POPC/POPE/TOCL at a 60:30:10 molar ratio). The protein was modeled using the CHARMM36m force field [6, 16, 17], and the CGenFF program was used to generate ligand topologies and parameters [18]. To ensure accuracy beyond standard protonation states, the pKa values of titratable residues were evaluated at a physiological pH of 7.4 using PROPKA, confirming that key active-site residues maintain their expected standard protonation states without anomalous shifts.


#### Simulation protocol
  All simulations were performed using the GROMACS 2024 software suite [19, 20]. Each system was solvated with TIP3P water molecules. Absolute electroneutrality was first achieved by adding the requisite number of excess neutralizing counterions (Na+ or Cl–). Subsequently, bulk NaCl was added to a concentration of 150 mM to mimic physiological ionic strength. Minimization and equilibration followed the rigorous six-step CHARMM-GUI protocol [17, 18] to gradually relax the membrane and solvent around the protein-ligand complexes:

  Energy Minimization: Steepest descent minimization was utilized for 5,000 steps to eliminate steric clashes.Equilibration (Steps 1–2, NVT Ensemble): The systems were heated to 303.15 K using a v-rescale thermostat. Strong harmonic position restraints were initially applied and systematically reduced from 4,000 to 2,000 kJ/mol·nm² for the protein backbone, 2,000 to 1,000 kJ/mol·nm² for sidechains, and 1,000 to 400 kJ/mol·nm² for the lipids over two 125 ps steps (using a 1 fs time step).Equilibration (Steps 3–6, NPT Ensemble): Pressure was maintained at 1.0 bar using a semi-isotropic C-rescale barostat. Across these four steps (Step 3: 125 ps with a 1 fs step; Steps 4–6: 500 ps each with a 2 fs step), positional restraints on the protein were gradually reduced to 50 kJ/mol·nm² for the backbone and fully released (0 kJ/mol·nm²) for both the sidechains and lipids to allow smooth structural adaptation.


#### Production runs
  In this work, each system was simulated three times (replicates) to ensure adequate sampling. Initial random velocities were generated for each replicate to avoid synchronization artifacts. In the NPT ensemble, production runs were performed for 1000 ns per replicate, yielding a cumulative sampling time of 3.0 µs for each system. A v-rescale thermostat (τ_t = 1.0 ps) maintained the temperature at 303.15 K, and a semi-isotropic C-rescale barostat (τ_p = 5.0 ps, compressibility 4.5 × 10⁻⁵ bar⁻¹) maintained the pressure at 1 bar. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method was used to calculate long-range electrostatic interactions with a real-space cutoff of 1.2 nm. The lengths of covalent bonds containing hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm, allowing for an integration time step of 2.0 fs.


### Markov state modeling

#### Dimensionality reduction and discretization
  Conformational kinetics and metastable states were analyzed using the PyEMMA software package (version 2.5.x) [21]. To prevent the introduction of artificial state transitions, the independent MD replicas were not physically concatenated prior to analysis. Instead, the featurized trajectories (utilizing backbone torsion angles (φ, ψ) for all residues, saved at a stride of 0.1 ns) were loaded as a discrete list of independent trajectories, ensuring trajectory boundaries were strictly respected during all mathematical projections and transition estimations.

  The high-dimensional feature space was projected onto its slowest kinetic components using Time-lagged Independent Component Analysis (TICA). TICA maximizes the autocorrelation of the data, rendering it superior to traditional spatial variance methods for resolving slow, functionally relevant kinetic transitions. TICA was performed using a lag time of 1.0 ns (10 steps), and the components were truncated to retain 95% of the cumulative kinetic variance. The continuous TICA subspace was then discretized into 200 microstates using k-means clustering. This specific number of microstates was selected after systematically testing distributions ranging from 100 to 500 states; 200 provided the optimal balance, ensuring a sufficiently high-resolution geometric partitioning of the free energy basins while maintaining robust transition counts between states to prevent sparse matrix singularities.


#### Model construction and validation
  Using the discrete microstate trajectories, a MSM was estimated [22]. Based on the implied timescale spectrum (Supplementary Figure S1), a Markovian lag time of 5.0 ns (50 steps) was selected for the transition matrix estimation. The implied timescales were calculated utilizing Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) rather than Bayesian sampling to ensure numerical stability and prevent sparse matrix singularities across the 200 microstates.

  To identify the primary metastable basins, the PCCA+ (Perron-Cluster Cluster Analysis) algorithm was utilized to kinetically coarse-grain the microstates into distinct macrostates, guided mathematically by the spectral gaps in the implied timescales. The reliability of the chosen lag time and the approximate Markovian behavior of the model within the projected subspace were assessed using the Chapman-Kolmogorov (C-K) test (Supplementary Figure S2) following established MSM validation protocols [22]. We acknowledge that the C-K test exhibits minor deviations from ideal Markovian behavior for the UQ2-bound system. In complex membrane proteins, such deviations frequently arise from unresolved hidden slow degrees of freedom—such as local lipid interactions or internal side-chain packing—that introduce non-Markovian memory effects not perfectly captured by the continuous TICA projection at short to intermediate lag times. Similar lag-time dependencies and Markovian deviations have been thoroughly documented in recent multiple-replica MD/MSM analyses of complex receptors [23–28]. While systematically testing alternative lag times (up to 10 ns) improved Markovianity, it substantially reduced kinetic resolution and statistical confidence due to diminished transition counts. Therefore, 5.0 ns was retained as the optimal operational lag time. Finally, to reconstruct the conformational free energy landscape, the stationary distribution of the MSM was reweighted onto the TICA coordinates using G(x) = -kBT ln(P(x)) [29, 30].


### Principal component analysis (PCA)
  To fully characterize the conformational landscape, both PCA and tICA were employed, as they capture complementary features of the ensemble. PCA was utilized to maximize structural variance, providing a macroscopic map of the physical boundaries and global geometric extent of the conformational space (e.g., identifying large-amplitude domain motions). Conversely, tICA was utilized to maximize kinetic variance (autocorrelation). Because tICA isolates the slowest dynamic transitions rather than purely spatial deviations, the tICA coordinates were exclusively used as the foundational state space for the subsequent kinetic clustering and MSM.

  Diagonalization of the covariance matrix yielded eigenvectors (principal components, PCs) describing collective modes of motion and corresponding eigenvalues representing their amplitudes. Trajectories were projected onto the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), which captured the dominant conformational variance. These projections were used to visualize conformational sampling and compare the global dynamic landscapes of AOX in the two ligand-bound states.


## Results and discussion

### Ligand-dependent binding modes (molecular docking)
  Molecular docking reveals fundamentally distinct binding modes between the substrate analogue UQ2 and the inhibitors ASCO and ferulenol within the catalytic cavity of T. brucei AOX. The UQ2 molecule adopts a centrally positioned and compact binding orientation, residing within the catalytic pocket without significantly obstructing the substrate access channel. This configuration is consistent with a catalytically competent pose, preserving the geometric accessibility required for substrate turnover.

  In contrast, both ASCO and ferulenol exhibit markedly different binding characteristics. The inhibitors penetrate more deeply into the hydrophobic cavity and extend laterally along the lipophilic channel, thereby occupying a substantially larger interaction surface area. As illustrated in Fig. 2, this expanded binding footprint results in partial occlusion of the catalytic entry pathway. The inhibitor conformations effectively span the channel walls, forming extensive hydrophobic and polar contacts that are not observed in the UQ2-bound state. Because the binding mode and spatial footprint of ferulenol highly overlap with those of ASCO, ASCO is utilized as the primary structural representative for detailed geometric mapping. Detailed spatial comparisons were mapped within the active site (Fig. 2). While UQ2 exhibits looser, permissive coordination with surrounding hydrophobic residues (Fig. 2b), ASCO establishes a tighter persistent interaction network with specific anchoring residues deeply within the pocket (Fig. 2c).

  This differential spatial occupation highlights a key mechanistic distinction: whereas UQ2 maintains an open and permissive binding geometry, the inhibitors impose a structurally restrictive configuration. The extended conformation of ASCO and ferulenol promotes channel closure and reduces cavity accessibility, suggesting an early-stage structural bias toward conformational confinement.

  Importantly, these docking observations provide the initial structural basis for the dynamic restriction phenomenon identified in subsequent MSM analysis. The increased steric occupancy and deeper penetration of the inhibitors are not merely indicative of stronger binding affinity, but rather reflect a ligand-induced reshaping of the catalytic environment. This supports a model in which high-affinity inhibitors act not simply as competitive blockers, but as conformational modulators that distort the geometry of the active site and predispose the enzyme toward a kinetically restricted state.

### Ligand-dependent structural dynamics
  To elucidate the dynamic effects of ligand binding, we performed comparative MD simulations of T. brucei AOX bound to the substrate analogue UQ2 and the inhibitors ASCO. From Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) based on Fig. 3A analysis of the three independent replicates, distinct structural evolution behaviours were observed. The UQ2 and ASCO systems reached an early equilibration plateau (< 100 ns) in all the replicates. The average backbone RMSD values and standard deviations across all independent microsecond trajectories quantitatively confirm that these complexes oscillate consistently around a defined mean (Supplementary Supplementary Table S1). The ASCO-bound complex maintained a highly stable average backbone RMSD of 0.60 ± 0.11 nm. In contrast, the UQ2-bound system exhibited a higher overall structural deviation, averaging 0.91 ± 0.29 nm further highlighting the restricted conformational variance of the ASCO-bound ensemble compared to the UQ2-bound state (Supplementary Table S1). Conversely, the ferulenol system exhibited a larger initial structural readjustment before plateauing at a higher average RMSD (~ 0.7 nm) relative to its crystallographic starting point. This structural evolution indicates that the static crystal structure represents a lattice-constrained conformation, and that solution-phase relaxation takes place upon release from packing forces [9].

  Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) profiles as shown in Fig. 3B, averaged over the triplicate ensemble, highlight a thermodynamic trade-off in the inhibitor-bound state. To establish a baseline for intrinsic protein flexibility, the apo-state dynamics were also evaluated (Supplementary Figure S1). The apo state exhibited the highest overall mean flexibility (0.31 ± 0.03 nm). Compared to the highly mobile apo state, the UQ2 substrate-bound trajectory exhibited moderate flexibility, distributed broadly throughout the active site loops (residues 150–200). On the other hand, a rigidification of the core binding pocket was induced by ASCO binding, yet it caused a significant fluctuation at the C-terminus (residues 300–329). This “local locking, distal whipping” behaviour suggests a mechanism of entropic compensation, where the energetic penalty of rigidifying the active site is offset by increased disorder in the C-terminal tail [21]. Crucially, analysis of the individual trajectories confirmed that this distinct dynamic signature was consistently reproduced across all three independent ASCO replicates, demonstrating a robust thermodynamic response rather than an isolated simulation artifact.

  Figure 3C illustrates the time evolution of protein–ligand hydrogen bonds over the simulation period, comparing ASCO with UQ2 (and ferulenol). The number of hydrogen bonds is plotted against simulation time (ns), where higher frequency and longer continuous occupancy indicate stronger and more stable interactions. ASCO exhibits more frequent hydrogen bond formation and longer persistence throughout the trajectory, maintaining consistent interactions with the binding site residues. In contrast, UQ2 (and ferulenol) show fewer hydrogen bonding events and more intermittent contacts, characterized by shorter-lived interactions and greater fluctuations. The sustained hydrogen bond profile of ASCO suggests enhanced binding stability and stronger ligand–protein affinity, reinforcing its potential as a more stable binder within the active site [22].

  The radius of gyration (Rg) profiles, which are used to indicate the overall structural compactness of the protein in complex with the inhibitor and UQ2, are shown in Fig. 3 D as a function of simulation time. The average Rg value of the inhibitor-bound system (2.10 ± 0.01 nm) is slightly larger than that of the UQ2-bound complex (2.05 ± 0.01 nm), which suggests a small expansion and change in conformation. These differences indicate minor structural changes, which may be linked to distal or loop-region changes with the binding of the inhibitors. The UQ2-bound system, on the other hand, has lower and less variable values of Rg, which is indicative of a less extended structural arrangement [31]. Although differences are observed, the two systems oscillate over a close Rg range along the trajectory, demonstrating that the overall structural compactness and integrity of the protein are conserved.


### Principal component analysis (PCA)
  When projected onto the PC1–PC2 subspace, the conformational ensembles exhibit pronounced ligand-dependent differences in their spatial variance that reflect distinct underlying energy landscapes, as shown in Fig. 4. Rather than assigning these principal components to specific physical domain movements, this projection highlights the overall thermodynamic extent of conformational exploration. The AOX–UQ₂ system (green) displays a broad and heterogeneous distribution spanning multiple regions of principal component space, indicative of extensive conformational sampling [32]. This dispersion reflects the presence of multiple accessible substates, consistent with a dynamically flexible ensemble [32].

  In contrast, the spatial variance of the AOX–ASCO ensemble (orange) is visibly constrained. As demonstrated by the population density in the overlaid projection, the ASCO-bound complex localizes primarily into dense, restricted vertical regions along PC1, exhibiting significantly reduced spatial variance compared to the substrate-bound state. This contraction indicates a suppression of large-scale structural exploration and a substantial loss of conformational diversity [33]. The clustering of conformations into these narrow, tightly packed basins demonstrates that the protein is spatially restricted to a limited set of structural states.

  Importantly, this confinement is not merely indicative of reduced flexibility but rather reflects a ligand-induced reshaping of the conformational energy landscape. The localizes of sampling into a dominant region of PC space foreshadows the formation of a kinetically stabilized macrostate, as quantitatively confirmed by the MSM [20] analysis (Fig. 5).

  Taken together, the PCA results provide a global dynamic perspective that complements the local structural and thermodynamic observations. While the UQ₂-bound system explores a broad and functionally permissive conformational space, ASCO binding drives the system into a restricted and kinetically confined ensemble. This transition from a multi-state dynamic landscape to a localized conformational basin represents a key step in the mechanism of inhibition, whereby global motions essential for catalysis are effectively suppressed.

  These findings strongly support the concept of dynamic restriction, in which inhibitor binding reduces the accessible conformational entropy of the system, thereby disrupting the active site dynamics and structural geometry typically required to support catalytic function.

### Markov state modeling and dynamic restriction
  To quantify the timescales and populations of ligand-dependent conformational states, MSMs were constructed for the UQ2- and ASCO-bound systems using the discretized trajectories projected onto the tICA space. The resulting models revealed a profound divergence in the extent of conformational restriction between the two systems. For the substrate analogue UQ2, the MSM identified a dynamic equilibrium distributed across three metastable macrostates comprising approximately 56.2%, 23.7%, and 20.1% of the population. In contrast, the ASCO-bound ensemble exhibited a constrained spatial variance, with the dominant macrostate sequestering approximately 98.5% of the population, leaving two negligible minor states accounting for approximately 0.7% each.

  Because the ASCO-bound system occupies a single highly populated basin while the UQ2 system remains broadly distributed, differences in the kinetic hierarchy of the models indicate distinct conformational behaviour between substrate and inhibitor binding. In particular, analysis of the implied timescales revealed that the ASCO-bound system displayed substantially slower secondary conformational dynamics compared with the UQ2 system. The extreme stationary population and longer secondary implied timescales observed for ASCO indicate stronger kinetic restriction of conformational transitions within the ensemble. This suggests that inhibitor binding stabilizes a severely constrained conformational landscape relative to the substrate-bound state. Taken together, these MSM results indicate that ASCO does not simply occupy the catalytic cavity but instead promotes the stabilization of a kinetically restricted conformational ensemble [34]. This dynamic confinement is consistent with the reduced conformational sampling observed in the inhibitor-bound system and supports the interpretation that ASCO limits the conformational flexibility required for catalytic turnover.


### Structural basis of the dynamic restriction
  The atomic determinants of the dynamic restriction mechanism were elucidated by integrating structural observations with the conformational free energy landscapes derived from MSM (Fig. 5). The quinol-binding cavity is embedded within a hydrophobic pocket formed by surrounding α-helices and positioned adjacent to the catalytic diiron center, consistent with previous structural studies of T. brucei AOX [6].

  As shown in Fig. 5A, the substrate-bound (UQ2) system exhibits a broad free energy landscape, which was kinetically coarse-grained into three metastable macrostates (56.2%, 23.7%, and 20.1%) using PCCA+. It is important to note that PCCA+ clusters states based on kinetic transition probabilities rather than pure spatial geometry. Therefore, regions that appear visually as a single broad geometric basin or interconnected minima in the projected landscape may contain hidden kinetic barriers, resulting in multiple distinct kinetic macrostates. Consequently, the MFPT matrix (Fig. 5C) aligns with these kinetic barriers, reflecting the transition timescales between the dominant metastable populations rather than strictly aligning with visual spatial minima. This topology enables rapid interconversion between conformational states, supporting the dynamic flexibility required for catalytic turnover. These findings are consistent with the structural observation that UQ2 adopts a centrally positioned and catalytically permissive binding orientation within the cavity.

  In stark contrast, the ASCO-bound system undergoes a dramatic topological localizes of the conformational landscape (Fig. 5B), where 98.5% of the ensemble is funneled into a single dominant macrostate. Although the macroscopic topology of the ASCO energy landscape appears visually similar to UQ2 in its broad coordinate bounds, quantitative analysis reveals a significantly narrower, steeper global energy minimum. This deep and highly localized energy basin indicates severe kinetic restriction, effectively trapping the enzyme in a non-productive conformation [35]. The corresponding MFPT network for ASCO (Fig. 5D) further highlights the absence of efficient transitions between states, demonstrating that the ASCO-bound system is kinetically isolated compared to the fluid interconversions observed in the UQ₂-bound ensemble (Fig. 5C).

  Structurally, this kinetic confinement is driven by the binding mode of ASCO, which penetrates deeply into the catalytic pocket and forms persistent stabilizing interactions, particularly near key residues such as Tyr220. These interactions reinforce the rigidification of the active site, reducing conformational flexibility and suppressing the large-scale structural exploration necessary for catalysis.

Trajectory analysis reveals that this trapped conformation is rigidly stabilized by a persistent hydrogen-bond network within the active site primarily anchored by key residues such as Tyr220 and Glu123. This effectively clamps the surrounding loops into a closed conformation. The extreme persistence of these inhibitor-stabilized contacts provides the structural rationale for the deep energy basin observed in the MSM analysis, confirming that the inhibitor effectively locks the enzyme into a non-productive, kinetically confined conformation.

  In striking contrast, the ASCO-bound system (Fig. 5C) exhibits a fundamentally different structural behavior. The inhibitor penetrates deeply into the hydrophobic pocket and forms a persistent hydrogen-bond network with key residues Tyr220 and Glu123, characterized by short and stable interaction distances (~ 2.6–2.7 Å). These interactions act as a structural anchor that rigidifies the active site and effectively clamps the surrounding loops (L1 and L2) into a closed conformation.

  This rigidification eliminates the spectrum of binding configurations observed in the substrate-bound state, collapsing the system into a single dominant macrostate, consistent with the 98.5% population observed in MSM (Fig. 5). The absence of intermediate interaction states and the persistence of strong hydrogen bonds indicate that the inhibitor enforces a deep, energetically favorable basin from which escape is kinetically unfavorable.

  Mechanistically, this transition can be understood as a shift from dynamic equilibrium to kinetic confinement. While UQ₂ maintains a balance between multiple conformational states, ASCO suppresses this heterogeneity by stabilizing a single, rigid configuration. The fluctuating, intermittent interactions observed for the substrate contrast sharply with the uniform, highly persistent hydrogen bonds in the ASCO-bound state (Fig. 3C), highlighting the loss of conformational variability upon inhibitor binding.

  Taken together, Fig. 5 provides direct structural evidence that high-affinity inhibition arises not merely from steric occlusion, but from ligand-induced restriction of conformational entropy. By eliminating access to alternative substates and suppressing global and local motions, ASCO effectively decouples the enzyme from the thermal fluctuations required for catalytic turnover. This dynamic restriction mechanism explains both the prolonged residence time and the exceptional potency of the inhibitor, and is consistent with emerging models of inhibition in interfacial and dynamically regulated enzymes [27, 28].


### Thermodynamic compensation: the “Whipping” tail
  Another interesting feature of the inhibitor-bound ensemble is the “local locking, distal whipping” phenomenon observed from the RMSF profiles. While the apo baseline demonstrates that the C-terminus possesses intrinsic flexibility, ASCO binding exacerbates this disorder, causing the tail to fluctuate even more intensely than in the completely empty enzyme. The rigidification of the active site upon ASCO binding is thermodynamically “paid for” by a massive increase in the entropic disorder of the C-terminus. This gives a structural rationale for the role of the C-terminal tail, which is not as highly conserved among AOX species as the catalytic core [29]. We suggest that this region acts as an “entropy sink,” which allows the protein to tolerate the rigidification of its catalytic core without incurring a prohibitive free energy penalty. This is akin to the entropic reservoir effect observed in calmodulin signaling [36].


### Implications for drug design: optimizing residence time
  These results are of great importance in designing the anti-trypanosomal agents of the next generation. Conventional drug discovery can be optimized with respect to thermodynamic affinity (IC50), which may favor fast-on, fast-off binders. Nevertheless, in the case of a target such as AOX, a high local concentration of ubiquinol in the membrane should also be considered a target of competition, and in this case, residence time is a superior predictor of in vivo efficacy [34]. The dynamic restriction behavior observed for ASCO represents the ideal profile for a long-acting antiparasitic. Future medicinal chemistry efforts should then focus on bridging the residues, including the Tyr220-inhibitor network, that stabilize this “closed” macrostate and thus enhance efficacy by deepening the dynamic restriction [10].


### Insights, comparisons, and limitations
  While static structures like the ferulenol-bound reference (PDB ID: 5ZDP) capture a singular, energy-minimized state dictated by crystal lattice constraints, our trajectories map the fluid, solution-phase behavior of the enzyme. A key insight gained here is that inhibitor potency is not merely a product of fixed, static steric occlusion within the active site. Instead, the MD simulations demonstrate that inhibitors function by severely restricting the protein’s accessible phase space, confining it to a deeply stabilized energetic basin. This directly contrasts with the native substrate analogue (UQ2), which maintains highly plastic, multi-state conformational sampling. Nevertheless, several boundaries of this study must be acknowledged. While the present study captures the key conformational transitions underlying AOX inhibition, it does not explicitly model l the chemical step of quinol oxidation at the diiron catalytic center. As such, the coupling between the inhibitor-induced structural confinement and the electronic processes governing substrate oxidation remains unresolved. Furthermore, due to the microsecond timescale limitations of classical molecular dynamics, these simulations cannot directly quantify absolute macroscopic ligand dissociation rates (koff) or absolute thermodynamic binding free energies [35].

  Future investigations employing QM/MM approaches will be essential to probe how the trapped conformation influences electronic coupling and catalytic reactivity at the diiron site, particularly in relation to proton-coupled electron transfer mechanisms [30]. Such analyses would provide a more complete description of how structural and electronic factors converge to inhibit enzymatic function.

  Despite this limitation, the current work establishes a robust dynamic and kinetic framework for understanding AOX inhibition. By integrating molecular dynamics, principal component analysis, and MSM, this study shifts the conventional perspective from a static, structure-based view of inhibition toward a dynamic, landscape-driven paradigm, in which ligand efficacy is governed by conformational restriction and dynamic restriction.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we integrated microsecond-scale molecular dynamics simulations with MSM and principal component analysis to define the atomistic and thermodynamic basis of potent inhibition in T. brucei AOX. We show that catalytic competence in the AOX–UQ2 complex arises from a fluid, highly connected free energy landscape, where the enzyme samples three metastable macrostates that enable rapid conformational interconversion and functional plasticity. In contrast, the high-affinity inhibitor ASCO exerts its picomolar potency by inducing a profound localization of this landscape. Rather than acting as a passive steric blocker, ASCO funnels 98.5% of the conformational ensemble into a single, deeply stabilized energy basin, thereby suppressing large-scale collective motions and kinetically isolating the enzyme from catalytically productive pathways. This trapped state is structurally reinforced by a persistent hydrogen-bond network at the active site, while the associated entropic penalty is compensated by increased distal flexibility at the C-terminus. Importantly, the parallel behavior observed for ferulenol confirms that this mechanism is not unique to ASCO; rather, both inhibitors share a unified mode of action by inducing substantial dynamic restriction and kinetically confining the enzyme, in stark contrast to the conformational plasticity maintained by the native substrate analogue UQ2. Together, these findings establish a unifying mechanism of inhibition based on dynamic restriction, in which ligand binding restricts conformational entropy and prolongs residence time by stabilizing a non-productive macrostate. More broadly, this work reframes AOX inhibition and potentially other interfacial enzyme systems, as a problem of conformational landscape control, offering a quantitative framework for designing next-generation inhibitors that exploit dynamic restriction and dynamic regulation rather than static binding affinity alone.
