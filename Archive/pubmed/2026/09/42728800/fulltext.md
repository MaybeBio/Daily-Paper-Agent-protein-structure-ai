# UFold-X: an enhanced Dual & Dynamic U-Mamba model for long-range RNA secondary structure prediction


## Abstract
  RNA secondary structure is essential for understanding the functions of non-coding RNAs, ribosomal RNAs, and viral genomes. However, accurate prediction of long RNA structures remains challenging due to complex long-range interactions and the limited availability of long-RNA training data. We present UFold-X, a dual-branch deep learning framework that combines a convolutional encoder for local structure modeling with a Mamba-based Visual State Space Module for capturing long-range dependencies. A dynamic gating mechanism adaptively integrates the two branches according to sequence length. UFold-X was evaluated on multiple benchmark datasets containing RNAs up to 5000 nucleotides. To rigorously assess generalization, we introduced a cross-clan benchmark for long RNAs. Under this stringent setting, UFold-X achieved performance comparable to state-of-the-art classical approaches while achieving the best performance among deep learning-based methods. Additional cross-family and within-family evaluations further demonstrated robust transferability and competitive predictive performance. UFold-X also maintained excellent computational efficiency, requiring only 0.08 s per sequence on average. To assess biological consistency, we developed a SHAPE-based reactivity prediction variant (UFold-X-R) and an integrated metric, the Hybrid Reactivity-Pairing Score (HRPS). UFold-X-R showed strong agreement with experimental icSHAPE data and achieved the highest HRPS among all evaluated methods. A user-friendly web server is available at https://ufold-x.ai4bread.com.


## Introduction
  RNA molecules play diverse roles in cellular and viral processes, functioning not only as carriers of genetic information but also as regulators and catalysts in transcription, translation, and splicing [1, 2]. The functional versatility of RNA is closely linked to its ability to form secondary structures through intramolecular base-pairing, which gives rise to characteristic motifs such as hairpins, bulges, internal loops, and pseudoknots [3–7]. These structural elements influence RNA stability, localization, and molecular interactions, underscoring the importance of accurate secondary structure characterization in both basic research and RNA-based biomedical applications [8–10].

  While experimental techniques such as NMR spectroscopy and X-ray crystallography can provide high-resolution insights into RNA structure, they are labor-intensive and constrained by sequence size or crystallization limitations [11–13]. Chemical probing methods, including SHAPE, DMS, and icSHAPE, selectively modify unpaired nucleotides and provide information about nucleotide pairing states through sequencing-based readouts, thereby offering experimental constraints on RNA secondary structure [14–18]. Although these methods have advanced transcriptome-wide RNA structure profiling, they remain sensitive to RNA quality and cellular conditions, and are often impractical for large-scale or long-transcript analysis [19, 20].

  As a result, computational structure prediction has become a practical and widely adopted alternative. Classical algorithms based on thermodynamic optimization (e.g. Mfold, ViennaRNA, RNAstructure) [21–24] or comparative genomics [25–27] have achieved success on small, conserved RNAs. However, they often struggle with long-range base pairs and complex topologies like pseudoknots. Deep learning-based models have recently emerged to directly learn base-pairing patterns from sequence data [28–31], offering improved flexibility and performance. Still, most are trained on short sequences and exhibit limited generalization to long RNAs, such as lncRNAs, viral genomes, and untranslated regions (UTRs), which frequently exceed 600 nucleotides in length [32].

  While RNA secondary structure prediction has advanced significantly, existing methods remain limited in their ability to accurately model long RNA sequences—defined here as transcripts exceeding 600 nucleotides. This limitation is critical, as long RNAs are both abundant and biologically important. Large-scale databases such as RNAcentral report that over 60% of annotated RNAs exceed this threshold [32]. These long transcripts include lncRNAs, circular RNAs, viral genomes, and extended untranslated regions (UTRs), which frequently form complex secondary structures essential for their biological function.

  The secondary structures of long RNAs are closely linked to regulatory mechanisms, including nuclear export, transcript stability, RNA–protein interactions, and subcellular localization. For example, the 17 kb Xist lncRNA forms multiple structured domains that orchestrate X chromosome inactivation [33], and the ∼30 kb SARS-CoV-2 RNA genome adopts conserved stem-loop elements that modulate replication, translation, and immune evasion [34]. Therefore, decoding the secondary structure of long RNAs is fundamental to understanding their functional roles and guiding therapeutic targeting.

  While thermodynamic algorithms such as RNAfold and Mfold are foundational tools, their cubic time complexity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $O(L^3)$\end{document}) limits applicability to long sequences. LinearFold [35], a notable advancement, improves runtime to linear complexity through beam search and uses the same nearest-neighbor energy model as RNAfold in the ViennaRNA package; however, because it prunes partial solutions during beam search, it may miss the true minimum free energy structure under the model. Deep learning models, although faster and more flexible, are often trained on short RNAs and struggle to capture global pairing patterns in longer transcripts.

  This creates a methodological gap: existing models either cannot scale to long sequences or cannot accurately capture their structural complexity. Addressing this gap requires a new modeling paradigm that is both biologically informed and computationally scalable—one that can integrate local pairing features and global context, adapt to varying sequence lengths, and maintain robustness across diverse RNA types. Therefore, improving RNA structure prediction for long-range contexts is not only a response to model deficiencies but also a proactive step toward meeting urgent biological demands.

  Recent advances in structured state space models (SSMs) offer a promising direction for efficient long-range dependency modeling. Mamba-based architectures have demonstrated strong performance in sequence modeling through selective state-space transitions. Building upon this foundation, U-Mamba [36] integrates Mamba blocks into a U-Net-style encoder-decoder architecture by replacing convolutional layers with state-space modules, enabling enhanced global context modeling while preserving hierarchical resolution transitions. In this design, two-dimensional feature maps are typically reshaped into token sequences and processed by one-dimensional state-space transitions, where spatial dependencies are implicitly captured through flattened representations.

  VMamba [37] further extends this paradigm by introducing Visual State Space Modules (VSSMs), which redesign the selective scanning mechanism to better align with two-dimensional spatial structures through structured multi-directional traversal. By preserving spatial consistency during state propagation, VMamba enables more explicit modeling of 2D interactions while retaining linear-time complexity. While conceptually appealing, such models are primarily designed for spatially continuous data and do not explicitly account for the discrete, pairwise constraints and symmetry inherent to RNA secondary structure formation. Moreover, their static fusion strategies limit adaptability across RNA sequences of varying lengths.

  To address the limitations of existing models in predicting RNA secondary structures for long transcripts, we propose UFold-X (Dual & Dynamic U-Mamba RNA Folding), a deep learning framework designed with biological insight and scalable modeling capacity. UFold-X is based on the observation that RNA structure formation involves both local pairing motifs and long-range interactions that span hundreds or thousands of nucleotides. For clarity, we define a long-range interaction as a base pair formed between nucleotides i and j that are widely separated along the primary sequence, with a sequence distance |i - j| ≥ L/2 nucleotides, where L denotes the length of the RNA sequence, unless stated otherwise. To model this effectively, UFold-X adopts a dual-branch architecture: a convolutional branch captures local stem-like structures, while a second branch leverages Mamba-based VSSM [38] to capture long-distance structural dependencies efficiently. A dynamic gating mechanism adaptively balances the contributions of these two branches based on input sequence length, allowing the model to generalize across RNAs of diverse sizes and structural complexity. This biologically inspired design reflects the hierarchical nature of RNA folding, where both proximal and distal interactions contribute to functional structure. Instead of focusing solely on architecture, UFold-X emphasizes adaptability to real biological sequences, particularly those that exceed the typical length range of training datasets.

  We evaluate UFold-X on multiple benchmark datasets spanning diverse RNA lengths and structural complexities. To rigorously assess model generalization, we introduce a systematic cross-clan benchmark for long RNA sequences, which serves as the primary evaluation setting in this study. Under this stringent protocol, UFold-X achieves performance comparable to well-established energy-based approaches while remaining the strongest deep learning-based method. We further perform cross-family and conventional within-family evaluations, where UFold-X consistently demonstrates strong transferability and competitive predictive performance across diverse RNA families and datasets. Beyond benchmark accuracy, UFold-X exhibits robust long-range interaction modeling capability, effective prediction of non-canonical base pairs, and excellent computational efficiency, requiring only 0.080 s per sequence on average.

  To further assess the biological relevance of predicted structures, we developed a SHAPE-based reactivity prediction variant (UFold-X-R) and introduced an integrated evaluation metric, the Hybrid Reactivity-Pairing Score (HRPS). UFold-X-R was trained on short-sequence SHAPE datasets and then applied to long RNAs, where its predicted reactivity profiles showed strong consistency with experimental icSHAPE measurements. By combining reactivity-structure correlation with pairing accuracy, the HRPS analysis confirms that UFold-X achieves both structural precision and biochemical interpretability, highlighting its potential as a biologically reliable framework for RNA structure prediction. A user-friendly web server is available at https://ufold-x.ai4bread.com.


## Materials and methods

### Overview of the UFold-X framework
  UFold-X is a deep learning-based framework designed to predict RNA secondary structures with high accuracy across a wide range of sequence lengths. It builds upon our previous work, UFold [31], which modeled RNA folding as an image-to-image translation task using a U-Net-based architecture. While UFold demonstrated strong performance on short and medium-length RNAs by capturing local pairing patterns, it faced limitations in generalizing to longer sequences due to its limited receptive field.

  To overcome this, UFold-X extends the UFold architecture by introducing a dual-branch design and a dynamic gating mechanism. Specifically, the local branch retains UFold’s convolutional backbone to detect short-range structural motifs such as stems and hairpins. In parallel, a new global branch incorporates VSSM based on the Mamba architecture [38], enabling efficient modeling of long-range base-pairing interactions. The dynamic gating mechanism adaptively balances the outputs of both branches based on input sequence length, allowing the model to scale effectively from short RNAs to ultra-long transcripts.

  UFold-X takes as input a multi-channel representation of the RNA sequence, encoding potential base-pairing signals and positional relationships. The model outputs a contact probability matrix, which is then decoded into a valid secondary structure through a post-processing step that enforces structural constraints. This biologically inspired design allows UFold-X to generalize beyond the length limitations of prior models, while retaining interpretability and high efficiency. The architecture is optimized for both accuracy and scalability, enabling secondary structure prediction for RNAs spanning thousands of nucleotides in under a second.


### Datasets

#### Benchmark datasets
  To comprehensively evaluate the performance and generalization ability of UFold-X—particularly on long and structurally diverse RNAs—we utilize a total of eight benchmark datasets, including both classical collections and newly constructed long-sequence sets.

  Our main training and testing source is the RNAstralign dataset [39], which contains 30 451 sequences across diverse RNA families. We preprocess it into three subsets: RNAstralign-600 (shorter than 600 nt), RNAstralign-1800 (600–1800 nt), and the full RNAstralign-all. RNAstralign-600 is split into 20 862 training and 2608 testing sequences; RNAstralign-1800 into 8793 training and 1035 testing sequences. This setup allows us to assess performance across both common short RNAs and moderately long sequences. All duplicates between training and testing sets are removed following E2EFold and MXFold2.

  We additionally include ArchiveII [40], a widely used benchmark of 3975 mostly short RNAs from 10 families, to enable comparison with prior work. To evaluate cross-dataset generalization on long sequences, we construct the RCSB dataset from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) [41]. Specifically, RNA-containing structures are first filtered from the PDB according to predefined length criteria, and the corresponding mmCIF files are downloaded. Secondary structures are then extracted using RNApdbee 2.0 [42] with all parameters set to their default values to ensure standardization. The resulting dataset is divided into RCSB1800 (40 sequences, length 600–1800 nt) and RCSB4000 (61 sequences, length 2800–5000 nt), with the latter serving as the evaluation benchmark for ultra-long sequences.

  To further enrich long-sequence coverage, we incorporate two additional structure databases from RNAsolo2 [43], a self-updating 3D RNA structure resource. The BGSU dataset (v4.4) includes 4494 representative RNAs across 19 577 structures, and the Rfam dataset (v15.0) provides 13 859 RNA structures from 136 of 4170 families. From each, we select sequences in two length ranges: BGSU1800/Rfam1800 (≤1800 nt) and BGSU5000/Rfam5000 (600–5000 nt). These subsets provide additional long and ultra-long RNAs from diverse structural contexts, supplementing both biological variety and structural complexity in our evaluation. The length distributions of all subsets are visualized in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S1.

  In summary, the training datasets used in this study include the RNAstralign-600 training set, RNAstralign-1800 training set, RNAstralign-all training set, RCSB1800, BGSU1800, BGSU5000, Rfam1800, and Rfam5000. The testing datasets include the RNAstralign-600 test set, RNAstralign-1800 test set, RNAstralign-all test set, ArchiveII, BGSU1800 test set, BGSU5000 test set, Rfam1800 test set, Rfam5000 test set and RCSB4000.


#### The RNA Mapping Database chemical reactivity dataset
  To train and evaluate the reactivity prediction framework (UFold-X-R), we used chemical mapping data from the RNA Mapping Database (RMDB) [44], which aggregates experimentally measured nucleotide-wise reactivities from diverse RNA constructs. A substantial portion of this dataset originates from the Eterna “Cloud Labs” initiative, where thousands of community-designed RNAs were synthesized and probed using selective 2′-hydroxyl acylation analyzed by primer extension (SHAPE) [45] and multiplexed chemical mapping protocols. Following the refinement described in DSRNAFold [46], we obtained 12 711 nonredundant constructs ranging from 101 to 1088 nucleotides. Each nucleotide is annotated with a quantitative reactivity score representing its chemical accessibility and pairing status. An 80:20 split was applied for training and testing UFold-X-R, using the experimental reactivity as the regression target. This dataset enables learning of the quantitative relationship between sequence context and biochemical reactivity, providing a strong foundation for generalizing to long RNAs.


### Redundancy reduction and data leakage prevention
  To ensure a comprehensive and reliable evaluation of model performance, we adopt different redundancy control and dataset partitioning strategies under different evaluation settings. For within-dataset evaluations, we strictly follow the original train/test splits provided by each benchmark dataset (e.g. RNAstralign), which are publicly available and widely adopted in prior work. For cross-dataset evaluations (e.g. training on RNAstralign-1800 and testing on RCSB1800/RCSB4000), we apply CD-HIT with a 90% sequence-identity threshold to reduce high sequence redundancy between training and test sets. Specifically, any test RNA sharing >90% sequence identity with a training RNA is excluded from the test dataset. We note that this procedure is intended to reduce sequence-level redundancy rather than to eliminate all homologous relationships, since homologous RNAs may still exhibit relatively low sequence identity. To further evaluate model transferability and generalization beyond sequence-identity-based filtering, we introduce additional homology-aware evaluation settings based on Rfam annotations. Specifically, the cross-family benchmark separates training and test RNAs at the Rfam family level and is used to assess transferability across distinct RNA families. Furthermore, we establish a more stringent cross-clan benchmark by enforcing separation at the Rfam clan level. Since Rfam clans represent higher-order homology groupings, the cross-clan benchmark serves as the primary evaluation setting for assessing homology-independent generalization in this study.

## Results
  To comprehensively evaluate the performance of RNA secondary structure prediction models under progressively more challenging evaluation settings, we organized the benchmarks according to the degree of distribution shift and the level of homology separation between the training and test sets. Specifically, the benchmarks can be categorized into four levels: within-family evaluation, cross-dataset evaluation, cross-family evaluation, and cross-clan evaluation.

  Within-family evaluation follows the conventional benchmark setting widely adopted in previous RNA secondary structure prediction studies and is primarily used for direct comparison with existing methods under commonly used experimental protocols. In this setting, the training and test RNAs are derived from the same data source and may still contain substantial homologous relationships.

  Cross-dataset evaluation introduces distributional differences by training and testing on different data sources, thereby providing an assessment of model transferability across distinct RNA distributions. Building upon this setting, we further introduce cross-family and cross-clan evaluations, which progressively increase the level of homology separation between the training and test sets.

  Cross-family evaluation separates the training and test RNAs at the Rfam family level. Although this setting partially alleviates homolog leakage and provides a more challenging assessment of transferability across RNA families, certain RNA families may still belong to the same higher-order evolutionary clan. Therefore, cross-family evaluation should be regarded as an intermediate assessment of transferability rather than a fully homology-independent benchmark.

  Cross-clan evaluation represents the most stringent benchmark setting in this study. By enforcing clan-level separation according to Rfam annotations, it minimizes homologous relationships between training and testing sets and provides the primary evidence for assessing model generalization beyond homologous RNA groups.

  We note that within-family and cross-family benchmarks are retained because they remain widely used in the current literature and facilitate direct comparison with previous studies. However, the cross-clan benchmark is regarded as the primary evaluation setting for assessing rigorous model generalization in this work.

  For the evaluation of non-canonical base-pair prediction, we adopt the same datasets and preprocessing pipeline as those used in the canonical base-pair experiments, while maintaining identical model architecture and training procedures. The only modification is introduced during the post-processing stage: when converting the predicted probabilistic contact matrix into the final optimal binary contact matrix, the canonical base-pairing constraints typically imposed during the decoding process are removed.

  In all experiments, we compare the performance of UFold-X with several state-of-the-art baselines under a unified preprocessing pipeline and evaluation protocol, ensuring fairness and consistency across models and datasets. The benchmark comparisons used RNAstructure v6.2, Mfold v3.6, LinearFold v1.0, EternaFold v1.3.1, CONTRAfold v2.02, SPOT-RNA (GitHub commit: dfd0277), e2efold (GitHub commit: 2aab9e7), and UFold v1.3.


### Performance under conventional within-family evaluation
  Within-family evaluation follows the conventional benchmark setting widely adopted in previous RNA secondary structure prediction studies. To comprehensively assess model performance under this setting, we considered four representative scenarios covering short RNAs (<  600 nt), mixed-length RNAs (0–1800 nt), long RNAs (600–1800 nt), and ultra-long RNAs (up to 5000 nt). These evaluations enable us to examine model behavior across diverse sequence-length ranges while maintaining direct comparability with previous studies.


#### Mixed-length RNA benchmark (RNAstralign-all)
  We first assess the general applicability of UFold-X across sequences of varying lengths using the RNAstralign-all dataset. The training set contains 29 655 RNA sequences ranging from 0 to 1800 nucleotides in length, while the test set includes 1428 sequences within the same length range.

  This mixed-length setting provides a comprehensive assessment of model performance across a broad range of RNA lengths and serves as the most representative conventional benchmark in this study.

  Notably, most existing secondary structure prediction models are trained primarily on short sequences and tend to degrade significantly when applied to longer RNAs.

  As can be observed from Fig. 3A, most traditional RNA secondary structure prediction models are designed for short sequences and exhibit noticeable performance degradation on longer RNAs. Traditional thermodynamics- or statistics-based models, such as RNAstructure (0.441), LinearFold (0.493), EternaFold (0.506) [50], and CONTRAfold (0.519) [51], show limited performance on this mixed-length dataset.

  This decline is primarily attributable to the exponential growth of the structural search space for longer RNAs, which makes accurate structure selection increasingly difficult. In practice, additional heuristic constraints [52] introduced for computational efficiency may further affect performance on very long-range interactions.

  Although SPOT-RNA (0.443) incorporates a Bi-LSTM module for global context modeling, it is primarily designed for mid-length sequences and lacks structural inductive bias for efficiently handling long-range base-pairing interactions. Furthermore, its LSTM layers are relatively shallow, and the model does not implement architectural optimizations or sparse attention mechanisms necessary for scaling to ultra-long RNA sequences. As a result, SPOT-RNA’s performance degrades significantly on long RNAs despite its LSTM-based backbone.

  Among the tested methods, e2efold achieves a relatively better performance with an F1 score of 0.662. Notably, this result is obtained by applying two separate models—e2efold-short and e2efold-long—to short and long sequences in the test set, respectively. This length-specific evaluation protocol gives e2efold an inherent advantage over methods that rely on a unified model for all sequence lengths.

  UFold, which adopts a fully convolutional U-Net architecture, achieves an F1 score of 0.773 on the RNAstralign-all test set when retrained under the same conditions. However, its reliance on local convolutional receptive fields limits its ability to model long-range base-pairing interactions, leading to performance degradation on longer sequences. In contrast, our model UFold-X reaches an F1 score of 0.920, representing a 19.0% improvement. This gain is attributed to UFold-X’s dual-branch architecture that integrates local and global modeling through a VSSM module, as well as a dynamic gating mechanism that adapts to sequence length. These enhancements enable UFold-X to capture complex RNA secondary structures more effectively across diverse length scales. Supplementary Fig. S3 further presents the performance comparison of UFold-X based on two different dynamic gating mechanisms on the RNAstralign-all dataset. Supplementary Table S1 reports the performance of different methods on RNA sequences shorter than 600 nt and those longer than 600 nt within the RNAstralign-all dataset. These results collectively demonstrate the limitations of existing models when applied to mixed-length RNA datasets and highlight the effectiveness of UFold-X in achieving robust and scalable performance across variable-length sequences.


#### Long-RNA benchmark (RNAstralign-1800)
  In this part, we evaluate the performance of UFold-X on the RNAstralign-1800 dataset, which includes RNA sequences ranging from 600 to 1800 nucleotides. This benchmark is particularly well-suited for assessing the ability of models to handle medium- to long-range dependencies. Due to the narrower sequence length range (600–1800 nucleotides) in the RNAstralign-1800 dataset, we are able to train a larger version of UFold-X under the same memory constraints. Specifically, we leverage the gated mechanism to activate only the global VSSM branch and omit the convolutional U-Net branch during training. This allows for a parameter-rich model with improved capacity, increasing the number of trainable parameters from 7.20 million (in the default full model) to 10.67 million for this setting. Figure 3B presents the F1 scores of our model and several representative baseline methods, and Supplementary Table S2 provides more detailed indicator values.

  Among the baseline methods, RNAstructure (0.370) and LinearFold (0.427) are classical thermodynamics- or statistics-based methods that do not require training and can be directly applied to predict RNA secondary structures. However, due to their limited modeling capacity, especially for complex or long-range structures, their performance on this dataset remains low. In addition, CONTRAfold (0.460) and EternaFold (0.456) represent probabilistic, learning-based approaches that leverage conditional log-linear models to capture RNA folding patterns from data. Unlike purely thermodynamic methods, these models incorporate data-driven parameter estimation, enabling improved flexibility in modeling structural preferences.

  For learning-based neural models, we retrained both e2efold and UFold using the RNAstralign-1800 training set. SPOT-RNA, originally designed for sequences under 600 nt, fails to train on longer inputs due to architectural constraints, and we thus used its pre-trained model for inference. As shown in Fig. 3B, SPOT-RNA achieves an F1 score of only 0.334. While it incorporates an LSTM-based module, the design lacks structural inductive biases and scalability mechanisms, making it less effective on longer sequences.

  By contrast, the retrained e2efold achieves an F1 score of 0.686, while UFold, trained under the same conditions, improves to 0.900. To ensure fairness, we also retrained UFold-X on the same dataset (denoted UFold-X), which achieves the highest overall performance: an F1 score of 0.955, a precision of 0.976, and a recall of 0.934, surpassing UFold by 6.1%, 6.6%, and 4.1%, respectively. These improvements are attributed to UFold-X’s dual-branch architecture, which combines local (short-range) convolutional modeling with the global (long-range) dependency modeling capability of the VSSM encoder. The dynamic gating mechanism further enhances adaptation to varying sequence lengths.

  In this experiment, both UFold-X and UFold restrict post-processing to canonical A-U, C-G, and G-U base pairs. We further investigate their performance under relaxed constraints that allow non-canonical base pairs. As shown in Fig. 3C, Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Fig. S4, UFold-X achieves an improved F1 score of 0.981, which represents a 2.72% gain over the canonical-only version and an 8.16% improvement over UFold under the same condition.

  To better assess UFold-X’s ability to model long-range dependencies, we conduct a focused experiment on long-range base pairs (defined as base pairs with distance greater than L/2). We extract 352 RNA sequences from the RNAstralign-1800 test set that contain such pairs and evaluate model performance on this subset. The results are summarized in Fig. 3D, Supplementary Table S4, and Supplementary Table S5.

  From Fig. 3D, we observe that traditional methods perform poorly in this setting, with F1 scores below 0.05. Learning-based models perform better, with e2efold reaching 0.202 and UFold achieving 0.315. In contrast, UFold-X achieves an F1 score of 0.590, precision of 0.461, and recall of 0.911, which represent relative improvements of 87.3%, 119.5%, and 9.5% over UFold, respectively. These results confirm the effectiveness of the VSSM encoder in capturing long-range structural features, which is a key reason for UFold-X’s superior performance on long RNA sequences.

  Finally, we compare the robustness of UFold-X and UFold across different sequence length ranges within the RNAstralign-1800 test set. Supplementary Table S6 shows that for sequences longer than 1500 nt, UFold exhibits a 1.5% drop in F1 score, whereas UFold-X shows only a 0.2% decrease. This suggests that UFold-X maintains stronger robustness as sequence length increases, a property we further validate on the RCSB dataset in the next section.

  To rigorously evaluate UFold-X under consistent conditions, we benchmark its performance using both within-dataset and within-family splits across four representative datasets: BGSU1800, Rfam1800, BGSU5000, and Rfam5000. Each dataset is randomly divided into training and testing subsets, and the reported results reflect the average F1 scores on the held-out test sets. Detailed split ratios and family-wise counts are listed in Supplementary Tables S7–S14.

  Figure 3E and Supplementary Table S7 summarize the results on BGSU1800. Traditional thermodynamic and statistical baselines, including RNAstructure (0.367), Mfold (0.417), LinearFold (0.410), EternaFold (0.424), and CONTRAfold (0.441), yield relatively low F1 scores. Among learning-based models, SPOT-RNA (0.341) and e2efold (0.298) underperform, whereas UFold achieves 0.864. UFold-X further improves to 0.877, demonstrating that its dual-branch design effectively integrates local and nonlocal dependencies under identical within-dataset conditions. Results under non-canonical base-pairing settings are reported in Supplementary Table S8.

  We next evaluate model robustness on the Rfam1800 dataset (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Table S9). Traditional methods, including RNAstructure (0.380), Mfold (0.457), LinearFold (0.445), EternaFold (0.451), and CONTRAfold (0.458), achieve moderate performance, while SPOT-RNA (0.364) and e2efold (0.376) remain limited. UFold reaches an F1 of 0.917, and UFold-X achieves 0.937, representing a 2.2% improvement under the same split protocol. Non-canonical pairing results are provided in Supplementary Table S10.

  To examine scalability on long and structurally complex RNAs, we apply the same within-dataset protocol to BGSU5000 and Rfam5000, which include all sequences shorter than 5000 nucleotides. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S5 and Supplementary Tables S11 and S12, UFold-X achieves F1 scores of 0.894 (BGSU5000) and 0.911 (Rfam5000), surpassing UFold by 0.2% and 3.5%, respectively. Under non-canonical pairing conditions (Supplementary Tables S13 and S14), the relative ranking remains unchanged, further confirming the model’s robustness across varying sequence lengths.

  In addition to overall performance, we further conduct a within-family analysis on Rfam5000 to assess UFold-X’s consistency across distinct RNA families. Three representative families—RF02540 (Bacterial LSU rRNA), RF02541 (Archaeal LSU rRNA), and RF02543 (Eukaryotic LSU rRNA)—are selected for detailed evaluation, containing 36, 111, and 51 test sequences, respectively. As illustrated in Fig. 4A and Supplementary Figs S6 and S7, UFold-X achieves F1-scores of 0.867, 0.918, and 0.814 on the three families, exceeding UFold by 5.1%, 4.0%, and 14.2%, respectively. These results demonstrate that UFold-X maintains high predictive accuracy and family-level consistency within the same dataset, effectively modeling conserved and divergent structural patterns across RNA lineages.


#### Validation on conventional short-RNA benchmarks (ArchiveII)
  Although our primary focus is on modeling long and ultra-long RNAs, we also evaluate UFold-X on short RNA sequences to ensure its general applicability. For this, we train UFold-X on the RNAstralign-600 dataset and test it on the widely used ArchiveII benchmark. In this experiment, we further evaluate RNAformer [53] and MXfold2 [29]. RNAformer is a transformer-based deep learning framework that models pairwise nucleotide interactions using attention mechanisms. MXfold2 combines thermodynamic folding principles with deep neural network-based scoring, providing a hybrid framework that integrates deep learning with classical RNA folding models. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S8, UFold-X achieves an F1 score of 0.880, which is comparable to UFold and clearly outperforms other mainstream RNA secondary structure prediction methods. These results suggest that UFold-X maintains competitive accuracy even on short sequences, further supporting its robustness across a wide range of RNA lengths.


### Cross-dataset evaluation

#### Transferability to independent RCSB datasets
  To evaluate transferability across different RNA data distributions, we test UFold-X on experimentally validated RNA structures collected from the PDB. These sequences, derived from the RCSB database, differ substantially from the training data in terms of source, sequence composition, and structural characteristics, providing a realistic cross-dataset evaluation scenario.

  We curated 101 RNA sequences ranging from 600 to 5000 nucleotides, filtering out redundant or incomplete entries and ensuring experimental resolution better than 3 Å. To facilitate length-dependent evaluation, the sequences were divided into two subsets: RCSB1800 (600–1800 nt) and RCSB4000 (2800–5000 nt). All models were trained solely on the RNAstralign-1800 dataset, ensuring no overlap between training and testing sequences.

  As summarized in Fig. 4B, Supplementary Tables S15 and S16, UFold-X slightly outperforms UFold on the RCSB1800 subset, achieving comparable accuracy to its training distribution and confirming its stability on unseen but length-matched sequences. However, the difference becomes more pronounced on the RCSB4000 subset: UFold-X attains an F1 score over 10 times higher than UFold and substantially surpasses e2efold-long, demonstrating superior extrapolation capacity to ultra-long sequences. Representative visualization examples are shown in Supplementary Figs S21 and S22, where UFold-X preserves correct long-range pairings even in highly structured RNAs.


#### Local-window analysis on ultra-long RNAs
  To further investigate UFold-X’s behavior on ultra-long transcripts, we performed a local-window analysis to determine whether the model can accurately recover local structural domains within long molecules. For each RNA in RCSB4000, a sliding window of 1500 nucleotides with a stride of 10 nt was applied across the entire sequence. Each segment was independently predicted, and the segment with the highest F1 score was retained as the representative result. This setup simulates realistic conditions in long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), which often contain semi-independent structural subdomains of similar length.

  As shown in Supplementary Fig. S9 and Supplementary Table S17, this analysis significantly improves the mean F1 score of UFold-X by 78.8% to 0.522, ~10.4 times higher than UFold under identical settings. The results indicate that UFold-X can generalize by capturing biologically meaningful local structures even when the global sequence context extends far beyond its training range.


### Cross-family evaluation
  Cross-family evaluation provides an intermediate assessment of model transferability across distinct RNA families while partially reducing homolog leakage relative to conventional benchmark settings. Although RNA families are separated according to Rfam annotations, certain families may still belong to the same higher-order evolutionary clan. Therefore, this evaluation focuses primarily on family-level transferability rather than fully homology-independent generalization.


#### Transferability across long-RNA families
  The preceding results demonstrate that UFold-X maintains robust performance within datasets and on previously unseen RNAs. To further assess transferability across long-RNA datasets and related RNA families, we conduct additional evaluations using the BGSU5000 and Rfam5000 datasets.

  We first examine the model’s ability to transfer from one long-RNA dataset to another. UFold-X pretrained on the BGSU5000 dataset was evaluated on three representative long RNA families—RF02540, RF02541, and RF02543—from the Rfam5000 test split. These sequences correspond to archaeal, bacterial, and eukaryotic large subunit ribosomal RNAs, respectively. We apply CD-HIT filtering (sequence identity threshold 0.9) to minimize potential overlap between training and test sequences.

  As shown in Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S10, UFold-X achieves F1-scores of 0.866, 0.919, and 0.771 on RF02540, RF02541, and RF02543, respectively. Performance on the first two families is nearly identical to within-dataset results, while the moderate drop of 5.3% on RF02543 is consistent with the greater structural heterogeneity of eukaryotic large-subunit rRNAs. These results indicate strong transferability from the BGSU5000 training distribution to another long-RNA dataset with similar biological function but different sources.

  Next, we investigate the model’s transferability across datasets with more pronounced differences in sequence length distributions and origins. UFold-X and UFold were trained separately on the BGSU5000 and Rfam5000 datasets, which include RNAs ranging from 600 to 5000 nt, and evaluated on the RCSB1800 and RCSB4000 test sets.

  As summarized in Supplementary Tables S18 and S19 and Supplementary Figs S11 and S12, UFold-X trained on Rfam5000 achieves the highest F1-score of 0.712 on RCSB4000, 2.4 times higher than the model trained on RNAstralign-1800 and 18.7% higher than UFold (0.600) trained on the same dataset. Similarly, UFold-X trained on BGSU5000 attains an F1 of 0.486 on RCSB1800, 1.6 times higher than its RNAstralign-trained counterpart and 19.4% higher than UFold (0.407). These results confirm that training on long-sequence datasets significantly enhances the model’s robustness for predicting unseen RNAs across different sources.


#### Cross-family benchmark based on Rfam family separation
  A critical yet often overlooked challenge in RNA secondary structure prediction is the ability of a model to transfer across RNA families that are not observed during training. Compared with conventional benchmark settings, cross-family evaluation provides a more stringent assessment of family-level transferability by enforcing separation at the Rfam family level.

  In this section, we evaluate the cross-family transferability of different RNA secondary structure prediction models under a cross-family setting. Specifically, the RNAstralign-1800 dataset is used as the training set, in which all RNA sequences originate from the RF00177 family in RFam. The test set is constructed from the bpRNA database and consists of 161 RNA sequences with lengths ranging from 600 to 1800 nt, drawn from four distinct RFam families (RF01959, RF01960, RF02542, and RF02545). The number of RNA sequences in each distinct RNA family is provided in Supplementary Table S20. Since the RNA sequences in the training and test sets belong to different RFam families, this experimental setup is regarded as a cross-family evaluation.

  The experimental results are summarized in Fig. 5A. As shown in the figure, UFold-X achieves the highest F1 score of 0.495 on the cross-family benchmark, demonstrating strong transferability across RNA families. It outperforms the second-best model, CONTRAfold (0.444), by a relative margin of 11.5%, and demonstrates an improvement of ~27.2% over UFold (0.389). Moreover, UFold-X attains the best performance on three out of the four RNA families. Traditional energy minimization-based methods exhibit relatively strong transferability in the cross-family scenario, with F1 scores ranging from 0.37 to 0.44. Among deep learning-based approaches, SPOT-RNA achieves an F1 score of 0.346 on this test set, whereas E2Efold performs poorly, yielding an F1 score of only 0.088. Detailed F1 scores for each RNA family are reported in Supplementary Table S21.

  These cross-family experimental results further demonstrate that UFold-X possesses stronger cross-family transferability compared with other deep learning-based methods.


### Cross-clan evaluation
  Previous studies have demonstrated that random or conventional sequence-level splits may not adequately control for RNA homology, because structurally related RNAs can retain highly conserved secondary structures despite limited sequence similarity. Separating training and test data according to structural or evolutionary relationships is therefore important for assessing the generalization of statistical and learning-based RNA secondary structure prediction methods [54, 55]. Motivated by these considerations, we established a cross-clan benchmark in which the test sequences belong to RNA clans not represented during model training.

  As discussed above, cross-clan evaluation serves as the primary benchmark for assessing model generalization in this study. While cross-family evaluation partially reduces homolog leakage, it remains insufficient for evaluating model behavior across more remote evolutionary relationships. In Rfam, RNA families are further organized into clans, which represent higher-order homology units containing structurally related but sequence-divergent RNA families. Consequently, clan-level separation provides a substantially more stringent assessment of homology-independent generalization.

  Despite its importance, cross-clan evaluation has rarely been explored in existing RNA secondary structure prediction studies, particularly for long RNAs. This limitation is largely due to the uneven distribution of annotated long RNAs across clans and the substantial heterogeneity in sequence length, structural organization, and base-pairing patterns among different clans. To address this gap, we systematically evaluate RNA secondary structure prediction models under a cross-clan setting for long RNAs, providing a more realistic assessment of model robustness when encountering structurally novel RNA classes.

  To construct the cross-clan benchmark, we collect all RNA sequences with lengths ranging from 600 to 1800 nt from the bpRNA database that are annotated in Rfam and categorize them according to official Rfam family and clan annotations. In total, 227 RNA sequences from 12 distinct RNA families spanning multiple clans are obtained. We then exclude RNA sequences from the RF00177 family (CL00111), which are used in the RNAstralign-1800 dataset, and retain 66 RNA sequences from 11 previously unseen RNA families as the test set (Supplementary Table S22).

  Due to the strict clan-level separation, RNA sequences from different clans often exhibit substantially distinct structural organizations and length distributions, making deep learning-based models trained on limited clans prone to severe performance degradation. To alleviate this issue while preserving strict cross-clan independence between training and test data, we adopt a controlled data augmentation strategy inspired by the original UFold framework. Specifically, we introduce random mutations to 5%–10% of the bases in the original RNA sequences to generate artificial sequences that remain structurally related yet sequence-distinct, resulting in 2640 augmented sequences. After removing redundant sequences with similarity greater than 0.9 using CD-HIT, a final training set of 2441 sequences spanning multiple RNA families and clans is obtained.

  We then apply CONTRAfold, trained on the bpRNA dataset, to predict the secondary structures of these augmented sequences and treat the predicted structures as reference labels. Although these labels are computationally inferred rather than experimentally validated, they are derived from a physics-based model and thus encode general thermodynamic and base-pairing principles, enabling effective training while avoiding information leakage. The augmented data are used as the training set, and the original 66 RNA sequences are retained as the test set for cross-clan evaluation. In addition, models that have not been trained on the bpRNA dataset are also included for comparison, consistent with the cross-clan nature of this evaluation.

  The evaluation results are shown in Fig. 5B. Under this stringent cross-clan evaluation setting, the performance of all methods decreases to varying degrees, highlighting the substantial challenge posed by clan-level distribution shifts. Traditional free-energy-based approaches generally achieve relatively strong performance in this scenario, consistent with their model-driven nature and reduced dependence on family-specific training distributions. Specifically, RNAstructure attains an F1 score of 0.393, while MFold achieves a comparable score of 0.390.

  UFold-X obtains an F1 score of 0.359, achieving performance comparable to several established classical approaches while remaining the strongest deep learning-based method under this setting. Compared with UFold (0.341), UFold-X exhibits a consistent improvement under clan-level separation. LinearFold achieves an F1 score of 0.358, which is also comparable to that of UFold-X. Detailed results for individual RNA families across different clans are reported in Supplementary Table S23.

  Overall, the performance of different methods varies across RNA families, and no single approach consistently outperforms others across all clans. These results suggest that cross-clan evaluation poses substantial challenges for RNA secondary structure prediction, particularly when substantial differences in structural organization, sequence length distribution, and biological characteristics exist between training and test data.

  Within this challenging setting, UFold-X demonstrates competitive and stable performance, indicating its robustness in cross-clan generalization scenarios. Compared with existing deep learning-based approaches, UFold-X achieves the strongest performance under this stringent evaluation setting while remaining comparable to several established classical methods. In addition to predictive performance, the computational efficiency and scalability of UFold-X are evaluated separately in the following inference-time evaluation section.

### Inference time
  Inference time is a crucial metric for evaluating model practicality, especially in long-sequence scenarios where computational complexity can cause certain models to scale poorly. Traditional free energy minimization algorithms typically rely on dynamic programming, which incurs high time complexity and slow inference speeds. Similarly, some deep learning-based approaches, such as SPOT-RNA, employ bidirectional LSTM architectures with extensive sequential dependencies and a large number of parameters, leading to limited computational efficiency.

  In contrast, UFold-X is built upon a U-Net backbone that offers inherent efficiency advantages. One branch of the architecture is composed of fully convolutional layers, allowing parallel computation and significantly reducing inference latency. The other branch utilizes a VSSM encoder based on Mamba, which replaces the standard Transformer attention mechanism. As a result, UFold-X effectively captures long-range dependencies while maintaining high computational efficiency.

### Web server
  To improve accessibility and usability for the research community, we have developed and deployed an upgraded web server for RNA secondary structure prediction based on the UFold-X model. Building upon our previous online platform, this new version incorporates the latest UFold-X architecture and provides a user-friendly interface that allows users to submit RNA sequences and obtain predicted secondary structures efficiently. Depending on the input sequence length, the web server automatically selects the most appropriate model (short-, medium-, or long-range) for prediction. A screenshot of the web interface is presented in Fig. 9. The server supports batch submission and long-sequence input, and is freely accessible at https://ufold-x.ai4bread.com.

  Compared to the earlier version, the updated server offers significantly improved capacity and flexibility. It supports the simultaneous submission of multiple RNA sequences and enables the prediction of much longer input sequences—features that were previously unsupported. In addition, the web-server workflow has been redesigned to support asynchronous prediction and persistent task tracking, allowing submitted jobs to run in the background rather than being tied to the active browser session. Users can optionally provide an email address when submitting tasks and later query recent jobs through the “My Tasks” page, which returns task-status information for jobs submitted within the past 3 days. Completed tasks can be opened to view details or download results.

  The revised server also includes stricter input validation and clearer user guidance. FASTA-format inputs are checked before prediction, and unsupported or malformed sequences are rejected with informative error messages. The server automatically determines the sequence length in nt and selects the appropriate prediction model internally, eliminating the need for manual length-range selection. A Help page and Quick Start Guide have also been added to clarify input requirements, supported nucleotides, output formats, and result retrieval.

  Our goal is to provide a practical, efficient, and publicly accessible tool for RNA structure prediction. By lowering the technical barrier to entry, we hope to empower more biologists and bioinformaticians to explore RNA structural characteristics without the need for complex installation or hardware resources. We believe that this enhanced web service will serve as a valuable resource for the broader RNA research community.


## Discussion
  RNA secondary structure plays a fundamental role in regulating gene expression, RNA stability, and intermolecular interactions. Accurate prediction of RNA secondary structures is thus critical for understanding RNA function and guiding downstream biological analyses. While traditional thermodynamic models and recent deep learning methods have made significant progress, major challenges remain—especially in handling long RNA sequences and modeling long-range base-pairing dependencies. As sequence length increases, structural complexity grows rapidly, and existing models often suffer from performance degradation or increased computational burden. Moreover, most current methods are designed and evaluated primarily on short RNAs, limiting their applicability to biologically important long RNAs, such as lncRNAs, rRNAs, and viral genomes.

  To address these challenges, we propose UFold-X, a deep learning framework specifically designed for long-range RNA secondary structure prediction. The core design of UFold-X is a dual-branch encoder–decoder architecture with a dynamic gated fusion mechanism, which enables simultaneous modeling of local base-pairing patterns and long-range structural dependencies. One branch employs fully convolutional layers to capture local interactions efficiently, while the other integrates a VSSM based on Mamba to model global dependencies across extended sequence contexts. By dynamically balancing the contributions of these two branches according to sequence characteristics, UFold-X effectively overcomes the locality limitations of convolutional models and the scalability issues of classical algorithms, providing a unified solution for RNAs of diverse lengths.

  Extensive experiments on multiple benchmark datasets demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of UFold-X. On long and ultra-long RNA benchmarks under within-family or cross-dataset evaluation settings, including RNAstralign-1800 and RCSB4000, UFold-X consistently outperforms both traditional thermodynamic approaches and existing deep learning models, with particularly strong performance in predicting long-range base pairs. On mixed-length datasets, UFold-X maintains stable performance across different sequence length ranges. Meanwhile, UFold-X achieves efficient inference, with an average inference time of only 0.08 s per sequence.

  Importantly, we further distinguish between conventional evaluation, cross-family transfer evaluation, and cross-clan generalization evaluation. In conventional within-family benchmarks, sequence-identity-based filtering is useful for reducing highly similar sequence redundancy, but it does not fully remove homologous relationships, especially for remote homologs with low sequence identity. Therefore, these benchmarks are mainly used to evaluate model performance under commonly adopted settings rather than to claim strict homology-independent generalization. Cross-family evaluation reflects the model’s transferability across different RNA families, while cross-clan evaluation provides a more stringent and homology-aware benchmark because the training and testing sets are separated at the Rfam clan level. Despite the pronounced structural and distributional shifts introduced by the cross-clan setting, UFold-X achieves competitive and stable performance, supporting its robustness under a stricter generalization scenario.

  Beyond predictive performance and generalization, we further validate the biological relevance of UFold-X. Through the proposed UFold-X-R framework, we extend the prediction objective to nucleotide reactivity signals derived from SHAPE and icSHAPE experiments, enabling direct inference of reactivity profiles from sequence and structural information. To quantitatively couple structural accuracy with biochemical realism, we introduce the HRPS, which integrates site-level reactivity correlation with multi-scale base-pairing accuracy. UFold-X achieves the highest HRPS among all compared models, demonstrating superior alignment between predicted secondary structures and experimentally observed biochemical profiles. These results confirm that UFold-X not only attains high structural accuracy, but also preserves meaningful biochemical interpretability, further supporting its applicability in biologically grounded RNA structure analysis.

  Despite its promising results, several limitations remain. First, like all deep learning methods, UFold-X relies on large-scale, high-quality annotated RNA structures for training. Although conventional benchmark settings are widely adopted in RNA secondary structure prediction, they may still contain homologous RNAs between training and test sets, potentially leading to optimistic estimates of model generalization. Sequence-identity-based filtering can reduce highly redundant sequences but cannot fully identify remote homologous relationships, since structurally or evolutionarily related RNAs may exhibit relatively low sequence similarity. Therefore, performance on conventional within-family and cross-dataset benchmarks should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. To address this issue, we introduced a cross-clan benchmark based on Rfam clan-level separation, which serves as a more rigorous assessment of homology-independent generalization. The current scarcity of experimentally validated structural data, particularly for rare or novel RNA types, may limit generalization. Second, deep learning models remain largely black-box in nature, lacking explicit biophysical interpretability—such as thermodynamic stability or free energy changes—that are valuable in biological modeling and design. Third, the added complexity introduced by the dual-branch architecture increases the number of trainable parameters, which may lead to higher memory and computation costs during inference.

  Future work could address these challenges in several ways. Integrating domain knowledge—such as energy-based constraints or known RNA motifs—into the learning process may improve interpretability and reduce training dependence. Moreover, the dynamic gating mechanism can be further refined by making the gating weights fully learnable and end-to-end trainable. Beyond RNA structure prediction, the UFold-X architecture holds promise for broader biomolecular modeling tasks, including protein structure prediction and RNA–protein interaction modeling. Finally, the growing availability of high-throughput structural profiling data (e.g. Cryo-EM [58], SHAPE-MaP [59]) and curated databases (e.g. Rfam [57], PDB [41], bpRNA [60]) is expected to improve model generalization across RNA families. As the field continues to advance, we believe that models like UFold-X—featuring hybrid architectures, efficient scalability, and the potential for biological integration—will play an increasingly important role in RNA structural biology and its applications in health and disease.
