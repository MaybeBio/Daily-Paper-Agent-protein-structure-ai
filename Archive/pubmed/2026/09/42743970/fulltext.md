# Structure-based virulence factor classification using a dual-driven graph transformer with a pretrained language model


## Abstract
  Bacterial pathogenicity relies on a multitude of virulence factors (VFs). Identifying these factors is crucial for comprehending the molecular mechanisms driving bacterial pathogenesis and pinpointing potential targets for antivirulence strategies. While the identification of VFs has received extensive attention, there is still a lack of prediction from the perspective of VF classes. Moreover, most computational methods focus solely on sequence information for virulence proteins, neglecting spatial structure information. Here, a novel Structure-based Dual-driven Graph Transformer framework named SDGT is proposed to identify multiple distinct VFs by leveraging graph data structures of virulence proteins and sequence representations generated from a pretrained protein language model. By representing virulence proteins at both the atom and residue levels, SDGT adaptively and comprehensively learns a graph representation of protein structure through the utilization of self-attention pooling. The results of extensive experiments demonstrate that SDGT significantly enhances the classification performance, achieving an accuracy of 0.7623 on an independent test dataset, outperforming other sequence-based and structure-based methods. Moreover, cluster analysis of pathogen VF features learned by SDGT proves the excellent specificity and similarity feature learning power of the proposed model. The current analysis suggests that SDGT is an effective tool for identifying of VF classes.


## Introduction
  Pathogenic bacteria are responsible for a range of diseases and pose a significant threat to human health, primarily through the action of multiple virulence factors (VFs). VFs are a group of effector molecules that aid pathogenic bacteria in initiating infection, harming the host, and disrupting host immunity [1]. The VFs of bacterial pathogens are associated with diverse functional categories, such as exotoxin, adherence, and immune modulation [2, 3]. Various VFs collaborate to facilitate pathogenic bacteria adhering to, replicating, and inflicting damage upon host cells [4]. Recently, multiple kinds of VFs, such as biofilm and quorum sensing system, have emerged as potential targets for antivirulence strategies [5–8]. Therefore, VF classification research serves the dual purpose of understanding the distinct molecular mechanisms underlying bacterial pathogenesis and identifying specific candidate targets for the treatment of bacterial infectious diseases.

  Currently, the available bioinformatic tools for VF identification can be divided into two groups: comparative genomic-based approaches and machine learning-based models [9]. Comparative genomic approaches involve the comparison of the genome sequence data of a predicted bacterial strain with a known VF database using sequence alignment tools to assign the VF class [10, 11]. For instance, Li et al. [12] released VRprofile, a tool that predicts both antimicrobial resistance genes and virulence proteins through homologous gene cluster searches. Later, by conducting iterative homology searching in the virulence factor database, Liu et al. [13] proposed a comparative pathogenomic tool known as the VFanalyzer to detect potential virulence genes. However, comparative genomic-based strategies can extract only conserved virulence genes, preventing the discovery of new VF-related genes whose evolutionary divergence has occurred from known virulence gene data.

  However, machine learning-based algorithms have increasingly been utilized to discover novel virulence genes. Machine learning-based approaches primarily leverage sequence data for VF predictions. Specifically, they concentrate on extracting statistical patterns and feeding these features into machine learning models. For example, random forest (RF) and support vector machine (SVM) have been extensively utilized for the prediction of VFs [14–16]. Garg and Gupta [16] incorporated protein sequence features, including evolutionary information and frequency components, into a bilayer cascade SVM to excavate VFs. They further proposed an enhanced version based on the most recent datasets of VFs, leading to a noteworthy improvement in prediction accuracy [17]. Recently, de Nies et al. [18] introduced PathoFact, a predictive tool capable of simultaneously identifying toxin genes and VFs by integrating the RF model with a hidden Markov model database for virulence. While machine learning-based approaches exhibit strong generalizability and sequence sensitivity, their reliance on manually crafted feature engineering may constrain their ability to represent statistical sequence patterns. Deep learning techniques offer the potential to address the aforementioned limitations by automatically learning the statistical patterns within provided data. Given their successful application across diverse research domains [19, 20], several deep learning approaches have been advanced for VF prediction. For example, DeepVF [21] is an ensemble learning framework that stacks multiple machine learning and deep learning models for VF identification. Recently, PreVFs-RG [22] employs a residual neural network and a recurrent neural network to extract the sequence features of virulence proteins. Following the idea of DeepVF, Singh et al. [23] constructed an ensemble learning model and innovatively introduced the Seq-Alignment feature to improve the prediction accuracy. For VF classification, Zheng et al. [24] pioneered the construction of a VF dataset, encompassing 160 495 sequences of virulence proteins with 3446 classes. Utilizing this dataset, the authors developed a neural network architecture consisting of two convolutional layers and two pooling layers to capture the statistical representations of VFs. Although the aforementioned models have demonstrated satisfactory performance in predicting or classifying VFs, they neglect structural information. These models solely employ one-dimensional sequences to depict input virulence proteins, potentially overlooking crucial spatial details essential for characterizing the functional properties of these proteins.

  Recently, graph neural networks (GNNs) [25], which are proficient in extracting structural features, have gained widespread application across diverse biomedical domains [26–31]. For instance, DeepFRI [32] represents proteins as contact maps based on the distance between residue pairs, subsequently adopting three graph convolutional layers to make predictions of protein functions. Following the idea of DeepFRI, Lai and Xu [33] introduced a graph-based approach referred to as GAT-GO, which involves the conversion of protein sequences into their respective contact maps through the utilization of the protein structure prediction tool RaptorX [34]. By introducing sequence language representation and structural information, HEAL [35] leverage a graph transformer to adaptively learn node embeddings and adopts a self-attention layer to capture graph-level features. The presented results illustrate the advantageous impact of representing proteins as contact maps derived from structural information on enhancing the model performance.

  With the development of large language model techniques, multiple protein structure prediction tools with high-resolution have been developed [36–39]. These structure prediction tools facilitate the comprehensive classification and mining of proteins with specific biological properties on a large scale [40–45]. In addition, Liu et al. [46] recently reconstructed a comprehensive classification of virulence proteins. In this taxonomy, all 32 general types of bacterial pathogens were categorized into 14 basal categories. This newly established dataset will serve as a crucial benchmark for future research in the classification of VFs.

  Here, using structural information, we develop a dual-driven graph transformer model, named Structure-based Dual-driven Graph Transformer (SDGT), which identifies multiple distinct VFs. First, we extract the initial sequence representations and predict the three-dimensional (3D) structure of virulence proteins by leveraging a pretrained protein language model. Second, we design a dual-driven graph neural network that consists of both residue-level and atom-level graph transformers to effectively extract and aggregate protein representations, thereby facilitating feature learning across multiple scales. Finally, we utilize a self-attention pooling module along with a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to predict all seven major types of VFs.

### An overview of SDGT
  The overall workflow of SDGT is displayed in Fig. 1 and consists of three main parts: (i) modeling the graph data structure of virulence proteins and extracting the initial feature representations based on a pretrained protein language model, (ii) extracting and aggregating protein representations based on a dual-driven graph transformer at both the atom and residue levels, and (iii) predicting VF classes based on self-attention pooling.   We chose ESM-2 [36] as the extractor for the initial HRes feature of the residue. ESM-2 is a pretrained protein language model through which we initialized the features of the residue. For the atomic feature HAtom, we combined the amino acid classes of atom, the type of atom, the relative distance to the nearest neighbor, and the direction angle of the atom as shown in Table 1. Both homogeneity and heterogeneity are preserved between atoms. We applied the dihedral angle of Cα in the residue (N-Cα-C) as the spatial position encoding the SRes of the residue, and the Cartesian coordinates of the original as the position encoding the SAtom of the atom. Position encoding enhances the structural nature of residue-level and atom-level embeddings.

### Effects of parameters
  To optimize the performance of SDGT, we fine-tuned the hyperparameters of the model. As shown in Fig. 2a, we performed detailed experiments on the mask rate M for self-attention pooling. The box plot represents the effect on the validation set, and the line plot represents the effect on the independent test set. When M = 0.7, the ACC of the model reaches a maximum value of 0.7623, and Pre reaches a maximum value of 0.7994. The GCN module is also an essential component of SDGT, and to optimize the model, in this section, we analyzed different GCN layers in the GCN module. We performed experiments on GCNs with 1 to 4 layers, and the experimental results are shown in Fig. 2b and c, and further discussion and analysis is in Note 2. We also evaluated the atomic neighborhood size using k = 5, 10, 15, and 20. The k = 10 setting provided the most favorable balance between predictive performance and computational efficiency and was therefore retained (Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Table S2, and Supplementary Fig. S2). In addition, the main hyperparameters of SDGT were set as follows: learning rate lr = 0.0001, random seed = 42, number of residue-level graph transformer layers N = 3, number of atom-level graph transformer layers K = 3, number of multihead attention heads h1 = 8 and h2 = 2.


### Evaluation of performance on the multi-classification task for VFs
  We segmented the dataset and used the training and validation sets to find the optimal learnable parameters for the model. The performance of the model was validated on an independent test set. The experimental results of our proposed method for multi-classification [56] of VFs are shown in Fig. 2d, where the Acc of our model reaches 76.23% and the Pre reaches 79.94%; the F1 score is equal to 73.67% for VF classification on the independent test set. The Acc on the validation set reached 80%, the Pre reaches 83.33%, and the F1 score reaches 78.5%. To intuitively observe the sensitivity of our model for each class of VFs, we displayed the classification effect of SDGT for each class of VFs (Fig. 3a). The SDGT classification model demonstrates elevated sensitivity to VFs associated with the effector delivery system and immune modulation, surpassing its performance in other classes on the independent test and validation sets. Specifically, a Pre of 91% and an F1 score of 87% are achieved for the effector delivery system on the independent test set. Additionally, the recall for immune modulation attains an impressive 97%. The confusion matrix of the SDGT classification results is shown in Fig. 3b, where darker colors on the main diagonal represent more classes that were predicted correctly. The accuracy of our predictions is remarkable for the following classes: adherence, effector delivery system, motility, and immune modulation on the independent test and validation sets. ### Cross-system evaluation of model on effector delivery system recognition
  Effector delivery systems typically involve multiple secretion systems that translocate effector proteins into host cells. The primary categories of these effector secretion systems include the types II–VII secretion systems, each with its distinct structure and function, finely adapted to different environments and mechanisms of host interaction. It is crucial to ensure that our model accurately recognizes all these types of secretion systems as effector delivery systems during the classification of VFs. In our filtered dataset, we specifically included types T2SS, T3SS, T4SS, T6SS, and T7SS, with T3SS representing the largest portion. Initially, while keeping other VF classes unchanged, we constructed the training and validation sets by including only T2SS, T4SS, T6SS, T7SS, while reserving only T3SS for the test set. We also reversed the scenario by using T3SS as both the training and validation set while including T2SS, T4SS, T6SS, and T7SS exclusively in the test set as shown in Fig. 4. Due to space limitations, we analyze and discuss the results of the cross-system evaluation experiments of the model in Note 5 in the Supplementary Information.

  On the test set, it is revealed that the performance of the variant model SDGT-w/o atom-D is the lowest. The Acc of the SDGT-w/o atom-D variant is 0.0493 lower than that of SDGT, which reveals that the dual-driven nature of our model is essential in the process of feature learning for the VFs and that approaching this phenomenon from the atom and residue levels together will yield superior results. The performance of the SDGT-w/o position variant is 0.0203 less accurate than that of the SDGT, and the remaining three metrics are also lower than SDGT. The variant model that performed second only to SDGT is SDGT-replace GAP, with an Acc of 0.0122 less than that of SDGT. Furthermore, we analyzed the performance distribution of the SDGT and variant models on the validation cohort. As shown in Fig. 3d, the distributions of Acc, F1 score, and recall of SDGT on the validation set are better than those of the other variant models. Overall, the above results show that both the position coding and self-attention module used in our model improve the performance for the VF multi-classification task. Computational profiling showed that complete SDGT required 78.76 min for 50-epoch training, 5.98 GiB peak training memory, and 18.54 ms graphics processing unit (GPU) inference time per protein (Supplementary Note 6 and Supplementary Table S3).


### Comparison with other methods on the multi-classification task for VFs
  To demonstrate the superiority of SDGT, we compared it to several benchmark methods. The following are the graph structure-based methods used: GCN [48], graph attention network (GAT) [57], GCN-Residual [48, 50], and GAT-Residual [50, 57], and the sequence-based method used: convolutional neural network (CNN) [58]. The model structure and parameters for comparison with SDGT are also described in detail in Table S4 of the Supplementary Information. The performance comparison on the independent test set is presented in Table 3. It is evident from the results that SDGT outperforms the other baseline methods in terms of the Acc, Pre, recall, and F1 score. Compared to those of the GCN, the Acc and Pre of SDGT improved by 1.45% and 2.23%, respectively. Moreover, SDGT surpasses GAT with notable margins, showcasing 11.6% improvement in Acc and a 9.64% enhancement in Pre. The primary factor contributing to the enhanced performance of SDGT is the integration of the GCN and transformer modules, enabling the simultaneous capture of both local and global structural information. To achieve more efficient multi-classification of VFs, it is essential to capture information from different perspectives across various classes and identify their unique characteristics. Here, SDGT uses dual-driven residue and atom graphs, so it can capture global residue information and local atomic features effectively improving the classification of VFs. To provide a more comprehensive and intuitive assessment of SDGT’s performance, we conducted a t-test to evaluate the differences between SDGT and several benchmark models, as shown in Fig. 5a. We also analyzed the numbers of samples correctly and incorrectly predicted by each model (Fig. 5b) and visualized the classification results of SDGT and its comparison models using t-SNE [59] (Fig. 5c). To assess the statistical reliability of the performance improvements, we performed paired bootstrap analysis between SDGT and each baseline method using the validation and independent test predictions. For each metric, the performance difference was calculated as SDGT minus the corresponding baseline. As shown in Fig. 5d, SDGT showed consistently positive performance differences over all baseline methods across Accuracy, Weighted Precision, Weighted F1, and Recall. A supplementary robustness analysis further supported the stable performance advantage of SDGT across the evaluated metrics (Fig. S3). A detailed analysis of the statistical comparisons in Fig. 4 and the robustness analysis in Fig. S3 is provided in Note 7 of the Supplementary Information.


### Comparison of SDGT with state-of-the-art VFs identification methods
  For de novo prediction of VFs, we applied SDGT for binary classification of VFs. First, we selected 3451 VFs from the multi-classification task as positive samples and collected an equal number of negative samples (VFDS1), resulting in a balanced dataset for VF identification. The dataset was then split into training, validation, and test sets in a ratio of 8:1:1. SDGT achieved an accuracy of 87.25% on the independent test set of VFDS1 (Fig. 6a). The values of AUC and AUPR reached 0.9375 and 0.9496, respectively. Meanwhile, the consistently excellent performance across multiple experiments on the validation set demonstrates the high robustness of the model (Fig. 6b).

  Additionally, we selected five state-of-the-art VF identification tools for comparison: PBVF [14], MP3 [15], VirulentPred [16], DeepVF [21], and BLAST [60]. To ensure a fair comparison, we constructed an independent test set, consisting of 576 VFs and 576 non-VFs (VFDS2), following the methodology used by DeepVF [21]. SDGT achieved an accuracy of 81.9% on the task of VF identification, surpassing other state-of-the-art models. The F1 score of SDGT was consistent with DeepVF, with both reaching 80.7%. However, SDGT demonstrated a much higher sensitivity of 81.2%, outperforming other tools, including DeepVF (Table 4). These results indicate that SDGT significantly improves the accuracy of VF identification. To clearly observe the learning process of SDGT, we visualized the residue and atom features of the initial samples using t-SNE [59] (Fig. 6c). The visualization results showed that distinguishing VFs from non-VFs at this stage was challenging. We then visualized the residue and atom features of the samples after being processed by the graph transformer separately, but it remained difficult to differentiate between positive and negative samples. In contrast, the fusion features learned by SDGT clearly distinguished VFs and non-VFs, demonstrating that SDGT can efficiently identify VFs at both the residue and atom levels.


### Interpretability of features
  The VFs of bacteria commonly exhibit a pronounced level of species specificity. For example, the prevalent VFs of Acinetobacter baumannii include outer membrane proteins and capsular polysaccharides. Here, we further investigated the abundance of 32 genera in the 7 VF classes according to our collected dataset. As shown in Fig. 7a, most of the adherence bacteria were Escherichia, Aeromonas, and Vibrio, while most of the effector delivery system are found in Legionella, Coxiella, and Chlamydia. On the independent test set, we also observed that the same class of VFs within a particular bacterial genus tended to cluster into a subgroup based on the feature space learned by SDGT. For instance, the genus Chlamydia, characterized by a gram-negative cell wall without peptidoglycan [61], constitutes a subgroup within the effector delivery system class, as shown in Fig. 7b. Moreover, the genus Legionella is a gram-negative bacillus with no-spore-forming ability [62] that forms a subgroup within the motility class, as shown in Fig. 7b. From the aforementioned findings, we may infer that the interpretability of the features learned via SDGT can enhance the classification performance.

  Next, we analyzed the features by clustering features using the complete linkage method and the Bray–Curtis distance metric; the results of the feature clustering are shown in Fig. 8a. In cluster I, 92.7% of the virulence proteins were present in the effector delivery system. In cluster II, 78.3% of the virulence proteins were present in motility, and in cluster VI, 86.5% of the virulence proteins were present in immune modulation. As shown in Table S5, we compared the number of VFs in each of the original classes and clusters. The results showed that feature clustering with an adequate sample class was significantly superior to clustering with a small number of samples. To further analyze the feature clustering results and the effect of unbalanced data on the clustering results, we performed the same experiment on the validation set as shown in Fig. 8b. We found that 95.5% of the virulence proteins exhibited motility in cluster VI, and 92.4% of the virulence proteins were present in the effector delivery system in cluster VII. Moreover, the data sample size directly impacted the validity of the clustering shown in Table S6. Taken together, we can conclude that SDGT possesses excellent specificity for feature learning for different classes of VFs, which enhances feature interpretability and facilitates the discovery and classification of VFs. In addition, the experiments also show that the data imbalance problem has a substantial effect on the capability of the features learned by the model.


## Discussion
  In this study, we proposed a novel structure-based dual-driven graph transformer method named SDGT for class identification of VFs. To efficiently learn specificity features across different classes of VFs, we initially leveraged pretrained model to predict the 3D structure of VFs and extracted initial residue features from sequences. Subsequently, we constructed atom and residue maps based on the 3D structures of VFs, incorporating positional encodings for atoms and residues. We then employed graph convolutional networks and self-attention mechanisms to uncover both local and global features of residues and atoms, extracting integrated information at the atom-level and residue-level. Finally, the self-attention pooling mechanism was utilized to adaptively fuse atom and residue features, enhancing the generalizability and performance of the model. We validated the outstanding performance and robustness of our model using independent test and validation sets. A comparison with other strategies highlighted the superior performance and generalizability of our model. Cluster analysis utilizing feature information learned by SDGT, further demonstrated its ability to extract specificity and similarity features for heterogeneous and homogeneous classes. These studies collectively indicated that the SDGT will be an efficient and effective tool in future classification tasks of VFs.

  Recent advances in protein structure prediction provide several opportunities for extending the SDGT pipeline. AlphaFold 3 can predict joint structures containing proteins, nucleic acids, ions, and small-molecule ligands, and may therefore provide interaction-aware structural information for virulence factors that function through molecular interactions [63]. ESMFold2 provides an updated language-model-based approach to all-atom structure prediction and could serve as an alternative to the current ESMFold coordinate-generation stage [64]. DeepAssembly models inter-domain interactions and may be useful for improving the structural representation of multi-domain virulence factors, particularly when the relative orientations of their domains are uncertain [65]. These methods would complement SDGT at the upstream structure-generation stage. Because the downstream graph-learning component uses predicted atomic coordinates to construct residue-level and atom-level graphs, alternative structure predictors can be incorporated while retaining the residue–atom dual-level architecture.

  However, the model still suffers from the following limitations. In the dual-driver graph transformer, we conducted atom-level and residue-level feature learning separately, without interactive learning. The features of atoms and residues can be learned by transforming each other during feature extraction, potentially resulting in more accurate feature information. In addition, the challenge of imbalanced data remains unresolved. In future research, we will conduct more in-depth and specific studies on VFs to categorize them in finer detail, focusing on particular types of VFs. For instance, we plan to conduct further classifications of secreted proteins from gram-negative bacteria and delve into subgroups such as the effector delivery system. These tasks aim to reveal further levels of biological relevance and information, providing valuable insights into the integration of computational methods and biological experiments.
