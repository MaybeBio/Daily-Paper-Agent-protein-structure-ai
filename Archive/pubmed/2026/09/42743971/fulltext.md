# HighFold4: extending AlphaFold3 to accurate cyclic peptide conformation prediction via custom chemical connectivity


## Abstract
  While AlphaFold3 has revolutionized protein structure prediction and supports noncanonical amino acids, its architecture always fails to reliably generate the closed-ring topologies characteristic of cyclic peptides. Existing adaptations, such as imposing distance constraints via an offset matrix, enforce ring geometry but cannot specify the chemical identity of the cyclization bond, leading to a restrictive bias toward amide- or disulfide-linked macrocycles. Here, we present HighFold4, a framework that adapts AlphaFold3 to explicitly incorporate user-defined chemical connectivity between residues, thereby enabling both topological closure and bond-specific cyclization. Without retraining the base model, HighFold4 achieves accurate, chemistry-aware prediction of diverse cyclic peptide conformations, as validated on 179 structures. This work established a new paradigm for the conformation construction of macrocyclic peptides with tailored ring geometry and linkage chemistry, significantly expanding the utility of deep learning in peptide-based drug discovery.


## Introduction
  The release of AlphaFold [1] has garnered widespread interest across the scientific community and significantly accelerate the development of deep learning-based protein modeling and design [2–10]. Building upon this foundation, the recent release of AlphaFold3 [11] marks another significant leap forward, through its extended capability to incorporate noncanonical amino acids into structure prediction. Noncanonical amino acids, which occur naturally but are not encoded in standard genetic translation, exhibit unique chemical and structural properties that have made them valuable in the design of functional biomaterials and pharmaceutical agents [12–15]. Advances in synthetic and chemical biology now enable the site-specific incorporation of diverse noncanonical residues into peptides, proteins, and enzymes, enhancing their stability, activity, and functional versatility [16–18]. By integrating a conformation-generation module that accommodates non-protein molecular components, AlphaFold3 marks an important step toward generalized biomolecular structure prediction. This expansion has stimulated considerable follow-up work and has catalyzed progress in adjacent fields [19–25].

  Nevertheless, while AlphaFold3 shows promise in modeling certain noncanonical peptides, its performance in predicting the structures of cyclic peptides, a therapeutically important class of molecules characterized by a macrocyclic ring that guarantees structure stability and often enhances target binding [26–30], remains biologically implausible in several cases. This shortcoming arises because the model’s conformation generation is heavily reliant on patterns present in its training data, rather than on explicit enforcement of the topological constraints inherent to cyclization [31]. Even when provided with cyclic peptide sequences and explicit connectivity information, the model often outputs linear conformations that lack ring closure. Such limitations underscore the need for caution when applying current versions of AlphaFold3 to real-world cyclic peptide design and analysis.

  To address this limitation, we introduced an offset matrix, a method widely used in AI-based structure prediction and generation models [32–34] such as AlphaFold [1] and RFdiffusion [35] to enforce cyclic constraints, into the AlphaFold3 framework, resulting in our proposed model called HighFold3 [36]. While HighFold3 generally performs well in benchmark tests, it introduced a new limitation. This model developed a strong bias toward amide- or disulfide-bridged macrocycles, as the distance constraint of offset matrix does not specify the chemical nature of the cyclizationbond.

  Consequently, HighFold3 may struggles to generate cyclic peptides linked by other bond types, such as side-chain-mediated bridges [37, 38], which limits its applicability for designing diverse cyclic peptide therapeutics. Therefore, developing a method that can simultaneously define the topological structure of the ring and the specific chemical connection mode remains a new problem.

  In this study, we aimed to develop an effective strategy for enhancing the predictive accuracy of AlphaFold for diverse cyclic peptide conformations. To this end, we adapted AlphaFold3 to explicitly define the atomic pairs involved in cyclization between specific amino acid residues, thereby enabling accurate prediction of cyclic peptide structures without the need for computationally intensive retraining or fine-tuning. This method, which we named HighFold4, allows for the prediction of cyclic peptides with user-defined connectivity, opening a path toward the tailored computational design of macrocyclic therapeutics with controlled ring geometry and bond type (see Fig. 1). Importantly, our framework is not intended to replace the established offset-based approach. Instead, HighFold4 integrates it as a foundational option, while offering our new method as an advanced alternative. This design allows users to select the most suitable strategy based on their specific cyclization scenario, thereby broadening the toolkit rather than narrowingit.


## Methods and materials

### Dataset
  The structural dataset used to validate the reliability of our work is divided into two subsets: set_canonical and set_noncanonical.

  Set_canonical includes cyclic monomers and complexes composed exclusively of canonical residues. Within this dataset, the cyclic peptide monomers were obtained from Bhardwaj et al. [34], whereas the cyclic peptide complexes were compiled from the ADCP study [39]. Additionally, we expanded the complex dataset by querying the Protein Data Bank (PDB) [40, 41] using the following specific criteria: Biologically Interesting Molecules Reference Dictionary (BIRD) attributes, a cyclic peptide classification, and a refinement resolution threshold of Set_canonical < 2.5 Å. The “Find Similar Assemblies” functionality was additionally leveraged to retrieve further homologous structures. Following the manual curation process and the deduplication of sequences, this subset contains 62 monomers and 32 complexes, with cyclic peptide lengths ranging from 6 to 39 residues.

  Set_noncanonical consists of cyclic monomers and complexes that incorporate both canonical and noncanonical residues. The original dataset was compiled from HighFold3 [36] and the work of Baker et al. [42]. Furthermore, we conducted a similar manual search for noncanonical cyclic peptides using the same methodology employed for the canonical dataset. Following a thorough validation and deduplication process, this dataset comprises 68 monomers and 17 complexes, with peptide lengths varying from 5 to 43 residues.

  Both subsets come from PDB [40, 41] and contain main-chain cyclization and cover a wide range of secondary structure types, including α-helices, β-sheets, and mixed conformations, ensuring a comprehensive assessment of structural diversity. The detailed information about dataset can be founded in Tables S1–S4.


### Method
  In the default AlphaFold3 architecture, the relative position encoding matrix within the feature engineering module is designed to capture the topological distance between any pair of residues [11]. This distance is originally defined as the minimum number of edges (bonds) separating two amino acids along the peptide backbone. Because AlphaFold3 implicitly assumes a standard linear topology, the default position offset between two residues is determined solely by the absolute difference of their sequence indices. However, this linear assumption fails for cyclic peptides, where head-to-tail cyclization [43] and potential disulfide bonds [44] fundamentally alter the underlying graph topology. For instance, in a cyclic structure, the terminal residues are directly bonded in 3D space, yet the linear matrix misinterprets them as being at maximum separation (Fig. 2A). This discrepancy in the position offset matrix introduces structural priors that contradict the physical reality of cyclic molecules, thereby limiting the model’s accuracy in predicting cross-linked macromolecular structures.

  To rectify this representation bias, HighFold3 introduces the Floyd for Cyclic Peptides (FCP) algorithm, a dynamic programming method adapted from the Floyd-Warshall framework [45]. This algorithm is employed to construct a customized Cyclic Peptide Position Offset Matrix (CycPOEM) that accurately reflects the non-linear topology. For a cyclic peptide sequence containing N amino acid residues, the construction of CycPOEM proceeds as follows: Graph Initialization: An adjacency matrix is first initialized based on the explicit topological connections of the cyclic peptide. Edge weights between adjacent residues sharing natural peptide bonds, the head-to-tail cyclization linkage, and any specified disulfide bonds are initialized to 1, while all non-bonded residue pairs are assigned an infinite distance. Dynamic Programming Update: The FCP algorithm executes three nested loops to iteratively traverse all residues. In this framework, the outer loop selects an intermediate residue, while the middle and inner loops iterate through the source and target residues, respectively. If passing through the intermediate residue yields a shorter topological path than the currently recorded value, the position offset for that residue pair is recursively updated. By replacing the static linear encoding with the dynamically computed CycPOEM, the model inherently perceives the topological collapse caused by cyclization and cross-linking at the input level. This provides accurate spatial constraint features for the subsequent geometric attention layers without requiring structural modifications to the core network blocks, as shown in the Fig. 2B.

  However, it is worth noting that HighFold3 operates primarily on residue-level position and distance matrices, lacking the granular capacity to explicitly define atom-type chemical bonds. Consequently, as a data-driven model bound by the composition of existing training datasets, HighFold3 inherently defaults to standard disulfide bracing or head-to-tail backbone amide cyclization. In contrast, practical medicinal chemistry campaigns frequently explore diverse, noncanonical cyclization linkages to expand chemical space and trap bioactive conformations. The inability of HighFold3 to generalize to these diverse bonding types limits its utility in real-world drug discovery pipelines.

  To address this problem, we have introduced definitions for covalent bonds between amino acids within polymers in HighFold4. While the original fold_input function in AlphaFold3 allowed for the input of intra-polymer covalent bonds, this feature was not functionally implemented in the model or during feature processing (Fig. 2C). Accordingly, we have refined the relevant modules in HighFold4. To enable the modeling of different topologies, we re-engineered the covalent connectivity pipelines within AlphaFold3, extending its small-molecule covalent feature construction protocols to accommodate polymer-polymer covalent linkages. Specifically, this connectivity is explicitly parameterized as an adjacency matrix G with a shape of N_token × N_token, where each row and column indexes an individual token, and the matrix dimension is strictly determined by the total token count N_token derived from sequence tokenization. The structural topology within G represents an undirected and symmetric connection relationship; for instance, if token 1 and token 10 possess a covalent linkage, the corresponding elements are flagged such that both G (1,10) and G (10,1) are set to 1, while all non-bonded pairs remain 0. To minimize computational and memory overhead during the feature generation phase, matrix G is recorded via a sparse data format. Subsequently, within the computational pipeline of the AlphaFold3 Evoformer module, this sparse matrix is inflated back to a dense state, merged with other ligand-associated connectivity relationships, and ultimately encoded into the pair_feat through a dedicated bond_embedding module.

  The complete algorithmic workflow of our proposed framework is detailed in Algorithm S1. By incorporating amino acid covalent bond representations during the data feature processing stage, our approach can accurately capture various cyclization patterns, including head-to-tail amide bond formation, disulfide bond formation, and other side-chain cyclization types.

  Subsequently, within the evoformer module, we perform feature crossover between the intra-polymer amino acid covalent bonds and the covalent bonds associated with small molecules, followed by linear encoding to enable Highfold4 to effectively incorporate these feature representations. This process is illustrated in Algorithm S2.

  Here, gather_idx represents the covalent bonding relationships between ligands and polymers, expressed in the sparse form of an adjacency matrix. In this matrix, rows and columns correspond to token positions, and the entries in gather_idx indicate the indices of tokens involved in covalent bonds. We constructed an adjacency matrix for the covalent bonds between amino acids within the polymer in the same format and performed feature concatenation accordingly.

  To illustrate how different computational mechanisms affect macrocyclic conformation, we evaluated the cyclic peptide GGGGG (MEG) as a methodological baseline (Fig. 2), where the prediction involves a head-to-side-chain lactam linkage between the G_1 N-terminus and the side-chain carboxyl of MEG_6. Under this specific benchmarking condition, AlphaFold3 entirely lacks the representation for macrocyclic topology, thereby treating the sequence under standard linear constraints and inevitably predicting the structure into an uncyclized, linear conformation where the reactive groups remain fully disconnected. In contrast, HighFold3 introduces a spatial distance matrix to enforce ring closure; however, because this mechanism relies purely on minimizing geometric distances rather than defining explicit chemical bonds, the model simply forces the two ends into proximity, which leads to an incorrect head-to-tail backbone connection involving the backbone carbon of MEG and fails to achieve the user-specified linkage customization. HighFold4 successfully addresses this spatial ambiguity by directly embedding explicit bond-type descriptions into its structural prediction architecture. As demonstrated by accurately orienting the macrocyclic ring through the side-chain carboxyl of MEG, HighFold4 demonstrate the capability to predict noncanonical cyclization strategies beyond standard disulfide or conventional head-to-tail linkages.

## Results

### The predictive performance of HighFold4
  First, we evaluated models’ performance on the canonical cyclic peptide dataset using multiple RMSD-based metrics (Tables S5–S13). For monomer predictions, the HighFold4 model exhibited strong performance across all structural levels. The average RMSD values for Cα atoms (RMSD_Cα), backbone atoms (RMSD_bb), and all atoms (RMSD_aa) were 1.428 Å, 1.343 Å, and 2.436 Å, respectively. Notably, the RMSD_Cα values ranged from 0.412 Å to 4.661 Å, underscoring the model’s high accuracy in capturing Cα atom positions (Fig. 3A).

  Furthermore, 29 predicted conformations achieved an RMSD below 1.0 Å relative to the native structure, indicating atom-level accuracy. For cyclic peptide-protein complexes, we first evaluated global accuracy using the Fnat metric, nearly 40% of the predicted samples attained an Fnat value > 0.8. In particular, for the complex 3ava, the predicted structure reached an Fnat of 1.0, demonstrating high consistency with the experimentally determined conformation.

  Regarding the ligand components within these complexes, several predicted structures achieved an RMSD_Cα of ~0.5 Å (Fig. 3B), with RMSD_bb and RMSD_aa also reaching comparable values. However, we observed that several samples exhibited exceptionally high RMSD values. For instance, in the case of 3avj, the RMSD_Cα of the binder reached 32.569 Å. In this case of 3avj, the target protein inherently features two alternative peptide-binding sites. Our automated batch RMSD calculate pipeline, by default, aligns the prediction exclusively with Site 1 as the reference ground truth. HighFold4, guided by the structural topography of the protein, predicted the complex assembly at Site 2. Crucially, because Site 1 and Site 2 are highly homologous and structurally symmetrical, the peptide adopts almost the same conformation in both pockets. When re-evaluating the prediction against the actual structural coordinates of Site 2, the RMSD_Cα dropped significantly to 0.196 Å, demonstrating that the model accurately identified a physically valid binding mode. However, this is a special case where the binding sites for the cyclic peptides are highly similar.

  When considering the influence of the protein on its surface, we directly quantified the structural divergence of the macrocycles by evaluating the RMSD_Cα value between their holo (bound) and apo (unobond) forms. The detailed information is provided in Table S13. In fact, the spatial conformation of a cyclic peptide is intrinsically affected by the topology of its protein target, leading to structural difference between its holo and unbound apo states. This conformational difference typically stems from multifactorial drivers; owing to their inherent backbone flexibility, many cyclic peptides change their spatial topologies based on target surface. Under these circumstances, specific intermolecular forces at the protein interface, such as hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, or hydrophobic effect, may change the conformation of corresponding atoms or side chains of the cyclic peptide binders. Taking Fig. 4A as an example, the monomeric cyclic peptide naturally populates a highly flexible loop ensemble in its apo form; however, upon interacting with the target, the loop structure undergoes distinct backbone twisting and side-chain reorientation, driving a hydrophobic moieties such as the phenyl group to close to the hydrophobic surface of the protein to maximize favorable hydrophobic interactions. Similar conformational transitions can also be observed between the holo and apo forms within our original data from Highplay [54]. We predicted the holo and apo structures of the active cyclic peptide sequences, and the average RMSD_Cα between the two states was 4.091 Å. (see Table S14).

  Conversely, if a cyclic peptide inherently possesses a rigidified structure, it can effectively resist severe structural change during binding, maintaining conformational stability without undergoing obvious changes. When exposed to the interfacial influences from the protein surface, such pre-organized macrocycles exhibit minimal structural deviation. As illustrated in Fig. 4B, the apo conformation of this specific cyclic peptide obtains a well-defined alpha-helix secondary structure. When interacting with the hydrophobic region of the target, this pre-existing alpha helix already provides an optimal stereochemical match for the binding pocket. Consequently, such structurally pre-organized peptides do not require extensive conformational remodeling to achieve binding. In addition, an interesting observation is that the model exhibited better performance in complexed structures compared to monomeric predictions. This improvement may be attributed to additional structural constraints imposed by the protein binding partner.

  In addition to canonical residues, we also evaluated the performance of our model on noncanonical cyclic peptides. A major strength of AlphaFold3 lies in its ability to model structures containing unnatural residues and small molecules. Therefore, in this experiment, we not only analyzed the overall structural deviations assessed by all-atom, Cα-atom, and backbone RMSD between predicted and experimentally observed structures, but also specifically investigated the predictive accuracy for unnatural residues. To quantify the similarity between predicted and actual unnatural residues, we employed RMSD_uaa.

### The performance comparison between HighFold4 and AlphaFold3
  We also employed the original AlphaFold3 model to perform the same prediction tasks (Section S3). The average RMSD_Cα values achieved by AlphaFold3 were 1.588 Å for canonical monomers, 8.163 Å for canonical complexes, 1.804 Å for noncanonical monomers, and 0.680 Å for noncanonical complexes. The notably high RMSD value observed for canonical complexes can be attributed to misplacement of the predicted binder at an incorrect binding site, with the RMSD evaluation being based on protein-level structural alignment. In summary, the predictive performance of AlphaFold3 in this context is generally comparable to that of HighFold4.

  However, as noted in the Introduction, explicit cyclization constraints were not incorporated into the AlphaFold. To evaluate this aspect quantitatively, we calculated the cyclization success rate for both our model and AlphaFold3. Notably, our model achieved a 100% cyclization success rate across all tasks. In contrast, AlphaFold3 performed poorly. It attained a perfect success rate only for noncanonical complexes and monomers, with rates of 88.24% and 85.29%, respectively (Fig. 5D and E), but dropped to 67.64% for canonical monomers (Fig. 5A). More notably, AlphaFold3 failed to correctly cyclize any canonical cyclic peptide complexes. Figure 5C presents two representative examples of its cyclization failure. The structure 2n07 is cyclized via an amide bond between Glu [1] and Glu [22], along with disulfide bonds between cysteine residues. However, AlphaFold3 correctly predicted only the disulfide bonds while missing the critical head-to-tail amide linkage, resulting in a predicted structure that is not biologically plausible. A similar limitation is observed in the prediction for PDB entry 6d3x, where AlphaFold3 also generated a linear, cycle-like peptide. In summary, unlike AlphaFold3 which consistently fails to form the essential bond for cyclization, thereby producing linearized structures, our model reliably accomplishes complete and biologically accurate cyclization in peptide modeling.


### The performance comparison between HighFold4 and HighFold3
  As noted in previous studies, existing methods often rely on an offset-based strategy to construct cyclic peptide conformations by AlphaFold. In this work, we compared such a baseline model called HighFold3 with our approach in terms of both RMSD metrics and cyclization success rate. The distributions of various RMSD values are presented in Fig. 3I–L. Our model demonstrates performance comparable to HighFold3 across these structural metrics. Specifically, in cyclic peptide monomer prediction, the average RMSD_Cα values were 1.428 Å (our model) and 1.403 Å (HighFold3), respectively. Similarly, for complex predictions, there were no significant differences between the two models in terms of RMSD-based accuracy. The detailed information about the predictive accuracy of HighFold3 can be founded in the Section S4.

  To further quantify the difference in structural prediction performance between HighFold4 and HighFold3, a quantitative paired statistical benchmark was performed to assess HighFold4 against HighFold3 (Table 1), defining ΔRMSD = RMSD_ HighFold3 – RMSD_ HighFold4 (positive values indicate superior HighFold4 precision). For canonical cyclic peptide monomers and complexes, both models exhibited comparable performance, with most ΔRMSD_mean values falling within a narrow margin (<0.1 Å, P > .05). This demonstrates that extending the network to support custom chemical linkages introduces no performance degradation. Conversely, on noncanonical entries, HighFold4 yielded some advantages, marked by positive mean/median improvements and win rates (the proportion of cases where HighFold4 achieved a lower RMSD than HighFold3) consistently above 50%. Accuracy is improved for noncanonical monomer total atoms (RMSD_aa, P = .013) and noncanonical amino acids (RMSD_uaa, P = .046), underscoring HighFold4’s advanced capability in modeling complex chemical topologies. It should be noted that in the majority of tested scenarios, HighFold4 and HighFold3 exhibit highly comparable performance. It should be noted that our contribution lies in achieving performance comparable to that of HighFold3, while extending the predictive scope to other structural scenarios, such as cyclic peptides involving side-chain bonding.


### Performance of models in predicting side-chain cyclized peptides
  It should be noted that such offset-based methods are may limited to standard cyclization types such as head-to-tail or disulfide bonds, and do not generalize well to cyclization mediated by specialized linkers. This limitation arises because the offset strategy primarily relies on spatial proximity between residues rather than explicitly modeling specific covalent bonds. In other words, while these methods can suggest which atoms might be connected based on distance, they are inherently constrained by the bonding patterns present in their training data, which predominantly consist of amide bond formations. In contrast, our method offers a more flexible and chemically aware approach to cyclization, capable of accommodating diverse bond types and noncanonical linkers. This capability significantly broadens the range of cyclic peptides. To objectively assess the capability gap between models in predicting such peptides, we constructed a dataset comprising side-chain cyclized cyclic peptides for structural prediction. That can be accurately modeled, highlighting the wider applicability and potential of our approach in designing and predicting structures. We selected several side-chain-cyclized peptide sequences from existing experimental literatures [55–58], none of which had experimentally determined PDB structures, for case analysis. For instance, in the case of compound 1 (Fig. 6A), where cyclization should occur between Lys [2] and Glu [9] via an amide bond formed between the ε-amino group of Lys [2] and the γ-carboxyl group of Glu [9], HighFold3 failed to recognize this constraint and generated a linear structure. Although HighFold3 correctly identified the residues requiring connection, it erroneously formed a standard amide bond rather than the specified side-chain linkage. Similar observations were consistently noted in Figs 6B and 4D. As shown in Fig. 6C, although HighFold3 recognized that Lys [5] should be connected to Pro [13], it generated a structurally implausible conformation with multiple atomic clashes. In contrast, HighFold4 successfully predicted the correct structure, cyclized via an amide bond between the ε-amino group of Lys [5] and the C-terminal carboxyl group of Pro [13].

  In summary, our comprehensive evaluation demonstrates that HighFold4 represents a significant advancement in predicting side-chain cyclized peptide structures. Unlike AlphaFold3, which largely failed to generate cyclic conformations, and HighFold3, which while showing improved structural accuracy still struggled with specific cyclization chemistry, HighFold4 consistently produced both geometrically accurate and chemically correct cyclic conformations. This capability stems from its specifically designed architecture that explicitly incorporates cyclization constraints during the structure generation process, enabling it to properly handle non-standard linkages while maintaining overall structural integrity. Furthermore, we employed AlphaFold3 to predict the structures of these peptides. Unsurprisingly, AlphaFold3 predicted all these cyclic peptides as linear chains, as illustrated in Fig. S1.


### The correlation between peptide property and predictive accuracy
  To further investigate the influence of secondary structure types and residue length on prediction performance, we explored the RMSD performance of HighFold4 in predicting our two datasets, set_canonical and set_noncanonical, where the included peptides cover different secondary structure types and residue lengths.

  As shown in Fig.7 our model exhibits distinct performance patterns across cyclic peptides of different lengths. For monomer predictions, the model achieved superior accuracy with longer peptides, as reflected by lower RMSD_Cα values. The average RMSD_Cα was 1.886 Å for peptides of length 5–9 residues, 1.626 Å for those with 10–14 residues, 1.466 Å for 15–19 residues, and 1.436 Å for peptides containing 20 or more residues. This trend of improved accuracy with increasing peptide length may be attributed to the greater propensity of longer sequences to form stable secondary structures, which are inherently less flexible and more constrained than the shorter, loop-dominated conformations. A similar relationship between peptide length and prediction accuracy was consistently observed in both RMSD_aa and RMSD_bb evaluations, further supporting the trend that longer peptides are generally modeled with higher accuracy.

  However, such phenomenon was not observed in the complex structure prediction task, where RMSD distributions showed no statistically significant correlation with peptide length. This absence of correlation may be attributed to structural constraints imposed by the protein target information, which inherently restrict the conformational freedom of the cyclic peptide ligands. Additionally, the limited sample size of longer peptides in our complex dataset may have hindered a comprehensive evaluation of length effects in protein-peptide interactions.

### The correlation between indicators and predictive accuracy
  What’s more, we also explore the correlation between popular indicators and RMSD values due to their application in the protein/peptide design tasks. Figure 8 illustrates the correlation between these metrics. A strong inverse relationship was observed between pLDDT and RMSD values in monomer structure dataset: structures with higher pLDDT scores generally exhibited lower RMSD values. Specifically, when the pLDDT exceeded 95, the corresponding RMSD values typically dropped to around 1. In contrast, the pTM score did not consistently reflect the reliability of the predicted structures. Even in cases with low pTM values, the cyclic peptide structures generated by AlphaFold3 often showed great similarity to the experimentally determined structures. Considering the predicted structures of 2m77 and 2lye as examples, both exhibit low pTM scores (0.03 in each case), yet achieve remarkably low RMSD_Cα values of 1.052 Å and 0.933 Å, respectively, indicating high structural similarity to their native conformations. It is particularly noteworthy that 2lye adopts a flexible loop fold, yet HighFold4 successfully captured its native structure with atomic-level accuracy.

  For the complex dataset, we further investigated the correlation between the RMSD_Cα values of peptide binders and global confidence metrics (pLDDT, pTM). In most cases, higher pLDDT scores correspond to lower RMSD_Cα values, indicating better local accuracy. However, no clear correlation was observed between pTM and structural deviation of the binders.

  Given the application of interface-specific metrics, ipTM and ipAE in assessing protein-peptide interactions, we also evaluated their relationship with binder accuracy. As shown in Fig. 8C, when ipAE fall below 0.1, most predicted binder structures achieve RMSD_Cα values under 1.0 Å, reflecting high structural similarity to native conformations. This supports the utility of ipAE as a reliable indicator for peptide binder design. Similarly, when ipTM exceeded 0.9, RMSD_Cα values remain consistently low, suggesting a positive correlation between interface confidence and prediction accuracy. In summary, both interface-specific metrics, ipAE and ipTM, can serve as effective indicators for assessing the plausibility of designed cyclic peptide binders. It must be emphasized that the confidence metrics generated by the model, such as pLDDT, serve only as diagnostic indicators for complex evaluation rather than definitive proof of binding. For instance, when constructing complexes using experimentally validated negative sequences, HighFold4 can still generate conformations with a pLDDT of ~80 (Table S15). This highlights a known forced binding bias inherent to AlphaFold-like architectures, which tend to output plausible-looking binding modes even in the absence of true biochemical interaction. Nevertheless, current mainstream cyclic peptide design frameworks, including RFdiffusion [35] and BindCraft [59], continue to rely on complex confidence scores as a core benchmark, given their general positive correlation with peptide-protein binding propensity. A recent study has also demonstrated that AlphaFold can discern such false-positive candidates at certain stages based on its confidence scores [60]. In practical virtual screening workflows, however, we do not rely on confidence scores alone. Instead, we implement a multi-parametric evaluation encompassing Rosetta energy scoring, hydrogen-bond networks, geometric complementarity, and various interaction features to eliminate the majority of false positives. Importantly, our previous work, HighPlay, has already validated this screening logic by successfully identifying bioactive cyclic peptide binders with confirmed target affinity [54]. For example, the false-positive conformations observed in Table S15 are frequently filtered out due to excessive internal steric clashes, unfavorable energy profiles, or poor packing. Experienced structural biologists can also rapidly evaluate these predicted complexes through visual inspection to judge their biochemical plausibility. Ultimately, as no virtual screening pipeline can completely delete false positives, users must apply these computational tools rationally, combining metric-driven filters with expert manual inspection, before proceeding to wet-lab experiments for conducting affinity validation.


### Fixed protein structure application in HighFold4
  In practical applications, researchers often need to model multiple cyclic peptide complexes that share the same protein target. Under the default configuration of AlphaFold3, users are required to repeatedly input the target protein sequence and wait for its structure to be predicted each time, a redundant and time-consuming process. To address this limitation, we have introduced an option that allows users to provide a pre-determined protein structure as input to AlphaFold3. This enables the model to focus exclusively on generating the structures of the cyclic peptide ligands, significantly accelerating the prediction process. Inspired by the work of Tang [61] called SiteAF3, we fixed the protein target structure during inference by implementing a conditional diffusion process, thereby avoiding repetitive protein folding calculations and enabling high-throughput modeling of cyclic peptides. This approach can also be viewed as a form of docking, in which the peptide ligand is flexibly sampled and optimized relative to a static protein structure, as displayed in the Fig. 9C.

  To evaluate the improvement brought by this strategy, we adopt the complex structure dataset. As shown in Fig. 9A and B, the prediction accuracy of Highfold4 in predicting canonical and noncanonical complex structure have increases at different degree. Notably, on the noncanonical complex dataset, the average RMSD values for Cα atoms (RMSD_Cα), backbone atoms (RMSD_bb), all atoms (RMSD_aa), and universal heavy atoms (RMSD_uaa) achieved by HighFold4 with SiteAF3 were 0.665 Å, 0.680 Å, 1.135 Å, and 1.034 Å, respectively, detailed results can be found in the Tables S16–S17. These values are consistently lower than those produced by HighFold4 without SiteAF3, demonstrating the efficacy of incorporating a fixed structural template in improving prediction precision. However, this approach is not suitable for allosteric proteins, whose conformations may undergo significant changes upon ligand binding. In such cases, freezing the protein structure could lead to inaccurate predictions, as the model would be unable to capture induced-fit or allosteric effects.


### Limitation
  While our method addresses specific cyclization challenges by enabling flexible ring closure via designated atoms, certain limitations persist. Many of these, such as the most frequent issue of chirality errors and other structural abnormalities, are inherited from the underlying AlphaFold3 architecture. This foundational constraint is compounded by the fact that the AlphaFold3 protocol itself is not trainable. As such, our current strategy is necessarily restricted to feature engineering to encode cyclic backbone information. Ultimately, a more robust solution would require the ability to fine-tune the AlphaFold3 model, allowing for joint optimization of both its architecture and the input feature design to fully adapt it to the unique demands of cyclic peptide prediction.


## Discussion and conclusion
  Based on the AlphaFold3 architecture, we proposed an atom-level cyclization method to achieve more accurate cyclic peptide structure predictions. This method generates the cyclic conformation by forming a bond between two specific atoms within the peptide, which serves as a strong constraint to guide the formation of the cyclic structure. Hierarchical RMSD evaluation was employed to assess our model’s performance in predicting both canonical and noncanonical cyclic peptides.

  For canonical cyclic peptide structures, our model achieved atom-level accuracy. Given that a major contribution of AlphaFold3 lies in its capability to model structures involving noncanonical residues and small molecules, we further investigated our model’s performance on cyclic peptides containing such noncanonical residues. For both noncanonical monomer and complex tasks, our model attained satisfying results. Additionally, we evaluated the model’s performance on unnatural residues, obtaining the satisfying RMSD_uaa values for monomers and complexes. All these results demonstrate the excellent capability of our model in predicting cyclic peptide structures.

  As previously noted, the original AlphaFold3 often generates cyclic-like linear peptides structures that resemble cyclic peptides but lack actual cyclization, making it difficult to evaluate their accuracy using RMSD values alone. To address this issue, we computed the cyclization success rate in our method to verify its ability to form genuine cyclic structures. In all our experiments, the cyclization success rate reached 100%, demonstrating the effectiveness and reliability of our approach. Our model achieved superior performance across all benchmarks, highlighting its strong capability in accurate cyclic peptide prediction. In addition, we applied the work of SiteAF3 into our work and improve the ability of HighFold3 in predicting cyclic peptide complexes. It is important to clarify that that HighFold4 does not replace HighFold3. Instead, it expands the application scope of the model on cyclic peptides based on HighFold3. For standard topologies, such as head-to-tail or disulfide-bonded cyclic peptides, both architectures are equally capable of producing high-quality predictions. Under these scenarios, users can select the optimal conformation based on standard complex metrics, such as pLDDT values and interface hydrogen-bonding networks. As for noncanonical architectures including side-chain-cyclized or custom bond-defined cyclic peptides, HighFold4 is the preferred tool. By explicitly integrating precise atomic connectivity constraints into the algorithm, HighFold4 effectively prevents unphysical geometries and generates chemically reliable cyclic conformations. The corresponding guidelines are outlined in Table 2. We hope this work can accelerate the application of AlphaFold3 in cyclic peptide structure prediction, thereby improving the improvement of the related clinic drugs.
