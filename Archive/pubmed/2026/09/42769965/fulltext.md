# Constrained diffusion-enabled construction of a highly expressed and robust peptide asparaginyl ligase


## Abstract
  Peptide asparaginyl ligases (PALs) hold great promise for peptide macrocyclization and protein bioconjugation, yet their broader application is limited by insufficient thermal stability and low recombinant expression. Here, we report FortiPAL-1, a diffusion-enabled PAL developed through an integrated computational-experimental strategy that couples de novo sequence generation with multi-model-guided mutagenesis and PAL-specific functional refinements. Compared with natural PALs, FortiPAL-1 exhibits markedly enhanced thermal stability (Tm = 69 °C versus ∼50 °C for typical PALs) and achieves a soluble zymogen expression level of 64 mg L−1 in E. coli, substantially exceeding reported expression levels for natural PALs, while maintaining catalytic efficiency comparable to the fastest known natural PAL, butelase-1 (kcat/Km = 1.3 × 106 M−1 s−1). Notably, the enhanced structural stability does not translate into a corresponding shift in the optimal reaction temperature, highlighting a decoupling between global folding stability and catalytic performance. Together, these results demonstrate how a constrained, diffusion-enabled engineering workflow can transform fragile enzyme scaffolds into robust, application-ready biocatalysts and highlight the importance of integrating generative protein design with experimental optimization to translate in silico designs into high-performance dynamic enzymes.


## Introduction
  The construction of complex peptide and protein architectures underlies a wide range of modern therapeutic and biomolecular technologies, from macrocyclic peptide drugs to site-specific protein conjugates.1–3 While chemical synthesis has achieved remarkable advances,4 its application to medium- and long-chain peptides or functionalized proteins remains challenging due to limitations in stereoselectivity, coupling efficiency, and biocompatibility.5 These limitations have driven growing interest in enzymatic ligation systems, which enable precise peptide-bond formation under mild and highly controlled conditions.

  A diverse array of enzymatic ligation platforms has been developed to address these needs,6,7 including natural cyclases,8–11 transpeptidases,12–14 protein splicing enzymes,15,16 and engineered ligating proteases,17–19 underscoring the broad utility of biocatalytic bond formation. Among them, peptide asparaginyl ligases (PALs) have attracted considerable attention owing to their high catalytic efficiency and broad substrate tolerance.20–24 PALs are a subfamily of plant legumains within the C13 cysteine protease family that uniquely catalyze peptide ligation rather than hydrolysis. They mediate precise end-to-end ligation and cyclization of peptides and proteins without auxiliary enzymes or cofactors, enabling applications in site-specific protein modification, complex peptide construction, and cell surface engineering.25–28 However, despite these advantages, their broader application remains limited by intrinsic shortcomings of natural PALs such as butelase-1, 10OaAEP1b,21 and VyPAL2,29 including low prokaryotic zymogen expression (most below 2 mg L−1),30,31 marginal thermal stability (with melting temperature often around 50 °C)32 and sensitivity to pH variation.33,34 Together, these factors restrict their operational window and prevent PALs from reaching the robustness required for preparative or industrial biocatalysis.

  Considerable efforts have been made to overcome these limitations through enzyme engineering. Strategies such as codon optimization,35 flexible termini-truncation,32 fusion expression with soluble tags,36 change of expression systems,29,37 and cap domain stabilization have improved recombinant expression to varying degrees, yet soluble yields generally remain below 20 mg L−1 in recombinant systems.38 Consensus design had produced improvements in expression but not thermal stability. Mutagenesis of ligase-activity determinant (LAD) regions has successfully tuned the ligase-protease balance, enhancing cyclization selectivity, but without addressing intrinsic folding or stability constraints.39 The dual-domain architecture, acid-dependent activation, and structural fragility of PALs further compound these challenges, underscoring the need for an integrated strategy capable of simultaneously optimizing multiple properties, including protein expression, stability, and catalytic activity.

  Recent advances in enzyme engineering have provided powerful strategies to enhance catalytic performance, stability, and manufacturability across diverse biocatalyst families.40–42 Rational design,43 directed evolution,44 and semi-rational design45 have enabled targeted modification of key residues or the exploration of large mutational landscapes guided by structural insight and high-throughput screening. More recently, deep-learning-based de novo protein design has expanded the accessible sequence space beyond natural scaffolds, offering unprecedented opportunities to construct novel or improved enzymes.46–48 However, these emerging technologies have not yet been applied in an integrated manner to PALs, whose structural instability, dual-domain architecture, and acid-dependent activation render single-objective optimization insufficient and often lead to trade-offs between expression, stability, and catalytic activity.

  In this study, we present a diffusion-enabled, multi-tier strategy to systematically address the coupled limitations of PALs. Rather than optimizing individual properties in isolation, this workflow sequentially stabilizes the enzyme scaffold, captures emergent expression-enhancing mutations, and subsequently refines catalytic selectivity and pH-dependent operational robustness. By integrating diffusion-based sequence generation, ensemble-guided stability design, and PAL-specific functional tuning, this study establishes a rational and generalizable framework for developing efficient peptide ligases and related dynamic enzyme families.


## Results

### Diffusion-based generation and selection of sequence-diverse legumain variants
  Experimentally characterized PALs are limited in number and originate from a narrow subset of plant legumains, many of which exhibit modest thermal stability and low heterologous expression. To expand the accessible sequence space beyond natural PALs and uncover additional beneficial sites, we applied SeqGenerator,49 a continuous Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM) designed for template-free generation of functional enzyme sequences. Diffusion models operate by gradually perturbing natural sequences through Gaussian noise and subsequently learning to reconstruct them via iterative denoising, thereby capturing both local and long-range dependencies among residues. This mechanism allows the model to learn an intrinsic, sequence-level representation of enzyme families, enabling efficient exploration of vast sequence landscapes that remain inaccessible to conventional high-throughput or structure-guided design. The diffusion strategy can integrate contextual information across entire sequences, allowing the generation of coherent, functionally consistent variants directly from amino acid data (Fig. 1a).

  Since the rarely-occurring natural PALs belong to a subclass of plant legumains, the diffusion model was trained using a curated dataset comprising 1500 plant legumain sequences (Fig. S1a), including 18 experimentally validated PALs reported previously.31 To ensure a rich genetic diversity within the training set, these sequences were clustered based on a sequence similarity threshold of 90%. Subsequently, SeqGenerator was trained for 100 000 steps with a batch size of 4. Unlike natural PAL sequences, which exhibit a degree of self-similarity (70% median pairwise identity), the generated sequences displayed more variability (63% median pairwise identity, Fig. 1b), which was further elucidated through t-SNE analysis (Fig. 1c). The natural sequences formed large clusters indicative of a high degree of self-similarity, whereas the PAL sequences generated by SeqGenerator exhibited smaller, more dispersed clusters, suggesting an expansion in sequence diversity.

  Despite this increased diversity, the generated sequences retained key functional features of PALs. Sequence logo analysis showed the preservation of conserved activity-related residues (Fig. 1d) and overall amino acid distribution of natural sequences (Fig. 1e and f). In addition, the quality assessments of these sequences, including the predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) using ESMFold50 and self-consistency evaluations using ProteinMPNN,51 demonstrated higher structural prediction confidence of the generated sequences (Fig. S1b). Based on these promising in silico evaluations, we selected 15 top-ranked generated sequences based on ProteinMPNN score (NeoLEG1-15) (Fig. 1c and Table S2), which preserve the catalytic residue (N59, H165, and C207 in butelase-1 numbering), and S1-pocket-forming residues (R61, H62, D164, E205, S235, and D257) (Fig. S2).


### Functional screening identifies active NeoLEG variants with enhanced cyclization bias
  To enable efficient evaluation of NeoLEG1-15, a unified expression and screening workflow was established. Specifically, NeoLEG1-15 were expressed by substituting N-terminal pro-domain from OaAEP1b to minimize sequence-dependent variability during initial folding. A split-PAL strategy was adopted,52 in which the cap and core domains were cloned separately on a pET-duet vector to ease the acidic activation step required for natural PALs (Fig. S3). Following a small-scale expression in SHuffle T7 E. coli, the clarified cell lysates were directly subjected to activity and thermostability assessments.

  All 15 candidates were first screened for catalytic activity in cyclization of a model substrate GIARGDYKSNSL (Fig. 2a). Reactions were performed at pH 5.0 and 37 °C, an acidic condition allowing auto-activation and reaction to take place spontaneously. Reaction progresses were examined by LC-MS and HPLC at different time points (Fig. S4). Among the designed variants, catalytic competence varied widely, ranging from no detectable activity to near-complete substrate conversion, indicating substantial functional divergence within the diffusion-generated sequence set. Five variants (NeoLEG1, NeoLEG6, NeoLEG7, NeoLEG8 and NeoLEG9) showed no detectable activity, whereas the remaining variants exhibited partial to extensive substrate conversion after 30 min reactions. Seven variants, including NeoLEG2, NeoLEG3, NeoLEG4, NeoLEG5, NeoLEG10, NeoLEG12 and NeoLEG14, achieved >70% substrate conversion. Among these, NeoLEG2 and NeoLEG4 preferentially catalyzed cyclization, yielding 83.4% and 66.7% cyclic product, respectively, corresponding to cyclization-to-hydrolysis (C/H) ratios of 9.83 and 2.56, respectively (Fig. 2b). Western blot analysis indicated that the observed activity differences were not driven by expression levels alone. For example, NeoLEG11 showed one of the strongest bands yet exhibited neither hydrolysis nor ligation activity, confirming that catalytic performance was sequence-dependent rather than expression-dependent (Fig. S5).

  Variants with higher cyclization activity, NeoLEG2 and NeoLEG4, were further evaluated for thermal tolerance (Fig. 2c). Enzymes were pre-equilibrated in reaction buffer (pH 5.0) at the indicated temperatures (35–60 °C) for 30 s, followed by substrate addition and incubation at the same temperature for 30 min. Both variants showed maximal activity at 55 °C under these assay conditions, exceeding the typical temperature optima reported for natural PALs. Importantly, NeoLEG2 consistently maintained a higher C/H ratio than NeoLEG4 across the temperature range tested, indicating a more robust ligation bias under thermal stress. Considering its superior cyclization selectivity and favorable temperature tolerance, NeoLEG2 was selected as the parental variant for subsequent engineering.


### Identifying stability hotspots through multi-tool ΔΔG prediction and core-domain mutational scanning
  To systematically identify stability-enhancing mutations in NeoLEG2, we first performed ΔΔG prediction using FoldX,53 Rosetta_ddg,54 and Pythia,55 which offer complementary strengths in empirical energy estimation, all-atom conformational sampling, and machine-learning-based stability prediction, respectively. Consensus ranking from these tools yielded a prioritized single-point mutant library (Tables S3 and S4). Residues within 5 Å of the catalytic triad were excluded to avoid perturbing catalytic function, resulting in 125 candidate mutations for mutagenesis, including 68 mutations across 26 sites in the core domain and 57 mutations across 28 sites in the cap domain (Fig. 3a and S6).

  We first performed activity screening on the core domain mutants using a fluorescence-based assay in which either cyclization or hydrolysis of the substrate GIARGDYE(Edans)SNSLK(Dabcyl) led to increased FRET signal. As both reaction pathways produce fluorescence, this assay was used as a primary screen for catalytic activity rather than a direct measure of ligation activity. Pre-screening experiments established that pre-incubation of NeoLEG2 at 55 °C for 20 min followed by a standard 30 min reaction at 37 °C produced a sensitive window for differentiating mutants (Fig. S7). Under these conditions, 20 mutants exhibited higher residual activity than NeoLEG2 (Fig. 3b). Re-evaluation under more stringent conditions (preheating at 60 °C for 10 minutes, followed by 37 °C reaction for 30 minutes) further narrowed the set to four single-point mutations N138D, S267C, S272L, and Q312E (Fig. 3c), which gave 177.3%, 225.8%, 161.8%, and 167.6% yield relative to NeoLEG2, respectively. Differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) confirmed their enhanced thermal stability, with melting temperatures of 61.81 °C, 63.87 °C, 63.95 °C, and 63.19 °C, respectively, compared to 60.86 °C for NeoLEG2 at pH 5.0 (Table 1). Western blot analysis of recombinant expression indicated comparable expression levels, confirming that the observed activity improvements primarily arose from increased thermal stability rather than expression differences (Fig. 3d).

  To assess potential synergistic effects and further enhance the thermal stability, these four beneficial mutations were assembled into all possible combinations, generating 11 multi-point variants. Most combinatorial mutants showed additive or synergistic stabilization, with the quadruple mutant N138D/S267C/S272L/Q312E/displaying the strongest thermal resilience, retaining nearly three times the catalytic activity of NeoLEG2 after thermal challenge and exhibited a melting temperature of 68.58 °C at pH 5.0 (Fig. 3e and S12). This represents an increase of nearly 20 °C compared to reported natural PALs.


### Structural mechanism underlying the stability gains of the core-domain mutants
  Molecular dynamics simulations at 300 K of the core-domain combinatorial mutant (N138D/S267C/S272L/Q312E) showed that the quadruple mutant adopts a more compact conformation than NeoLEG2, as indicated by a reduced radius of gyration (Rg) and faster convergence to a stable RMSD plateau (Fig. 3f and S8a). Local flexibility analysis identified two thermally labile loop regions (residues 165–169 and 232–236) that exhibited markedly reduced RMSF amplitudes in the mutant (Fig. 3g). These regions are typically prone to conformational shifts under thermal stress, and the decreased flexibility implies greater structural rigidity, which likely contributes to the improved thermal tolerance. In contrast, solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) and the number of hydrogen bonds showed only minimal changes (Fig. S8b and c). Among the mutated sites, the only notable local interaction change was the formation of an additional hydrogen bond introduced by the N138D substitution (Fig. 3h). These observations indicated that enhanced stability does not primarily arise from altered hydrophobic packing or expanded hydrogen-bond networks.

  To directly probe the temperature-dependent conformational behavior, we performed temperature-gradient MD simulations (45–70 °C) for both the wild-type NeoLEG2 and the 4 M mutant. RMSD analysis showed that both proteins retained structurally stable at 45, 50, 55, 60, 65, and 70 °C; however, above 50 °C, RMSD amplitudes increased substantially, and by 60 °C the wild-type displayed clear signs of structural disorganization, whereas the mutant retained relatively stable backbone fluctuations (Fig. S9a). RMSF analysis further revealed that, although the specific residues exhibiting increased fluctuations differed across temperatures, the affected positions consistently clustered within flexible loop regions located on the same surface at the catalytic triad (Fig. S9b and c). Such loop elements are particularly susceptible to temperature-induced mobility and are likely to influence the catalytic geometry indirectly. Importantly, the catalytic triad itself exhibited comparable RMSF values before and after mutation, suggesting that the improved thermal robustness was achieved without rigidifying the catalytic center. Together, these results suggest that the enhanced stability of the combinatorial mutant is primarily associated with increased global compactness and suppressed dynamics in key flexible regions, rather than from the formation of new stabilizing interactions or long-range cooperative effects.


### Cap-domain mutations enhance expression and enable final multidomain combination
  As cap-domain stabilization facilitating recombinant expression was observed in the engineering of VyPAL2 into VyOpt1.56 Guided by this observation, we examined whether shortlisted mutations in the cap domain of NeoLEG2 exert similar effects. Screening of cap-domain mutations using the same FRET assay revealed that most variants displayed fluorescence levels comparable to or higher than NeoLEG2 under preheating at 60 °C for 10 min followed by a 30 min reaction at 37 °C (Fig. 4a). Western blot analysis demonstrated that these elevated fluorescence signals largely reflected increased protein expression, rather than improved thermal stability (Fig. 4b). Subsequently, six most beneficial mutations were combined, and the variant, S319P/S388E/S389E/R425P/S427G/S454C showed the most pronounced expression enhancement (Fig. 4b), with the band intensity approximately 20 times higher than NeoLEG2.

  Building on this finding, we combined six cap-domain mutations that collectively conferred enhanced expression with the four core-domain stabilizing mutations to generate a combinatorial mutant 10 M (N138D/S267C/S272L/Q312E/S319P/S388E/S389E/R425P/S427G/S454C). Western blot analysis demonstrated that the recombinant 10 M exhibited an approximately 40-fold higher expression level than NeoLEG2 (Fig. 4c), while preserving the elevated thermal resilience imparted by the core-domain mutations. These results demonstrate that core-derived stability mutations and cap-derived expression mutations act synergistically, enabling the construction of a PAL variant with both high stability and improved manufacturability.


### LAD-guided engineering suppresses hydrolysis and yields the final FortiPAL-1 variant
  Although NeoLEG2 and its derivatives exhibited relatively high C/H ratio, they did not conform the canonical LAD motifs identified in natural PALs, which are short peptide motifs flanking the S1 substrate-binding pocket governing the hydrolysis-versus-transpeptidation ratio.31 To further reduce hydrolytic side reactions observed in NeoLEG2-derived variants, we applied targeted LAD mutagenesis on the multi-domain variant 10 M. Screening of LAD mutants revealed that the substitution of the gatekeeper residue Gly237 at LAD1 with Ala, together with substitution of the Thr167-Thr168 dipeptide at LAD2 with Gly-Ala (Fig. S10), further reduced hydrolytic byproduct formation and yielded an optimized new peptide asparagine ligase, designated FortiPAL-1 (Fig. S11). This NeoLEG2-derived 13 M variant was subsequently subjected to systematic enzymatic characterization to assess the effects of these modifications on catalytic performance.


### Biochemical characterization of FortiPAL-1: expression, stability, thermal resilience, and catalytic performance
  FortiPAL-1 was successfully expressed in E. coli using the split-PAL format. SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed that the molecular weight of the activated enzyme matched the theoretical value (34.55 kDa) (Fig. 5a). After purification, FortiPAL-1 zymogen proenzyme was obtained at approximately 64 mg L−1, over 40-fold of NeoLEG2 (Fig. 5b). We further evaluated the recovery rate of core domain after activation and SEC purification. Starting from 30 mg of FortiPAL-1 zymogen, the estimated amount of recovered core domain from the four collected SEC fractions was not less than 19 mg, corresponding to an estimated core-domain recovery of approximately 40 mg L−1 based on the zymogen expression level (Fig. S13). We speculate that this efficient recovery may be facilitated by the split-expression strategy, which appears to promote dissociation of the cap domain during activation (Fig. S13c).

  The pH dependence of FortiPAL-1 was evaluated at 37 °C for 5 min using GIARGDYKSNSL (E : S = 1 : 5000). The enzyme was catalytically active across pH 4.5–7.5, with maximal activity at pH 6.0 (Fig. 5c). Parallel DSF measurements revealed that FortiPAL-1 exhibited Tm values up to 68.46 ± 0.13 °C at pH 5.0, which is substantially higher than the reported values of natural ligases such as butelase-1 (Tm ≈ 52 °C at pH 6.0) and OaAEP1b-C247A (Tm ≈ 51.95 °C at pH 5.0) (Fig. 5f, S12 and Table S5). Temperature-activity profiling showed that FortiPAL-1 maintained high activity between 45 and 55 °C across all tested pH values, with maximal activity at 55 °C, followed by a decline at higher temperature (Fig. 5d). This temperature gap between reactivity-optimal and the global unfolding temperature indicates that thermal inactivation is likely driven by local perturbations at the active site rather than global denaturation.

  To evaluate thermal resilience, the enzyme was incubated at 60 °C and 65 °C for 10 min, followed by rapid cooling on ice for 5 min, and activity was assayed at 55 °C. Samples treated at 60 °C recovered activity comparable to the untreated control, whereas treatment at 65 °C led to moderate loss of activity, suggesting that FortiPAL-1 tolerates transient thermal stress with partial reversibility up to its near-melting regime (Fig. 5e).

  Catalytic efficiency of FortiPAL-1 was examined using the cyclization substrate GIARGDYASNAL at its optimal reaction pH and temperature (pH 6.0 and 55 °C), yielded a kcat/Km of 1.34 × 106 M−1 s−1, which is comparable to the most efficient known ligase butelase-1 (Fig. 5g). These results demonstrate that sequential engineering of stability, expression, and hydrolysis suppression culminated in FortiPAL-1, a highly stable, high-expression, and catalytically efficient peptide ligase.

  To examine substrate specificity of this artificial ligase, three peptide libraries were constructed to probe the influence of residues at P2′, P1″ and P2″ positions, namely RGDYNSX: GLARY, YRNSL: XLARY, and YRNSL: GXKRY (X = 20 natural amino acids). The P2′ position showed stringent selectivity, favoring Leu/Val/Cys/Met/Ile/Phe. In contrast, the incoming nucleophile exhibited substantially broader tolerance. When P2″ was fixed as the preferred Leu, nearly all residues were accepted at the P1″ position, except Pro, which led to increased hydrolytic byproduct formation (∼65%). When Gly was introduced at P1″ to facilitate nucleophilic attack, P2″ tolerated a wide range of residues, with bulky hydrophobic side chains (Trp, Leu, Phe, Ile) resulting in near-complete substrate conversion and elevated ligation-to-hydrolysis (L/H) ratios (>4), particularly for Trp (L/H = 12.64) (Fig. 5h). These results indicate differential positional preferences adjacent to the NSL motif, with P1″ accommodating diverse residues and P2″ favoring bulky hydrophobic side chains, sharing the same key positional preferences with natural PALs. Notably, disfavored residues at the P2″ position tended to promote hydrolysis rather than remaining unreactive. In addition, the RGDYNSL-GLARY substrate pair exhibited lower overall conversion than the YRNSL-GLARY/GLKRY substrate pairs under identical conditions, suggesting that reaction outcomes are influenced by the combined effects of multiple substrate positions beyond those explicitly varied here.


### Surface charge engineering enhances pH stability
  Despite its improved stability and expression, FortiPAL-1 remains an acidic enzyme that exhibits optimal activity at pH 6.0, and its activity dropped sharply at pH 7.0 and above. To improve catalytic performance under near-neutral conditions, we applied an EDC/NHS-mediated surface charge modification strategy that has previously been shown to enhance conformational stability of human legumain by neutralizing acidic surface residues.57

  Specifically, this approach neutralizes the negative charged surface of the enzyme by conjugating ethanolamine with side chain carboxylic acids and thereby increased the isoelectric point to improve structural robustness and catalytic adaptability at higher pH values. Following surface charge modification, the modified enzyme exhibited improved pH adaptability, with enhanced catalytic performance observed under mildly alkaline conditions (pH 6.5–7.5) compared with the unmodified enzyme (Fig. 5i). Consistent with this observation, DSF further showed that the modified enzyme retained substantially higher melting temperatures across pH 6.0–7.5 compared with the unmodified protein (Fig. 5j). Together, these results demonstrate that surface charge engineering effectively broadens the operational pH window of FortiPAL-1 and enhances its thermal stability and catalytic performance under neutral reaction conditions.


## Discussion
  Unlike stable scaffold proteins, catalytic enzymes rely on conformational gating,58 loop rearrangements,59–61 pH-coupled structural transitions,62 or metastable intermediate states to achieve activity.63 These dynamic features make them functionally powerful but often structurally fragile. Attempts to improve activity, stability, or operational robustness often produce trade-offs rather than synergistic gains.60,64,65 Taking PAL as a representative, stabilization must avoid rigidifying conformational transitions; expression often reflects domain-specific folding barriers rather than global stability; and catalytic selectivity can be modified only after the scaffold is sufficiently stabilized to tolerate active-site perturbation. For this reason, we employed a complementary set of physics- and ML-based generative and stability scoring models, whose consensus improves robustness against model-specific biases and is better aligned with the requirements of stabilizing a dynamic, low-homology, and de novo-designed legumain scaffold. Importantly, diffusion-based sequence generation did not merely expand sequence diversity but altered the natura of the engineering starting point. The resulting NeoLEG scaffolds represent pre-optimized yet non-evolutionarily saturated sequences that tolerate extensive downstream stabilization, expression enhancement, and functional tuning without catastrophic loss of activity, a property rarely observed in natural PALs.

  Thermal characterization of FortiPAL-1 revealed a decoupling between its structural stability and functional stability, reflected by its high melting temperature (∼70 °C) and lower optimal catalytic temperature (55 °C, at pH 6.0). This indicates that the loss of activity at elevated temperatures is not caused by global unfolding. Instead, elevated temperatures may cause local perturbations of the finely tuned loop dynamics that position key catalytic residues or alter substrate-binding kinetics, thereby hindering a productive enzyme–substrate complex formation. Similarly, studies on other enzymes have demonstrated that elevated temperatures can selectively disrupt catalytically relevant loop dynamics without causing global unfolding, ultimately leading to reduced enzymatic activity.66,67 This interpretation is further supported by the observation that FortiPAL-1 does not rank among the top candidates based solely on predicted ΔΔG scores (Table 1). Molecular dynamics analysis provides a mechanistic explanation consistent with this view. Rather than producing wholesale rearrangements in hydrogen bonding or hydrophobic packing, the stabilizing mutations primarily redistribute local flexibility and rigidify thermally sensitive distal loops. This pattern suggests a form of long-range entropic stabilization, in which mutations located away from the flexible elements reduce conformational heterogeneity in regions critical for maintaining catalytic geometry. Such effects are well-documented in hyper-stable natural enzymes and engineered scaffolds and are particularly relevant for PALs, whose catalytic cycle involves conformational gating and pH-coupled rearrangements. Consistent with this mechanism, FortiPAL-1 exhibited strong thermal recovery after transient heat exposure, a critical attribute for industrial processes involving repeated temperature fluctuations or physicochemical stress.

  Another important outcome of this work is that several PAL-specific optimizations. Including cap-domain mutations to enhance soluble expression, LAD-guided tuning to bias ligation over hydrolysis, and surface-charge neutralization to broaden pH tolerance, can be effectively applied to an artificially designed enzyme scaffold, provided that the underlying structure is sufficiently stabilized.

  The expression-enhancing effect by cap-domain mutations did not arise from changes to intrinsic catalytic stability but instead appears to reflect enhanced folding efficiency during heterologous expression. Although the precise molecular origin remains to be fully elucidated, the location of these expression-promoting residues at turning regions of the cap domain provide a plausible explanation for their folding-enhancing effects. These observations indicate that, in dynamic enzymes, recombinant expression can represent an emergent and partially orthogonal optimization axis that is not reliably predicted from stability metrics alone.

  Notably, NeoLEG2 does not conform to the canonical LAD motifs identified in natural PALs yet still biased the reaction outcome toward ligation. This observation suggests that, while LAD1 and LAD2 provide a useful and transferable engineering handle, they were originally derived from studies of natural sequences, and their effects are likely sequence-dependent and influenced by the surrounding scaffold architecture. In the case of NeoLEG2, which originates from a de novo-designed sequence background, additional structural or dynamic factors beyond the canonical LAD motifs may therefore contribute to the ligase-protease balance. It should be noted that full implementation of canonical LAD configurations, as appeared in most natural PALs as Val/Ile in LAD1 and Ala-Pro/Ala-Ala in LAD2,31 was constrained in this study by expression and folding limitations of the engineered variants. As a result, LAD-guided tuning in FortiPAL-1 represents a compromise between improving ligation selectivity and maintaining sufficient expression and stability, rather than an exhaustive optimization of hydrolysis suppression. Importantly, the increased L/H ratio observed upon LAD-guided modification highlights the practical utility of this PAL-specific engineering handle, even when applied to non-canonical sequence scaffolds.

  Interestingly, although both the P2′ and P2″ positions are expected to engage the S2′ region, their optimal residue preferences differed, with the highest L/H ratio and ligation yield observed for the YRNSL: GW substrate combination. This preference may reflect substrate-pair-dependent effects, including potential contributions from partial reaction reversibility, in which the NGW ligation product is less readily reprocessed than the NSL-containing substrate, thereby shifting the reaction equilibrium toward product formation. By contrast, disfavored P2″ residues altered reaction partitioning without abolishing turnover. While the structural basis of this behavior remains unresolved, these results highlight an additional layer of substrate-dependent selectivity beyond canonical LAD-based positional rules.

  Surface-charge neutralization to broaden pH tolerance, originally reported for human legumains, represents another PAL-specific optimization step. Although these refinements on expression, directionality, and pH-tolerance are case specific, they are enabled by prior scaffold stabilization, and the overall multi-tier engineering strategy is broadly applicable. As diffusion-based and other generative protein design models continue to mature, their effective integration with constraint-guided experimental engineering will be critical for transforming in silico designs into robust biocatalysts.


## Conclusions
  Taken together, this study demonstrates that constraint-guided, multi-tier engineering can effectively resolve the coupled challenges of stability, expression, and catalytic performance in dynamic enzyme scaffolds. Using PAL as a model system, we show that scaffold stabilization, followed by expression enhancement and targeted functional refinement, enables the generation of a robust ligase variant with improved thermostability, high soluble zymogen expression in E. coli, and expanded pH tolerance. The resulting enzyme, FortiPAL-1, thus provides a more industrial-relevant platform for peptide ligation, macrocyclization, and bioconjugation. More broadly, this work illustrates how integrating generative protein design with constraint-aware experimental optimization can transform fragile natural or de novo-derived protein scaffolds into practical biocatalysts for chemically demanding applications.
