## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The abstract presents a conceptually valuable contribution to the field of generative model interpretability, addressing a real gap in therapeutic sequence design. However, the evidence provided in the abstract is insufficient to evaluate the technical soundness, statistical rigor, or biological validity of the proposed method. The claims of validation and utility are plausible but not verifiable from the supplied material.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Computational biologists, machine learning researchers, and therapeutic antibody engineers would be interested. The method addresses a practical need for interpreting generative models trained on positive-only data, which is common in biological settings where negative data are unreliable or ill-defined. The potential to validate generative design strategies without negative labels has broad applicability in protein engineering and drug discovery.
- **Major strengths** The problem addressed is well-motivated and practically important. The use of Integrated Gradients for autoregressive generative models is a reasonable extension of established attribution techniques. The inclusion of synthetic datasets with known ground truths for validation is a strong methodological choice, as it allows for controlled assessment of statistical behavior.
- **Major Concerns** None identified from the supplied material, as the abstract provides insufficient detail to raise specific technical concerns. However, the lack of evidence prevents a full assessment.
- **Minor Comments** None identified from the supplied material, as the abstract is too brief to support specific minor critiques.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** The abstract does not provide any quantitative results, such as attribution accuracy metrics, comparison to baseline methods, or statistical significance tests. Without these, the claim that GAMA "recovers biologically relevant features" is not established. Additionally, the abstract does not describe how Integrated Gradients is adapted to autoregressive models, which is a non-trivial technical step.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: The concept of attribution for generative sequence models is novel and addresses a clear gap. Scientific importance: High, given the growing use of generative models in therapeutic design. Interdisciplinary readership: The work bridges machine learning and immunology, which could attract a broad audience. Technical soundness: Not assessable from the abstract; the adaptation of Integrated Gradients to autoregressive models requires careful handling of sequential dependencies and baseline selection. Readability for nonspecialists: The abstract is clear and accessible, though technical terms such as "Integrated Gradients" and "autoregressive" are not explained.
- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The abstract is promising, but the lack of quantitative results and methodological detail prevents a supportive recommendation. A full manuscript with rigorous validation would be needed to assess the case.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that GAMA "enables model interpretability and the validation of generative sequence design strategies" is unsupported by the abstract, as no demonstration of interpretability outcomes or design validation is provided.
- The claim that GAMA "recovers biologically relevant features" from experimental antibody-antigen binding data is unsupported, as no specific features or biological findings are described.
- The claim that GAMA was "assessed" using synthetic datasets is unverifiable, as no statistical metrics or comparison to ground truths are reported.
