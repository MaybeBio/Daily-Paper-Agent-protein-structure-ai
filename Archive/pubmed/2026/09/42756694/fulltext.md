# Computational Design of RNA Aptamers Targeting Oncogenic
miR-10b Using T‑SELEX and Molecular Dynamics-Based Stability
Metrics


## Abstract
  Aptamers are versatile single-stranded nucleic acids with high specificity and stability, making them ideal for targeting challenging biomolecules. Their structural flexibility enables selective recognition of nonprotein targets such as microRNAs (miRNAs). Among these, miR-10b plays a pivotal role in cancer progression and metastasis through its oncogenic 5p and 3p arms. We propose designing aptamers specifically targeting miR-10b through a computational framework to enable precise and efficient regulation of aberrant miRNA activity in cancer. Herein, we introduce a multiscale computational workflow built on the T_SELEX framework, integrating sequence generation, folding of secondary and tertiary structure prediction, then virtual screening, quantum mechanical (QM) calculations, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and MM-GBSA analysis for the design and evaluation of RNA aptamer candidates targeting pre-miR-10b and its mature 5p and 3p arms. This robust workflow enables large-scale identification and structural evaluation of computationally promising RNA aptamer candidates for RNA-targeted drug discovery. In this study, a library of 1100 22-mer aptamers was virtually screened, with heatmap and interaction energy analyses revealing strong affinities toward hsa-miR-25-5p. Molecular docking identified aptamer557 as the top binder to miR-10b-3p with −545.96 docking score and aptamer899 as the best for miR-10b-5p with −482.55 docking score. QM single-point calculations assessed electronic stability via total molecular energy and the HOMO–LUMO gaps, showing model-dependent variations. MD simulations incorporated novel stability metrics combining RMSD area integration and changepoint detection, alongside hydrogen bonding, RMSF, and radius of gyration analyses. The miR-10b-3p complexes exhibited lower RMSF and RMSD values with more persistent hydrogen bonds, reflecting stronger and more stable interactions than 5p complexes. Interestingly, despite both the aptamer and target miRNA adopting folded secondary and tertiary structures, partial Watson–Crick interactions were observed during both docking and molecular dynamics simulations, particularly at accessible loop and terminal regions, suggesting a hybrid binding mechanism involving localized base pairing together with structure-dependent structural recognition. MM-GBSA binding energy analysis further supported these findings, confirming that our T-SELEX-guided in silico strategy identifies computationally promising aptamer-like candidates and provides a robust framework for the rational design of RNA-targeting therapeutics.


## Introduction
  Aptamers, first introduced in 1990 by Ellington and Szostak, are short single-stranded nucleic acids capable of binding to a wide range of molecular targets, including proteins, small molecules, ions, and whole cells. Due to their high specificity, tunable affinity, and structural flexibility, aptamers have been extensively explored in analytical, diagnostic, and therapeutic applications. −     Compared with antibodies, aptamers offer several advantages; they are less immunogenic, can be synthesized entirely in vitro, exhibit enhanced chemical stability, and allow for shorter generation times and easier modifications. −    Despite these benefits, the clinical translation of aptamers has been limited, with only one aptamer, pegaptanib, reaching FDA approval. −   This bottleneck is largely attributed to the time-consuming SELEX (systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment) procedure and uncertainties regarding in vivo activity.

  SELEX is an iterative selection process involving large oligonucleotide libraries, target binding, partitioning of bound and unbound species, amplification of high-affinity candidates, and progressive enrichment over multiple rounds. Although highly effective, conventional wet-lab SELEX is labor-intensive, costly, and often requires weeks to months of repeated experimental cycles with variable success rates. To overcome these limitations, several modified approaches such as SOMAmer-based SELEX, thioaptamers, and X-Aptamers have been developed. ,  Among these innovations, we previously introduced T_SELEX (theoretical-SELEX), a computationally guided SELEX framework designed to preserve the selection logic of classical SELEX while replacing repetitive laboratory cycles with in silico enrichment and structural validation. In T_SELEX, the process begins with sequence generation using the base randomization algorithm (BRA), which creates diverse virtual aptamer libraries while preserving sequence uniqueness and structural feasibility. This is followed by secondary structure prediction using RNAfold and tertiary structure generation using RNAComposer, where folding is a critical step because aptamer recognition depends not only on sequence complementarity but also on the formation of stable, functionally relevant three-dimensional conformations.

  The classical binding step is then represented by RNA–RNA interaction prediction and molecular docking, where candidate aptamers are evaluated against the target based on interaction energy, structural complementarity, and binding orientation. The separation step is computationally achieved by ranking candidates using docking scores, confidence scores, fitness quality, and predicted interaction energies. Finally, enrichment can be performed through iterative filtering using quantum mechanical calculations, molecular dynamics simulations, and MM-PBSA binding free energy analysis to retain only the most stable and energetically favorable candidates. Unlike conventional in silico docking workflows that often rely only on single-step virtual screening, T_SELEX maintains the progressive enrichment architecture of SELEX by integrating sequence generation, multiscale folding, structural prediction, docking, and postdocking refinement into a unified selection framework. For this reason, the term “SELEX” remains appropriate, as T_SELEX preserves the conceptual principles of systematic ligand evolution and enrichment while replacing experimental rounds with computational selection cycles. ,  This approach enables rapid generation of aptamer-like candidates designed against both conventional protein targets and emerging nonprotein targets such as microRNAs (miRNAs).

  MiRNAs are small noncoding RNAs that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally and play central roles in cancer development and progression. −   Dysregulation of miRNAs has been linked to tumor initiation, metastasis, and therapy resistance, making them attractive candidates for therapeutic intervention and biomarker discovery. ,  Among these, miR-10b is of particular interest due to its dual role in cancer biology. In some cancers, such as glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), miR-10b-5p and miR-10b-3p arms function as oncogenes by promoting proliferation, invasion, and immune evasion. −     Conversely, in other contexts such as uterine leiomyosarcoma, miR-10b-5p exhibits tumor-suppressive properties. ,  This functional heterogeneity highlights the therapeutic complexity of miR-10b and the need for precise inhibition strategies tailored to cancer type and microenvironment. Recent preclinical studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of inhibiting miR-10b using antagomirs, nanoparticles, and liposome-based delivery systems. −       However, these approaches face challenges such as off-target effects, limited stability, and delivery inefficiency. Here, we propose targeting these mature miR10b arms using aptamers. The aptamer tunable structure, high specificity, and ability to discriminate between closely related miRNA isoforms (5p vs 3p arms) make them ideal candidates for selectively inhibiting miR-10b-5p and miR-10b-3p.

  In this study, we applied the T-SELEX computational framework to design and evaluate RNA aptamer candidates targeting both miR-10b-5p and miR-10b-3p, as well as the precursor pre-miR-10b. By integrating sequence generation, RNA folding, molecular docking, quantum mechanical calculations, molecular dynamics simulations, and binding free energy analysis, our aim was to identify structurally stable and energetically favorable candidates capable of selectively interacting with oncogenic miR-10b. This approach seeks to accelerate the rational discovery of RNA-targeting aptamer-like therapeutics and provide a computational foundation for future experimental validation in miRNA-driven cancers.


## Methodology

### Data Generation and Virtual
Screening
  A data set of 1100 aptamers, each 22 nucleotides in length, was generated using the base randomization algorithm within the T-SELEX framework. Secondary structures of these sequences were predicted using the RNAfold algorithm, while tertiary structures were modeled through RNAComposer. The BRA was used because of its ability to generate a diverse library of unique, nonredundant RNA aptamer sequences, enabling broad exploration of sequence and structural space while maintaining computational feasibility for large-scale virtual screening and downstream structural analyses. To predict secondary structures, the minimum free energy (MFE) structure was used as the primary representative conformation for large-scale screening. It is acknowledged that RNA molecules often exist as structural ensembles rather than a single unique conformation, and alternative suboptimal structures may also contribute to biological function and binding behavior. In this study, the MFE structure was selected as a computationally practical reference for tertiary structure generation and docking.

  These RNAComposer-generated structures were treated as starting conformations rather than final models, as tertiary RNA structures typically require further refinement for improved physical realism. The library size of 1100 sequences was selected to balance sufficient exploration of sequence diversity with computational complexity and cost for downstream analyses. Since each candidate required secondary structure prediction, tertiary structure modeling, molecular docking, quantum mechanical calculations, molecular dynamics simulations, and MM-PBSA binding energy evaluation, significantly larger libraries would have imposed substantial computational cost without proportional improvement in candidate enrichment. Regarding the targets, this study focused specifically on the precursor of hsa-miR-10b and its two mature arms (5p and 3p). For structural preparation, these miRNA targets were modeled in a manner consistent with the aptamer data set. The target sequences were obtained from miRbase (hsa-miR-10b, hsa-miR-5p and hsa-miR-3p). Owing to their short sequence length, one of these three miRNA structures, hsa-miR-10b-5p, exhibited an unfolded minimum free energy (MFE = 0 kcal/mol) conformation. In this case, kinetically accessible local minima with positive free energy were considered. As such, for hsa-miR-10b-5p (which of course did not fold into a stable structure), the local minima structure were selected for downstream analysis.

  Virtual screening of the aptamer library against miR-10b arms was conducted using the docking module of the T-SELEX program, which applies HDOCK molecular docking algorithms to rank aptamer–miRNA complexes based on predicted interaction energies and binding complementarity. For virtual screening, not all 1200 generated aptamers were used, as the study specifically focused on structurally folded aptamer-like candidates. Aptamers with MFE = 0 kcal/mol were excluded because they did not adopt stable, folded, predicted secondary and tertiary structures. After this filtering step, 819 folded aptamers were retained and subsequently docked against the three miRNA targets: pre-miR-10b, miR-10b-5p, and miR-10b-3p.


### Post-Docking Analysis
  For each aptamer–miRNA complex, T-SELEX generated up to 100 docking poses using the HDOCK algorithm to account for the structural flexibility of RNA molecules and the absence of a single rigid binding pocket. These poses were ranked according to docking score and confidence score. From this larger pool, the top 10 highest-confidence models were retained for structural inspection and interaction analysis. Among these, the first five models were selected for quantum mechanical calculations and molecular dynamics simulations based on confidence score, docking score consistency, hydrogen bonding patterns, and structural diversity of the binding interface. Postdocking analysis was performed to refine and statistically evaluate the results of the virtual screening. Docking scores for each aptamer–miRNA complex were collected and normalized. Z-scores were calculated to enable comparative assessment across the entire data set. Visualization of the results was carried out through scatter plots, correlating docking scores, Z-scores, and predicted fitness values. This approach provided a robust statistical basis for identifying aptamers with the strongest predicted binding to the mature miR-10b-5p and miR-10b-3p.


### Quantum Mechanical Calculations
  To further assess binding interactions, the best five models from the top four aptamer–miRNA complexes were subjected to quantum mechanical (QM) calculations. Single-point energy calculations were carried out using the GFN2-xTB semiempirical method, which includes dispersion corrections and is well suited for large biomolecular complexes. These calculations enabled evaluation of conformational stability, electronic properties, and intermolecular interactions. Relevant parameters extracted included total energy, HOMO–LUMO gap, dipole moment, and gradients, providing deeper insight into the electronic structure of the aptamer–miRNA complexes.


### Molecular Dynamics Simulations
  The same docked complexes were further investigated using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to assess structural stability over time. Simulations were carried out using an in-house PySMQM workflow that we implemented within the context of GROMACS simulations, a statistical mechanics and quantum mechanics extension we developed for RNA studies. Complexes were solvated in a cubic water box with TIP3P solvent and neutralized with counterions. Topologies were prepared using the Amber force field. Each system underwent energy minimization followed by gradual heating from 0 to 300 K under the NVT ensemble to avoid abrupt structural perturbations. This was followed by pressure equilibration under the NPT ensemble to stabilize density and solvent distribution before the 100 ns production MD simulations were performed under periodic boundary conditions. A total of 40 complexes were simulated.


#### Stability Metrics
  RNA complexes are attributed to high RMSD values because of their fluctuating and dynamic structural nature. Short RNAs often display RMSD values above 3 nm, with these high values typically arising from structural motifs such as hairpin loops and long exterior tails. Unlike proteins, which form stable secondary structures such as α-helices and β-sheets, RNAs do not achieve the same level of compactness and therefore exhibit greater structural variability. As a result, RNA RMSD values fluctuate considerably, making it difficult to analyze and interpret their stability using conventional measures. To address this challenge, we introduce an algorithm that quantifies the degree of stabilization of the RMSD trajectory, denoted by τ. This degree of stabilization is defined as the total number of detected changepoints, representing significant alterations in the RMSD time series.

  Changepoint detection is carried out using the Pruned Exact Linear Time (PELT) algorithm, which identifies points in the time series where statistical properties change, thereby marking structural transitions within the RNA complex. In this method, we employ an l 2-based least-squares loss function to quantify the fit within each segment. The cost function minimized by PELT is defined as 1 C=∑i=1m(∑t=τi−1+1τi(xt−μi)2)+βm where x  t  are the RMSD values, μ i  is the mean of segment i, τ i  are the changepoints, m is the number of segments, and β is the penalty term. The penalty parameter β governs the trade-off between model fit and complexity, with larger values suppressing spurious segmentation. Once β is fixed, PELT achieves exact minimization in linear time. The degree of stabilization τ is then defined as the number of changepoints, where smaller values indicate greater stability. Detected changepoints are visualized directly on the RMSD trajectory, highlighting statistically significant transitions.

  It is important to emphasize that τ captures only the number of transitions and does not fully reflect the magnitude of the RMSD. High RMSD values may persist even when transitions are infrequent. To account for the magnitude of the RMSD, we introduce a complementary metric, the RMSD area, which quantifies the cumulative deviation over time. A higher mμ indicates unstable complexes with frequent or large deviations, whereas a lower mμ reflects stable complexes with minimal transitions and small deviations.


### MM-PBSA Binding Free Energy
Calculations
  Binding free energies for 40 aptamer–miRNA complexes were estimated using the MM-PBSA method implemented in GROMACS. Trajectory, topology, and index files obtained from the MD simulations were used for the calculations. A dielectric constant of 100 was applied to account for the highly charged RNA backbone and conformational fluctuations. Energies were averaged over the final 5000 frames of the simulations to ensure equilibrium sampling. The calculations included vacuum potential energy, polar solvation energy, and nonpolar solvation energy, using SASA and SAV models, with additional comparisons to WCA. Statistical analysis was performed to compare binding affinities and stability across all complexes.


## Results and Discussion

### RNA–RNA
Interaction Predictions
  This RNA–RNA interaction study was performed with designed aptamers and against 13 oncogenic miRNAs, which included miR-10b and its matures arms. The interactions were determined using the IntaRNA algorithm. In the T-SELEX workflow, the interaction energies between aptamers and various oncogenic miRNAs were analyzed and visualized using a heatmap (Figure  ). In this context, darker colors represent more negative interaction energies, indicating stronger and more thermodynamically favorable RNA–RNA binding. The heatmap reveals considerable variation in aptamer performance across different targets, reflecting the unique nature of each interaction. Notably, hsa-miR-25-5p consistently shows the most favorable interaction profile, suggesting strong hybridization and structural complementarity with the aptamer sequences. This is visually represented by darker shades on the heatmap, while weaker interactions (e.g., yellow regions) suggest reduced binding stability, likely due to poor base pairing or disrupted secondary structures. To better rank the targets, average interaction energies were computed for each target across all aptamers (Supporting Information Table S1). The strongest average interaction was observed with hsa-miR-25-5p (−5.013 kcal/mol), followed closely by hsa-miR-122-5p (−4.979 kcal/mol) and hsa-miR-155-5p (−4.407 kcal/mol), identifying these as top candidates for further validation in terms of 3d interactions. Interestingly, miRNAs with 5p arms generally exhibited more negative (stronger) interaction energies compared to their 3p counterparts, despite partial sequence complementarity. This trend suggests that 5p mature miRNAs may possess structural or sequence features that enhance aptamer binding affinity. Detailed interaction energies and position-wise minimum energy heatmaps for the top-ranked aptamers against pre-miR-10b, miR-10b-3p, and miR-10b-5p are provided in the Supporting Information (Figures S2 and S3).

  To explore potential relationships between aptamer interaction patterns across different miRNA targets, a clustered correlation heatmap was generated (Figure  ). This analysis aimed to determine whether certain targets exhibit similar interaction energy profiles, suggesting shared binding characteristics among aptamers. However, the heatmap revealed no strong positive or negative correlations between the miRNA targets, with most correlation coefficients falling below 0.5 and none exceeding 0.7. This lack of significant correlation indicates that the aptamers from the BRA-generated library bind uniquely to each target, displaying no consistent interaction trend across different miRNAs. Notably, even miRNA pairs from the same precursor such as 5p and 3p arms did not show strong correlation, underscoring that aptamers may recognize structural and sequence features specific to each arm rather than the shared precursor sequence. Principal component analysis (PCA) and K-means clustering of aptamers based on interaction energies with multiple oncogenic miRNAs are provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S1). This finding supports the conclusion that aptamer-target interactions in this data set are highly specific and individualized.


### Virtual
Screening
  The HDOCK score is a software-dependent scoring function that estimates the favorability of a predicted binding pose based on structural complementarity, intermolecular interactions, and geometric fitting between the aptamer and the target RNA. Therefore, more negative docking scores indicate relatively more favorable predicted binding conformations, but they do not represent absolute quantitative affinity measurements virtual screening of RNA aptamers against RNA targets presents a unique challenge due to the absence of defined active sites and the complex folding nature of RNA molecules. In this study, all aptamers were docked using HDOCK against the miR-10b precursor and its mature 5p and 3p arms to evaluate their binding behaviors (Figure  ). Docking scores from the first model of each aptamer reveal a consistent vertical sigmoid-like trend across all three targets.

  Interestingly, the docking results show that aptamers bind more strongly to the mature miRNA arms, particularly the 3p arm, followed by the 5p arm, with the precursor miRNA demonstrating the weakest binding. This finding contrasts with previous interaction energy studies using IntaRNA, which showed that the 5p arm bound more strongly than the 3p arm. The discrepancy may stem from the fact that interaction energy predictions do not account for RNA 3D conformation, whereas docking inherently considers spatial structure. For example, the top-scoring aptamer achieved a docking score of approximately −550 against miR-10b_3p and approximately −500 against miR-10b_5p. Despite sequence similarity between 3p and 5p, their differing 3D structures, shaped by base pairing and loop arrangements, result in significant variations in aptamer interaction.

  The molecular docking results for pre-miR-10b and its mature 5p and 3p arms, ranked by the docking scores of the first model, are presented in Table  . For miR-10b-3p, aptamer557 emerged as the top candidate with a docking score of −545.96, significantly outperforming the next best aptamer, aptamer274, which scored −514.52. This large score gap suggests that aptamer557 may possess a particularly favorable structural conformation for binding. Other top-performing aptamers include aptamer279, 128, and 734. Interestingly, fitness scores do not always align with docking ranks. For instance, aptamer734 has a higher fitness quality score than aptamer274 despite a lower first-model docking score, indicating that the overall structural reliability of aptamer734 across multiple models may be superior. Notably, all the top aptamers exhibited high confidence scores (above 0.99), reflecting strong reliability in the predicted interactions and docking poses.

  A similar trend was observed in miR-10b-5p, where aptamer899 ranked highest with a docking score of −482.55, followed by aptamers 536, 413, 332, and 278. Although aptamer536 had the second-best docking score, its fitness quality was significantly lower than aptamers899 and 413, reinforcing the notion that docking score alone may not fully reflect the robustness of aptamer-target binding. Aptamers 899 and 413, however, had similar fitness quality scores, indicating consistent model behavior. Confidence scores for the 5p arm were slightly lower than those observed for the 3p arm, which corresponds with the higher (less negative) docking scores, since confidence scores are derived from docking score metrics. For the precursor miRNA, the top-ranked aptamers were 895, 465, and 1085. While Table   provides a summary of key results, the full data set, including additional aptamers and performance metrics, is available (https://github.com/KPMOKGOPA/MSc_supplementary_data/tree/main/Docking_results). Postdocking calculations, including fitness quality and Z-score analysis, were performed to evaluate the consistency of the first docking model relative to the full set of generated poses, and these results are presented in the Supporting Information (Figure S7).

  Interaction bonds, particularly hydrogen bonds and pi–pi stacking, play a vital role in stabilizing RNA–RNA complexes and influencing their structural integrity. In this study, we analyzed these interactions within the top two ranked aptamers for each miRNA target across five docking models using a custom Perl script that was ran within Discovery studio, and the results are summarized in Figure  . For clarity, the figure is organized by target rows (miR-10b-3p, miR-10b-5p, and pre-miR-10b) and interaction-type columns representing hydrogen bond counts at 2.5 Å, hydrogen bond counts at 3.0 Å, and π–π stacking interactions. Hydrogen bonds were assessed using two distance thresholds of 2.5 Å and an extended 3.0 Å between the target (chain A) and aptamer (chain B), allowing a more inclusive detection of bonding events. The analysis revealed that first-ranked aptamers consistently formed more hydrogen bonds than their second-ranked counterparts, indicating stronger potential interactions, with the exception of miR-10b-5p, where aptamers 899 and 536 exhibited an equal number of hydrogen bonds at the 2.5 Å threshold for model 5. While these results provide a useful initial insight into binding potential, it is important to note that they are based on static docking models and do not account for physiological factors like solvents and ion concentrations; thus, molecular dynamics simulations are necessary for a more accurate assessment of bond stability. In contrast, pi–pi stacking interactions were rare across most aptamer–miRNA complexes, likely due to structural constraints and unfavorable orientations of folded RNA molecules. The highest observed pi–pi interactions five in total occurred in model 2 of aptamer895 against the precursor miRNA, suggesting a unique structural alignment that may enhance complex stability in this specific case.

  The interaction analysis suggests that binding is not driven exclusively by classical Watson–Crick antisense pairing, but rather by a combination of partial base pairing and structure-dependent aptamer recognition. Since both the aptamer and the target miRNA adopt folded secondary and tertiary structures prior to docking, the interaction interface is constrained by loops, stems, bulges, and terminal regions rather than extended fully complementary linear strands. Examination of the docking interaction tables (Tables S2–S4) shows that several stable contacts occur between loop regions, terminal residues, and noncanonical interaction sites, with hydrogen bond distances generally ranging between approximately 1.6 Å and 3.0 Å, indicating strong and structurally relevant intermolecular interactions. For example, aptamer899 interactions with miR-10b-5p involve terminal and loop-region contacts such as A5–U19, C6–G18, U7–G17, C8–G16, and G4–U20, indicating localized complementary recognition rather than long consecutive Watson–Crick base-paired stretches. These observations suggest that both partial Watson–Crick pairing and three-dimensional structural recognition contribute to complex stability rather than purely antisense-mediated hybridization.

  The docking poses of the top two aptamers against miR-10b-3p and miR-10b-5p were visualized across ten models each, as shown in Figure   (with additional poses provided in the Supporting Information). Specifically, Figure   highlights the diverse binding conformations of aptamer899 with miR-10b-5p, demonstrating its structural flexibility and multiple binding possibilities in the absence of a defined active site, a common characteristic of RNA–RNA interactions. Each model (M1–M10) is represented by a distinct color, and while the poses vary, consistent patterns emerge. Aptamer899 frequently binds through its stem region in models 1, 3, 4, 7, 8, and 9, indicating a recurring interaction mode that suggests a preference for the target’s groove region. In contrast, models 2, 5, 6, and 10 show binding via the aptamer’s hairpin loop, pointing to an alternative but equally favorable binding strategy. Although the individual poses are not identical, the repeated engagement of the stem and loop regions implies these are the most structurally favorable domains for aptamer899 binding to miR-10b-5p. The representative other docking poses of the top-ranked aptamer–miRNA complexes, including aptamer899–miR-10b-5p, aptamer536–miR-10b-5p, and aptamer577–miR-10b-3p, can be found in the Supporting Information (Figures S4–S6), where models M1–M10 illustrate the major binding conformations generated during docking analysis.


### QM Calculations
  The total energy and HOMO–LUMO (H–L) gap analyses for the aptamer–miR-10b-3p and aptamer–miR-10b-5p complexes across five models are presented in Figure  , with detailed values available in the Supporting Information. Figure   visually illustrates the general energy trends across models. For miR-10b-3p complexes, aptamer577 consistently exhibits the lowest total energy values, especially in model 2 (−3007.37 Eh), indicating higher relative stability compared to other aptamers. Similarly, in miR-10b-5p complexes, aptamer413 shows notably low total energies across models, with model 1 reaching −3014.18 Eh. These low energy values suggest strong binding potential and stable complex formation. The HOMO–LUMO energy gap analysis shows variability across models and aptamers, with no uniform pattern. Generally, model 1 tends to have smaller H–L gaps, which may suggest higher reactivity or lower kinetic stability, while subsequent models (particularly models 3 and 4) often show increased gaps before declining again by model 5. However, this trend is not consistent across all aptamers. For example, aptamer577 shows a steady increase in the H–L gap from model 1 to model 5, whereas aptamer279 shows irregular fluctuations. In the miR-10b-5p group, no definitive trend is observed either some models like model 1 of aptamer278 and model 3 of aptamer899 show minimal H–L gaps, but this does not directly correlate with total energy-based stability. These findings suggest that the electronic properties of aptamer–miRNA complexes are highly conformation-dependent, varying significantly between docking models and aptamer-target pairs (the exact values H–L gaps and total energies can be found Supporting Information Tables S5–S8).


### Molecular
Dynamics

#### RMSD for Complexes
  The RMSD analysis in Figure   demonstrates that all aptamer–miR-10b-5p complexes exhibit RMSD below 3 nm throughout the simulations, reflecting overall structural integrity despite inherent RNA flexibility. For instance, model 1 of aptamer899–miR-10b-5p fluctuates until approximately 80 ns but remains below 3 nm, indicating relative stability with conformational transitions typical of RNA dynamics. Among the tested complexes, aptamer899–miR-10b-5p stabilizes more consistently than others, though some models show delayed equilibration beyond 20 ns. By contrast, aptamer331–miR-10b-5p maintains RMSD values under 2 nm with limited fluctuations, suggesting greater stability and reduced structural variability. These findings highlight the capability of HDock to generate RNA–RNA complexes that equilibrate within physiologically relevant ranges, even though further refinement of its scoring function may improve docking accuracy.

  To complement the RMSD fluctuation analysis, the degree of stabilization (τ), as defined earlier, was calculated. The highest τ values were observed in model 4 of aptamer536–miR-10b-5p (τ = 19) and model 1 of aptamer413–miR-10b-5p (τ = 15), reflecting extensive structural transitions and reduced stability. In contrast, model 1 of aptamer331–miR-10b-5p and model 2 of aptamer899–miR-10b-5p displayed the lowest τ (τ = 2), confirming their stability over time. Overall, aptamer331–miR-10b-5p complexes consistently exhibited the most stable dynamics, while aptamer536– and aptamer413–miR-10b-5p complexes showed higher variability. Taken together, the RMSD and τ analyses indicate that aptamer331 forms the most structurally stable complex with miR-10b-5p, whereas aptamer536 and aptamer413 undergo larger conformational transitions during simulations.

  In Figure   where the target is miR-10b-3p against the five models of best aptamers (aptamer557, 274, 734, and 279), it is evident that all complexes seem to stabilize below 2 nm except for model 5 of aptamer274–miR-10b-3p and model 1 of aptamer734–miR-10b-3p, with degrees of stabilization (τ) of 18 and 26, respectively. This suggests that these complexes are generally stable; however, model 5 of aptamer274–miR-10b-3p and model 1 of aptamer734–miR-10b-3p are notable exceptions. The RMSD of model 5 of aptamer274–miR-10b-3p shows chaotic behavior and does not stabilize, eventually reaching an RMSD of 6 nm. This indicates that the complex is undergoing major structural transitions and possibly having either miR-10b-3p or aptamer274 unfolding, or even worse, both of them unfolding. Of course, it would be an overstatement to conclude that the complex is unfolding based solely on RMSD (and this appears to be the case visually), but more analyses are needed to quantify and confirm this hypothesis. Overall, the aptamer557 models have degrees of stabilization (τ) below 4, suggesting that the 557 models, which were ranked top 1 based on molecular docking, have the most stable model complexes compared to the rest.


#### Stability Metric Analysis
  The stability metric plots in Figure   provide a comparative overview of aptamer stability across both miR-10b-5p and miR-10b-3p arms. In Figure  , the total error was obtained by combining the uncertainty arising from fluctuations in the RMSD data with the numerical uncertainty introduced during the Simpson’s rule integration process, providing an overall estimate of the reliability of the calculated stability metric. For the miR-10b-5p complexes, aptamer331 emerges as the most stable, with consistently low stability metric (mμ), as defined earlier, values ranging between 1 and 3 across its models, reflecting minimal RMSD fluctuations and reliable stability. Aptamer899 models also displays relatively low μ values (2.5–4.5), suggesting stable interactions, though less consistent than aptamer331 models. By contrast, aptamer413 and aptamer536 models exhibit broader mμ ranges (3–7.5 and 2.6–8.7, respectively), highlighting greater structural variability and reduced stability. For the miR-10b-3p complexes, aptamer279 is identified as the most stable, with model 2 particularly showing the lowest mμ value of 1, indicative of optimal structural stability. Aptamer577 models demonstrate moderate stability with mμ values from 1.5 to 2, while aptamer734 shows notably higher mμ values (3–9), reflecting substantial instability compared to the others. Collectively, these results suggest that aptamer331 is the most reliable candidate against miR-10b-5p, while aptamer279, especially model 2, shows best stability against miR-10b-3p. The absence of error in the RMSD area term obtained in the calculation of mμ further supports the robustness of the stability metric analysis. The detailed stability metric calculations, including RMSD area, degree of stabilization (τ), and composite stability metric (mμ) for all five models of the selected aptamer–miRNA complexes against miR-10b-5p and miR-10b-3p, are provided in the Supporting Information (Tables S9 and S10).


#### PCA for Complexes
  Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to characterize the dominant motions of RNA-aptamer complexes over the course of the simulations, with frames divided into five 20 ns intervals. The first two eigenvectors (PC1 and PC2) capture the largest structural fluctuations, allowing visualization of key conformational changes. By analyzing the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), large-scale motions such as opening, closing, rotational shifts, and flexible domain movements could be identified, providing deeper insight into the dynamic behavior of the complexes beyond conventional RMSD analysis. The PCAs of miR-10b-5p and miR-10b-3p arms-aptamer complexes are reported in Figure  . In the miR-10b-5p complexes, some models (e.g., 536-m1, 413-m1/m3, 331-m2) display clockwise or regular motions, while others (899-m3/m4, 536-m3, 331-m4) remain more compact with limited excursions. Similarly, in the miR-10b-3p complexes, models such as 557-m3, 734-m2, and 279-m2 show directional motions, whereas 557-m5, 274-m1/m2, and 279-m3 retain constrained trajectories. Certain systems, such as 274-m5, exhibit more erratic or chaotic motion, indicating structural instability or high flexibility. Overall, the PCA plots reveal distinct dynamic behaviors across aptamer-RNA complexes, with RNA motions spanning a broader PC1/PC2 range (−50 to 50) than typical protein systems, consistent with RMSD and Rg analyses and highlighting the inherently flexible nature of RNA-aptamer interactions.

  The molecular dynamics hydrogen bond analysis further supports the PCAs and the possibility of partial Watson–Crick stabilization during simulation. Several top-ranked aptamer–miRNA complexes maintained persistent or increasing hydrogen bond populations over the 100 ns trajectories, suggesting that stable intermolecular contacts were preserved beyond the initial docking poses. This behavior indicates that localized Watson–Crick-type interactions, particularly within loop and terminal regions, may become further stabilized during MD while still coexisting with structure-dependent aptamer recognition. These hydrogen bond persistence patterns can be observed in Figures S12 and S13 in the Supporting Information.


### MM-PBSA
  MM-PBSA binding energies were used as comparative indicators of relative binding stability among the selected aptamer–miRNA complexes rather than direct measurements of experimental affinity. These calculated energies reflect the balance of van der Waals, electrostatic, polar solvation, and nonpolar solvation contributions derived from molecular dynamics trajectories. While more negative MM-PBSA values suggest thermodynamically favorable interactions and greater structural stability, it is important to note that these values should be interpreted as relative computational estimates and not as direct substitutes for experimental K d values, which require biochemical validation. The MM-PBSA analysis of aptamer–miR-10b-5p complexes (Figure  ) highlights substantial variation in binding energies across different models. Aptamer331 models show the strongest interactions, with model 1 and model 5 reaching −660.282 kJ/mol and −544.148 kJ/mol, respectively, indicating very stable binding. This may be due to the fact that the aptamer331 models 1 and 5 complexes showed to have closely related binding orientations at the RNA–RNA interaction interface, however, not structurally identical. The conserved interacting regions allowed both conformations to maintain favorable van der Waals and electrostatic interactions, resulting in comparable thermodynamic stability. This observation was further supported by molecular dynamics analyses, which showed that both models displayed stable RMSD behavior, persistent hydrogen bonding, and low composite stability metric (mμ) values, confirming that similar binding energies arise from related stable binding poses rather than structural duplication.

  Aptamer899 and aptamer536 display mixed profiles, with some models achieving moderately negative binding energies (−364.93 to −476.642 kJ/mol) while others are close to zero or slightly positive, reflecting weaker or less favorable interactions. Aptamer413 exhibits variable binding, with model 2 showing a positive energy (20.329 kJ/mol), suggesting poor binding. Overall, these results indicate that strong and stable aptamer-RNA interactions depend on the specific model conformation, while higher positive electrostatic energies in some systems suggest repulsive interactions that may hinder complex formation.

  For miR-10b-3p complexes (Figure  ), aptamer274-m1 exhibits the most negative binding energy at −701.202 kJ/mol, indicating exceptionally strong and stable interactions. Aptamer734-m2 also shows favorable binding (−460.337 kJ/mol), while other models within each aptamer series display varying affinities due to structural and conformational differences. Aptamer577-m2 (−604.52 kJ/mol) and aptamer279 models demonstrate strong but slightly less favorable binding. The contributions from van der Waals, electrostatic, polar solvation, and SASA energies collectively shape the overall binding profile. These results emphasize that model-specific conformations critically influence aptamer binding strength, with certain aptamers forming highly stable complexes suitable for further experimental validation.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we applied a comprehensive T-SELEX-guided computational workflow to design and evaluate RNA aptamer candidates targeting oncogenic miR-10b and its mature 5p and 3p arms. The workflow integrated large-scale virtual screening, molecular docking, quantum mechanical calculations, molecular dynamics simulations, and MM-PBSA binding free energy analysis to identify structurally stable and energetically favorable RNA–RNA complexes. Heatmap analysis of interaction energies revealed target-specific binding profiles, with hsa-miR-25-5p, miR-122-5p, and miR-155-5p showing the strongest overall affinities. Docking and hydrogen bond analyses highlighted the structural flexibility of the designed aptamers and identified key binding conformations responsible for favorable interactions. Furthermore, an interesting finding was that even though both the aptamer and the target miRNA adopted folded secondary and tertiary structures, partial Watson–Crick interactions were still observed, primarily arising from accessible nonself-interacting bases located in loop regions and terminal residues. This suggests that binding occurs through a hybrid mechanism involving both localized complementary base pairing and structure-dependent three-dimensional recognition, rather than purely classical antisense hybridization.

  QM calculations confirmed model-dependent variations in total energies and HOMO–LUMO gaps, demonstrating the importance of conformation in determining electronic stability and binding behavior. Molecular dynamics analyses, including RMSD, RMSF, PCA, hydrogen bond persistence, and the newly introduced composite stability metric (mμ), provided a quantitative framework for evaluating conformational stability over time. These analyses showed that aptamer331 and aptamer274 consistently formed the most stable complexes with miR-10b-5p and miR-10b-3p, respectively. The agreement between hydrogen bonding dynamics and MM-PBSA binding energies further supported the reliability of these candidates. Importantly, the stability metric framework introduced in this study provides a robust post-MD analytical tool for RNA–RNA interaction studies, particularly for highly flexible systems where conventional RMSD interpretation alone may be insufficient. This approach may be extended to other RNA-targeting therapeutic studies and computational aptamer discovery pipelines.

  Although the present work provides strong computational evidence for promising candidate aptamers, the conclusions remain limited to in silico predictions and relative comparisons within the computational framework. Experimental validation through biochemical binding assays, structural characterization, and functional inhibition studies will be necessary to confirm the predicted interactions and therapeutic potential of these candidates. Future studies should also clarify whether binding is primarily driven by structure-dependent aptamer-like recognition or by partial antisense-mediated Watson–Crick hybridization. In addition to candidate identification, the study introduces a multiscale computational enrichment framework integrating sequence generation, RNA folding, docking, quantum mechanical calculations, molecular dynamics simulations, and a newly proposed composite stability metric for evaluating highly flexible RNA–RNA systems. The robustness and modular design of this workflow make it broadly applicable to other RNA-targeting therapeutic and aptamer discovery studies.
