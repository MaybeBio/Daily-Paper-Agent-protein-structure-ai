# A dual gating mechanism controls target‐strand cleavage in Cas12j: Implications for engineering efficient nickases


## Abstract
  The rapid expansion of CRISPR technologies has unveiled a diverse repertoire of RNA‐guided endonucleases, among them the compact Cas12j, which has emerged as a promising genome‐editing tool. Cas12j cleaves the two DNA strands sequentially; however, the molecular mechanism that regulates this cleavage remains incompletely understood. Here, we combine extensive all‐atom molecular dynamics simulations with well‐tempered metadynamics, totaling approximately 175 μs of cumulative sampling, to investigate how target‐strand accessibility to the catalytic site is controlled. Our results are in agreement with previous experimental observations, and furthermore reveal new mechanistic details that are difficult to access experimentally, namely a coordinated dual‐barrier mechanism governing target‐strand accessibility that can be fine‐tuned through targeted mutations in the α7‐helix and/or the REC2 loop. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for tuning Cas12j activity along the nuclease‐to‐nickase spectrum, supporting the rational engineering of genome‐editing tools with controlled target‐strand cleavage kinetics, and offering a path toward applications that bypass dependence on Non‐Homologous End Joining (NHEJ) or Homology‐Directed Repair (HDR).


## INTRODUCTION
  Genome editing enables targeted modification of DNA sequences (Khalil, 2020; Li et al., 2020). There are many methods for modifying the genome. One of the most powerful being the use of RNA‐guided endonucleases (Gaj et al., 2016; Jacinto et al., 2020). Over a decade ago, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier demonstrated that a ribonucleoprotein complex from the bacterial adaptive immune system could be repurposed for precise genome editing (Doudna & Charpentier, 2014; Jinek et al., 2012). These systems typically induce DNA double‐strand breaks, repaired by endogenous cellular pathways through either error‐prone Non‐Homologous End Joining (NHEJ)—yielding insertions or deletions—or precise Homology‐Directed Repair (HDR), depending on the availability of a repair template (Mengstie & Zawdie, 2021). However, repair outcomes via NHEJ or HDR are often difficult to control (Chen et al., 2019; Shen et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2020; Ye et al., 2018). An attractive alternative is the generation of single‐strand DNA break. Restricting cleavage to the non‐target‐strand produces a nick rather than a double‐strand break, enabling more controlled genome editing outcomes (Davis & Maizels, 2011). This strategy offers several advantages, including reduced off‐target effects, minimal induction of error‐prone NHEJ‐associated insertions and deletions, lower risk of chromosomal rearrangements, and an increased proportion of precise HDR‐mediated edits relative to mutagenic repair outcomes (Chen et al., 2019; Jeong et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Rodgers & Mcvey, 2016; Yang et al., 2020). Here, we provide a mechanistic framework for tuning nickase‐like activity in a compact RNA‐guided endonuclease that natively induces double‐strand breaks.

  The first genome‐editing applications were based on the ribonucleoprotein complex named CRISPR‐Cas9 which consists of a protein with domains that help in recognition and catalysis (Jiang & Doudna, 2017). It uses a guide RNA to recognize a specific DNA sequence adjacent to a short motif called Protospacer Adjacent Motif (PAM). The endonuclease unwinds the DNA double‐strand, which consists of the non‐target‐strand (nts) and the target‐strand (ts), and the guide RNA binds to the ts through sequence complementarity to form an RNA–DNA hybrid (R‐loop). Once the R‐loop is formed, the Cas9 conformation changes to the cleavage state, in which its catalytic domains cleave both the nts and the ts upstream of the PAM sequence by a Mg2+ assisted mechanism (Dagdas et al., 2017; Huai et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2016; Pacesa et al., 2022; Palermo et al., 2016; Sternberg et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2019).

  It was subsequently demonstrated that this mechanism could be adapted for genome editing in human cells (Kim et al., 2013; Mali et al., 2013). This discovery opens an avenue for the modification of the genome to potentially silence disease‐causing genes. The initial success of Cas9 catalyzed intensive research efforts that identified numerous alternative systems and related endonucleases (Koonin et al., 2023; Zhang, 2019). A prominent family emerging from these investigations is Cas12, which constitutes a highly diverse collection of RNA‐guided endonucleases, with remarkable structural and functional variability. This diversity reflects multiple independent evolutionary events that shaped the Cas12 family (Shmakov et al., 2017; Yan et al., 2019) Cas12 proteins, in general adopt a more compact size, compared to Cas9, which is very important for their delivery into human cells, particularly via adeno‐associated virus vectors, which impose stringent packaging constraints of roughly 4700 base pairs (Liu et al., 2023; Sioson et al., 2021; Søndergaard et al., 2020). Mechanistically, Cas12 proteins employ PAM recognition and R‐loop formation strategies analogous to those of Cas9, but in contrast to Cas9, DNA cleavage by Cas12 proteins occurs through a stepwise process. This mechanism involves pronounced conformational rearrangements that sequentially position the active site for cleavage of the nts, followed by rearrangement for the ts‐cleavage (Aldag et al., 2021; Swarts, 2019; Yamano et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019). Recent work on Cas12a has proposed the molecular basis of this second step using molecular dynamics simulations, validated by in vitro cleavage assays, and identified an α‐helical lid within the RUVC domain that anchors the crRNA:ts duplex via electrostatic contacts and guides its traversal toward the active site, a process assisted by inward‐bending motions of the REC2 and NUC domains (Saha et al., 2024). Complementary cryo‐EM structures of sequential R‐loop and cleavage intermediates further showed that the RUVC active site must conformationally reset between cleavage of the two strands (Strohkendl et al., 2024). More broadly, during target recognition, hybridization between the guide RNA and the ts, displaces nts, which becomes exposed as single‐stranded DNA within the R‐loop structure. Because the active site preferentially accommodates single‐stranded substrates, the displaced nts is readily positioned into the active site and cleaved first. In contrast, cleavage of the ts requires a substantial conformational rearrangement that positions the ts into the same catalytic core (Omura et al., 2025). This second cleavage event should therefore represent a kinetically and structurally regulated step in the reaction pathway that could potentially be exploited to engineer better variants.

  Among the Cas12 family, Cas12j is one of the most compact RNA‐guided endonucleases, and it adopts a bilobed architecture composed of a recognition (REC) lobe and a nuclease (NUC) lobe (Pausch et al., 2020). The recognition lobe, including the PI, OBD, REC1, and REC2 domains, mediates target DNA recognition by guide RNA complementarity, whereas the nuclease lobe comprising the RUVC and ZR domains harbors the catalytic machinery responsible for DNA cleavage (Figure 1a,b). The experimental work by Pausch et al. revealed that a key regulatory element inhibiting the movement of the ts into the active site is the α7 helix of the REC1 domain, which sterically constrains the ts and, thereby, delays its access to the active site, allowing the nts‐cleavage (Pausch et al., 2021). Helix α7 consists of a positively charged central region and a negatively charged tip (Figure S1a). Displacement of α7 away from the ts relieves the steric barrier and enables ts‐cleavage, whereas movement of α7 toward the ts reinforces steric hindrance and inhibits ts‐cleavage. Furthermore, the study proposed that ts‐cleavage kinetics are relatively slow in the wild‐type (WT) enzyme (k = 0.10 min−1), consistent with the presence of a regulatory barrier. Alanine substitutions in the negatively charged tip of α7 (E159A, S160A, S164A, D167A, E168A) had little effect on ts‐cleavage kinetics, with a little decrease in the cleavage rate (k = 0.09 min−1), whereas mutations in the positively charged core (K146A, R150A, K153A, R157A) increased the ts‐cleavage rate (k = 0.17 min−1) (Pausch et al., 2021). Strikingly, replacement of the α7 tip with a short glycine–serine linker (Δ155–176 (GSSG)) resulted in a substantial acceleration of ts‐cleavage (k = 1.78 min−1), confirming that α7 is part of a mechanism that fine‐tunes cleavage kinetics (Pausch et al., 2021). The experimental study suggested also a potential regulatory role for a loop in the REC2 domain connecting helices α17 and α18 (the REC2 loop) (Figure S1b), however, its mechanistic contribution was not clearly defined. Structurally, this loop has been resolved close to the ts and contains five lysine residues (K522, K523, K526, K527, K528), whose positively charged side chains are well positioned to interact electrostatically with the negatively charged oxygen atoms of the phosphate backbone of the ts nucleotides (Figure 1c). It has been proposed that such interactions could stabilize the ts and constrain its transition into the active site. This suggests that the REC2 loop may function in concert with the α7 helix, forming a coupled steric and electrostatic regulatory network that governs ts accessibility and therefore, ts‐cleavage kinetics. However, several mechanistic questions remain. The conformational motions by which α7 constrains the ts are not yet defined. Although the REC2 loop has been proposed to restrict the ts above the active site, its functional role and effect on cleavage kinetics have not been fully explored. It is also unclear whether α7 acts independently or in coordination with elements such as the REC2 loop to regulate access of the ts to the active site. Moreover, it remains unclear which interaction most strongly affects ts‐cleavage kinetics. Finally, the geometric requirements of the ts for productive cleavage have not been systematically explored. While access of the ts to the catalytic site is considered a prerequisite for cleavage, it remains unknown which α7‐REC2 conformations are catalytically competent. Productive cleavage is expected to require an antiparallel alignment of the ts and nts, enabling proper positioning of the scissile phosphate within the catalytic core, whereas parallel or perpendicular arrangements are likely to be incompatible with catalysis (Paul & Montoya, 2020; Stella et al., 2018; Swarts et al., 2017). Whether such geometric constraints contribute to the regulation of ts‐cleavage remains an open question.

  Herein, we explore such open questions and provide a comprehensive mechanistic description of the steric and electrostatic regulation governing ts‐cleavage in Cas12j. First, we explore the conformational changes of the α7 helix and how its displacement enables the ts to enter the active site. We identify a distance threshold between α7 helix and REC2 loop, beyond which the ts is released, and below which it remains sterically constrained. We determine REC2 loop as an integral component of the regulatory mechanism that controls ts access to the active site and exploit such information for engineering Cas12j variants. Targeted mutations were introduced at the REC2 loop: lysines were substituted either (i) by alanines to abolish both positive charge and hydrogen‐bonding capacity, (ii) by arginines to introduce longer side chains and increased hydrogen‐bond donor potential, or (iii) by glutamines as an intermediate case featuring polar but uncharged side chains of shorter length. The dynamics of hydrogen‐bond formation therein were analyzed for the wild‐type and mutants to identify combinations of mutations within the α7 helix and REC2 loop that either hinder or facilitate ts displacement. Interestingly, stronger electrostatic interactions progressively bias the ts toward non‐productive geometries, indicating that electrostatics influence not only ts accessibility but also its catalytic alignment. This strategy enables the construction of a complete energetic and mechanistic framework describing how steric and electrostatic interactions regulate gating of the ts in Cas12j. We propose variants with either low energy barriers for ts‐cleavage that may promote double‐strand break formation and engagement of NHEJ or HDR pathways (Liao et al., 2024; Rodgers & Mcvey, 2016; Schubert et al., 2021) or with high or prohibitive energy barriers, effectively suppressing ts‐cleavage and converting Cas12j into an nts‐nickase.


## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

### Models and sampling of Cas12j dynamics
  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to characterize the conformational space of Cas12j and elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying ts‐cleavage. Initial coordinates were obtained from the most complete available cryo‐EM structure of the Cas12j–sgRNA–dsDNA complex in the nts‐cleavage state (PDB ID: 7LYT). Missing residues were modeled as described in Section 4. The wild‐type (WT) system and three additional variants were first constructed by introducing charge‐altering mutations exclusively in the REC2 loop (while keeping the α7 helix unchanged). These include REC2‐A (Lys → Ala), REC2‐R (Lys → Arg), and REC2‐Q (Lys → Gln). For each of these systems, 15 independent classical MD trajectories of 1 μs each were generated. In addition, three groups of mutants were designed to probe the combined effects of α7 helix and REC2 loop perturbations. Both the positively charged core and negatively charged tip of the α7 helix were mutated (K146A, R150A, K153A, R157A and E159A, S160A, S164A, D167A, E168A respectively), yielding the α7PN variant, whereas in a combination of α7‐REC2 variants, we produced α7PN‐REC2‐A, α7PN‐REC2‐R, and α7PN‐REC2‐Q. Furthermore, we introduced variants, where the negatively charged tip of α7 was mutated, resulting in α7N and the α7N‐REC2‐A, α7N‐REC2‐R, α7N‐REC2‐Q combinations. Finally, we introduced variants with only the positively charged core of α7 mutated, generating the α7P variant and the following combinations: α7P‐REC2‐A, α7P‐REC2‐R, and α7P‐REC2‐Q, with additional controls (REC2‐D/E/Y/W). In all cases, REC2 loop mutations correspond to either Lys → Ala (−A), Lys → Arg (−R), or Lys → Gln (−Q) or Lys → Glu (−E), Lys → Asp (−D), Lys → Tyr (−Y), Lys → Trp (−W) substitutions, as defined above. For each α7 helix–REC2 loop combined variants, five independent 1‐μs classical MD trajectories were run. In total, this simulation strategy encompassed 20 Cas12j variants (16 main systems and four controls) and yielded an aggregate classical MD sampling time of 140 μs, providing a comprehensive dataset to probe the coupled roles of the α7 helix and REC2 loop in regulating ts gating into the active site. All systems were solvated in explicit water with 150 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2 and simulated at neutral pH, 310 K.


### The steric role of α7 helix and its coordinating movement with REC2 loop
  To investigate the movement of α7 helix to either constrain or release the ts, we first characterized the dominant collective motions of the protein using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The first five principal components (PCs) were selected for further analysis as they account for more than 40% of the total protein motion (see Figure S2 for the exact contribution of each vector). To locate the Cas12j regions that contribute most to these collective motions, we calculated the weighted‐average RMSF based on the first five PCAs (see Section 4). This analysis revealed two prominent peaks corresponding to the α7 helix (with the highest fluctuation observed for aa #165–170) and the REC2 loop. These results support a dual‐barrier model in which steric hindrance and electrostatic interactions act in concert to regulate ts accessibility in Cas12j. Cas12a is the only other Cas12 effector for which the molecular mechanism governing ts‐cleavage has been investigated in comparable detail. Combined mutagenesis, structural analysis, and molecular dynamics simulations showed that a gate in REC2 domain and a loop in NUC domain cooperatively regulate ts‐cleavage, while differences in REC2–NUC loop conformational dynamics correlate with cleavage efficiency among Cas12a orthologues (Newman et al., 2025). Other studies identified a RUVC lid guiding ts traversal and showed the active site resets between strand cleavages (Saha et al., 2024; Strohkendl et al., 2024). Together, these findings suggest that regulated ts gating may represent a broader mechanistic principle within the Cas12 family, although the structural elements implementing this regulation appear to be effector‐specific; that is, Cas12a has no α7 equivalent, so the parallel with the RuvC lid is functional rather than structural. Whether similar mechanisms exist in other Cas12 or Fanzor (Fz) effectors remains an important open question.

  In the WT system, the peak associated with the α7 helix is smaller compared to that of the REC2 loop, whereas in the α7PN, α7N, and α7P variants, the peak corresponding to the REC2 loop is higher. Additionally, smaller peaks are observed for aa #666–693, corresponding to the ZR domain, which is highly flexible as also reported in the experimental study, and in aa #470–515, corresponding to a region adjacent to the REC2 loop (Figures 1d and S3). These results indicate that such structural elements play a central role in the dominant large‐scale dynamics of the system.

  By further elaborating on the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), which together account for over 30% of the total motion (Figure S2), we observe that these modes capture the dominant large‐scale conformational rearrangements of the system, whereas the remaining components primarily describe smaller, local fluctuations. In both PC1 and PC2, the motions of the α7 helix are qualitatively similar, rather than representing distinct mechanistic modes. In particular, aa #165–170 of α7 helix—those directly interacting with the ts—consistently move away from the DNA, with a more pronounced displacement observed along PC1. Importantly, this motion does not correspond to a rigid‐body displacement of the entire helix, but instead arises from localized rotations around specific regions. Along PC1, these conformational changes are directly reflected in the structural transitions depicted in Figure 2a–d, indicating that this analysis primarily captures the dominant motion described by this principal component. Notably, the displacement of the α7 helix along PC1 is strongly coupled to the motion of the REC2 loop: as the helix moves away from the ts, the REC2 loop simultaneously shifts away, consistent with relief of the electrostatic constraint (Figure 2a–d). This coordinated motion provides direct structural support for the proposed dual‐barrier gating mechanism controlling ts access to the active site.

  In the WT system, the α7 helix exhibits two concurrent hinge‐like motions: one centered around the peptide bond between A163 and R164, immediately preceding aa #165–170, and a second located just upstream of the positively charged core (Figures 2a and S4). This dual‐hinge behavior enables substantial displacement of the helix and is accompanied by a pronounced movement of the REC2 loop, consistent with release of electrostatic constraints. In contrast, in the α7PN, α7N, and α7P variants, rotation occurs predominantly around the region preceding the positively charged core (Figure 2b–d). Among these, the α7PN variant shows reduced α7 helix displacement relative to α7N and α7P (RMSD along PC1 = 6.6 Å for WT, 13.4 Å for α7PN, 17.2 Å for α7N, and 29.1 Å for α7P; Table S1), whereas α7N exhibits almost negligible movement of the REC2 loop (RMSD along PC1 = 24.1 Å for WT, 15.5 Å for α7PN, 4.2 Å for α7N, and 12.2 Å for α7P; Table S2) (Figure 2c). In contrast, the α7P variant displays the largest α7 helix displacement, accompanied by a comparably pronounced shift of the REC2 loop (Figure 2d) (Tables S1 and S2). This non‐rigid, localized character of the helix displacement is reflected in the RMSD[aa #146–165]/RMSD[aa #125–145] ratio, which is higher in α7PN, α7N, and α7P (15.2, 13.8, and 13.7, respectively) than in WT (5.2) (Table S3), indicating that displacement in the variants is concentrated in the segment adjacent to the ts‐proximal aa #165–170 rather than distributed across the full α7 helix as in WT. For reference, the two concurrent hinge motions in the WT system correspond to angular displacements of 113.0° (PC1) and 38.5° (PC2) (Figure S4). This is in agreement with the experimental results where the α7Ν exerted almost half the ts‐cleavage rate compared to the α7P (0.09 compared to 0.17 min−1). Furthermore, hydrogen bond analysis and distance distributions between the REC2 loop and the ts confirm that the REC2 loop actively engages the ts through hydrogen bonding interactions, with the extent of this engagement being strongly dependent on the nature of the residues at the REC2 loop. Arginine substitutions form the most frequent and stable hydrogen bonds with the ts, followed by lysine, while glutamine substitutions maintain weaker polar contacts. Alanine substitutions, lacking hydrogen bond donor capacity, abolish these interactions entirely. Moreover, even mutations in α7 helix affect the hydrogen bonds formation, with α7P forming fewer hydrogen bonds compared to WT and α7N (Figures S5–S8). Finally, RMSD analysis between initial and final structures along PC1 further supports this non‐rigid behavior, indicating that the first half of the α7 helix (preceding the positively charged core) remains relatively stable, whereas the second half undergoes substantial displacement away from the ts, with an approximately 5.0‐fold increase in RMSD for WT and approximately 15.0‐fold for α7PN, α7N, and α7P (Table S1). The larger increase observed in the variants relative to WT is likely attributable to the dual‐hinge mechanism of the latter, whereby rotation around the A163–R164 peptide bond enables the helix to move away from the ts more efficiently, distributing the displacement across two pivot points rather than one. To further probe the electrostatic and hydrogen‐bonding requirements at the REC2 loop, we generated negatively charged (REC2‐D and REC2‐E) and aromatic hydrogen‐bond‐capable (REC2‐Y and REC2‐W) variants. Results for the REC2‐D, REC2‐E, REC2‐Y, and REC2‐W variants are presented in detail in Figures S9–S11. Briefly, REC2‐D and REC2‐E variants populated free energy basins shifted toward higher REC2 loop—ts nucleotides distances, consistent with the electrostatic repulsion between the anionic carboxylate side chains (D and E) and the negatively charged ts phosphates backbone. The longer side chain of the glutamate amplifies this repulsion relative to aspartate. We note that an in‐vivo mutant could express protonated (neutral) variants of REC2‐D, and REC2‐E, however our variants are used solely as negatively charged controls. In contrast, REC2‐Y and REC2‐W populate basins concentrated at lower REC2 loop—ts nucleotides distances (Figure S10c,d), indicating that both aromatic variants keep the REC2 loop in close proximity to the ts despite their limited or negligible hydrogen bonding, and suggesting that donor availability rather than REC2 loop positioning is the limiting factor for these two variants.

  In addition to the dominant motions, both PC1 and PC2 capture smaller yet consistent displacements within a secondary region of the REC2 domain (aa #470–515), suggesting that this segment also contributes to the overall conformational response. In all cases, according to the PC1 vector, this secondary REC2 region moves in a coordinated manner with the REC2 loop—either toward or away from it depending on the variant—indicating coupled internal rearrangements within the domain (Figure 2a–d).

  All conformations from the WT, α7PN, α7N, and α7P systems were projected onto the PC1–PC2 space and clustered using the HDBSCAN algorithm (see Section 4). This analysis revealed a dominant cluster encompassing the majority of sampled conformations, along with another cluster associated with the α7N mutant and two clusters unique to the α7P mutant (Figures 3a and S12). A linear fit of the dominant cluster population reveals an approximately linear relationship of the form PC2 = a·PC1 + b. Examination of conformations along this linear fit shows that decreasing PC1 values correspond to a nearly linear increase in the distance between the α7 helix (aa #165–170) and the REC2 loop (Figure 3a inset), indicating a strong coupling between this principal motion and the separation of these structural elements. This approximately linear relationship between PC1 and PC2 is consistently observed across all variants (Figure S13), indicating that the same dominant conformational behavior is preserved regardless of the mutations introduced. This is a critical observation: despite the diverse physicochemical nature of the mutations, the global conformational machinery of the protein remains intact, with mutations modulating the energetics of transitions rather than disrupting the overall fold. This suggests that the observed mutational effects modulate a shared underlying conformational landscape, rather than reflecting inadequate sampling (Figure S14), though PCA alone cannot establish the functional or kinetic significance of this behavior. Importantly, the combined analysis of PC1 and PC2 highlights a consistent structural relationship between these coupled motions: as the separation between the α7 helix and the REC2 loop increases, the secondary REC2 region (aa #470–515) moves toward the REC2 loop. Also, the ZR domain remains flexible, consistent with prior experimental observations (Figure 3b) (Pausch et al., 2021). Collectively, these PCA‐based analyses indicate that α7 helix displacement is tightly coupled to REC2 loop motion, providing structural, but not kinetic, support for the dual‐barrier gating hypothesis examined in the sections that follow.


### Two distinct kinetic pathways for ts release
  Although classical MD simulations provide detailed insights into protein dynamics, they inherently carry the risk of inadequate sampling of the conformational space. To characterize energetically the systems and further enhance sampling, to avoid that the system remained trapped in a local or global minimum on the free energy surface, we employed well‐tempered metadynamics (see Section 4). The free energy landscapes were constructed using two distance‐based collective variables (CVs) designed to capture the coupled steric and electrostatic gating mechanisms. CV1 was defined as the distance between the CoM of the Cα atoms of aa #165–170 in the α7 helix and the CoM of the Cα atoms of aa #522–528 in the REC2 loop. CV2 was defined as the distance between the CoM of the Cα atoms of aa #522–528 in the REC2 loop and the CoM of oxygen atoms of the phosphate groups of the nucleotides A and B of the ts (Figure 1c).

  The free energy landscapes obtained from metadynamics reveal a structured conformational space that can be systematically described in terms of blocked and released states (Figure 4). The blocked ensemble comprises three substates, B0, B1, and B2, which differ in the relative contributions of steric and electrostatic barriers. Among these, B0 represents the dominant blocked configuration (no access to the active site) and it occurs in the majority of sampled trajectories. In this state, the ts remains in close proximity to the REC2 loop, as reflected by short REC2 loop–ts nucleotide distances, indicating persistent electrostatic interactions. Notably, B0 is characterized by the absence (or near absence) of steric hindrance, while electrostatic trapping alone is sufficient to prevent ts access to the active site. In contrast, the less frequently populated B1 and B2 substates, which are presented in Figures S16–S18, correspond to configurations where electrostatic interactions still retain the ts, but steric effects vary. In B1, both steric and electrostatic barriers are simultaneously active, with short α7 helix–REC2 loop separations reinforcing the blocked configuration. In B2, the α7 helix–REC2 loop distance is significantly increased, relieving steric hindrance; however, electrostatic interactions alone remain sufficient to maintain the ts in a blocked position. Upon release from the blocked ensemble, the system transitions into three distinct released states. The first, R0, represents a marginally released configuration in which the REC2 loop–ts nucleotide distance exceeds the electrostatic interaction cutoff, while the α7 helix–REC2 loop separation also surpasses the steric hindrance threshold (see 1D projections in Figure S15). This state corresponds to the initial lifting of the electrostatic barrier and marks the onset of ts escape from the REC2 loop. The second released state, R1, is characterized by relatively small α7 helix–REC2 loop distances combined with large REC2 loop–ts nucleotide separations. In this configuration, the ts is displaced away from the REC2 loop, while the α7 helix remains in relatively close proximity. Notably, in the REC2‐A mutant, this state spans a broad range of REC2 loop–ts nucleotide distances, reflecting increased flexibility and reduced electrostatic stabilization (Figure 4b). The third released state, R2, corresponds to configurations in which both the α7–REC2 loop and REC2 loop–ts nucleotide distances are large, indicating complete release of the ts from both steric and electrostatic constraints. As in R1, the REC2‐A system exhibits extended sampling of REC2 loop–ts nucleotide separations within this state, consistent with a flattened free energy landscape and enhanced conformational freedom (Figure 4b). Importantly, not all states are universally accessible across systems. In the WT system (Figure 4a), all common conformational states (B0 and R0, R1, R2) are accessible, indicating a shallow free energy landscape that permits transitions between both blocked and released configurations. This observation suggests that these states are intrinsic to the native functional dynamics of the enzyme, representing the baseline conformational ensemble explored during the catalytic cycle. Consequently, any redistribution or selective stabilization of these states observed in mutant systems can be directly attributed to the introduced mutations, which perturb the underlying steric and electrostatic balance governing ts gating. For the REC2‐A mutant (Figure 4b), the conformational landscape is markedly different, with the system sampling exclusively the released states R1 and R2, and effectively lacking blocked configurations. Although shallow local maxima are present in the free energy surface—forming small barriers that separate regions corresponding to blocked‐like configurations—these barriers are associated with free energy costs significantly exceeding thermal fluctuations (ΔF‡ ≈ 3.0 kBT). As such, transitions into blocked states are not thermally accessible at 310 K, rendering these configurations practically inaccessible. This behavior is consistent with the removal of positive charge in the REC2 loop, which abolishes electrostatic trapping, and allows the system to freely explore released conformations. The REC2‐R variant (Figure 4c) exhibits a highly restricted conformational landscape, sampling predominantly the blocked (B0) and fully released state (R2), while the other released states (R0 and R1) are effectively absent. In particular, the region corresponding to R0 becomes energetically unfavorable in REC2‐R and is replaced by pronounced free energy barriers. This behavior reflects the enhanced electrostatic interactions introduced by arginine substitutions, which not only stabilize the blocked state but also reshape the energy landscape to favor either electrostatically trapped configurations or fully released states, while disfavoring intermediate transitions. The REC2‐Q variant presents an intermediate scenario (Figure 4d), in which the system predominantly samples the marginally released state (R0). While configurations at shorter REC2 loop–ts nucleotide distances are occasionally visited, the free energy surface lacks well‐defined, deep minima corresponding to blocked states, in contrast to the WT and REC2‐R systems. Instead, the landscape is characterized by a relatively shallow and broad basin centered around R0, indicating weakened electrostatic stabilization and a reduced ability to sustain distinct, long‐lived conformational states. The free energy landscapes for all systems are shown in Figures S16–S18.

  Collectively, our results indicate that arginine substitutions in REC2 loop alone (REC2‐R) stabilize electrostatically trapped states that are compatible with a nickase‐like configuration, thereby hindering ts release. Analysis of the energy barriers reveals a pronounced mutation‐dependent reshaping of the conformational landscape (Figure 5). In the WT system, transitions from the blocked state (B0) to released states can proceed through three distinct pathways (B0 → R0, B0 → R1, and B0 → R2). Among these, the B0 → R0 transition exhibits the lowest energy barrier (relative energy barrier is equal to 1.0) (Figure 5a), which lies below thermal energy at 310 K (Table S4), indicating that ts release is accessible under physiological conditions. In contrast, the REC2‐R mutant displays a markedly different behavior. Therein, the transition from B0 to the released state (B0 → R2) is associated with a substantially higher energy barrier (relative energy barrier is equal to 3.3). Thus, substitution of lysine residues with arginine increases the energy barrier by approximately 3.5‐fold relative to the WT, effectively stabilizing the blocked state and suppressing ts release. By comparison, alanine and glutamine substitutions favor ts‐released conformations, lowering energetic constraints and promoting efficient progression toward double‐strand break formation. Moreover, the substitution of the negatively charged residues in α7 helix along with the substitution of the lysine with alanine has as results the existence of the blocked state even at those variants (α7PN‐REC2‐A and α7N‐REC2‐A; steric barrier). Taken together, these results suggest that the balance between steric accessibility and electrostatic trapping governs not only the conformational landscape but also the functional outcome of DNA cleavage. Importantly, all variants can be consistently interpreted within a unified mechanistic framework, in which modulation of electrostatic interactions in the REC2 loop tunes the gating process and, consequently, Cas12j activity.

  Figure 5 shows that mutations in the α7 helix alone have only a minor effect on the relative energy barriers, indicating that this region does not play a dominant role in controlling the transition toward ts‐released states. In contrast, substitutions in the REC2 loop have a much stronger impact. In particular, lysine‐to‐arginine substitutions shift the system toward higher energy barriers, which our simulations predict would translate into nickase‐like behavior.

  On the other hand, substitutions to glutamine—and even more so to alanine—significantly lower the energy barriers, predicted to promote double‐strand break activity (for instance REC2‐A and REC2‐Q). Figure 5 further shows that our results are consistent with the experimental data, as the energy barrier for WT is about 1.5 times higher than that of α7P, and this agrees with the reported experimental rate constants (k = 0.10 and 0.17 min−1 for WT and α7P respectively). Based on these trends, a combination of the short glycine–serine linker in the α7 helix (Δ155–176 (GSSG)) with alanine or glutamine substitutions in the REC2 loop is likely to represent an optimal strategy for minimizing energy barriers and maximizing double‐strand break activity. Experimental results show that combining Δ155–176 (GSSG) with a lysine in the REC2 loop increases the rate constant by approximately 20‐fold relative to the WT (Pausch et al., 2021), suggesting that alanine substitution could further augment this enhancement. Accordingly, as mentioned before, substituting lysine with arginine in the REC2 loop (REC2‐R) is likely an optimal strategy to increase the energy barrier between the blocked and released states, and could thereby be tested as a candidate strategy for enhancing nickase‐like activity (for example via in vitro cleavage kinetics assays). In summary, the REC2 loop is the dominant determinant of the ts‐release energy barrier. Arginine stabilizes the blocked, nickase‐like state, while alanine and glutamine favor release, whereas α7 helix mutations alone have comparatively little effect.


### Electrostatic gating controls both target‐strand release and productive positioning
  To further elaborate on the free energy surfaces described in Figure 4, we analyzed the relative orientation of the ts with respect to the nts across the identified conformational states B‐R (Figure 6). Productive cleavage requires an antiparallel (180°) alignment between strands, whereas parallel (0°) or perpendicular (90°) geometries are incompatible with cleavage. To quantify this, we performed k‐means clustering of metadynamics‐derived minima using the angle between nts and ts backbone vectors and their relative distance separation (Figure 6a). In the structural representations, antiparallel, perpendicular, and parallel conformations are shown in royal blue, purple, and Indian red, respectively (Figures 6b and S19), with representative structures shown in Figure 7.

  Consistent with the experimental observations, indicating that the ts dynamically samples multiple conformations above the RUVC active site, our analysis reveals substantial conformational heterogeneity across the free energy landscape. As shown in Figure 6, the B0 state predominantly populates catalytically competent conformations, with the ts adopting a near‐antiparallel orientation relative to the nts. Despite this favorable geometry, cleavage does not occur, as energetic barriers prevent productive access of the ts to the active site. Upon partial barrier release in the R0 state, the conformational landscape becomes increasingly heterogeneous, enabling sampling of both productive and non‐productive geometries. Transition toward more fully released states—characterized by increased separation between the α7 helix and the REC2 loop, as well as between the REC2 loop and the ts nucleotides—progressively destabilizes the catalytically competent alignment, leading to the predominance of non‐productive conformations. This behavior is consistently observed across all systems examined, including the WT and variant complexes. The only exception is the R1 state of the WT, for which limited sampling was observed due to the deep and narrow nature of the corresponding free energy minimum.

  Importantly, WT exhibits a finite population of antiparallel ts orientations in the R0 state, indicating that this conformation supports productive catalysis and is consistent with its ability to generate double‐strand breaks (Figure 6b). The REC2‐A variant similarly retains a fraction of catalytically competent conformations, further supporting its propensity for double‐strand cleavage. In contrast, REC2‐R predominantly populates released states lacking antiparallel alignment, reinforcing the conclusion that this variant favors nts‐nickase activity. REC2‐Q behaves comparably to REC2‐A, maintaining a measurable population of productive geometries and thus supporting double‐strand break activity. Notably, the apparent absence of blocked states in the α7N variant seems at first inconsistent with experimental observations; however, analysis of ts orientation within its released states reveals that the vast majority of conformations are not compatible with catalysis, with only a minor fraction adopting a productive alignment (Figure S19b). Thus, despite increased accessibility, the lack of proper strand orientation limits cleavage efficiency in this variant. Results for all systems are summarized in Figures S20–S23.

  Figure 7 provides representative structural snapshots illustrating the range of ts–nts orientations observed across the different conformational states. In the B0 state (Figure 7a), the ts adopts a near‐antiparallel alignment relative to the nts, representing a catalytically competent configuration. A similar productive geometry is also observed in the partially released R0 state (Figure 7b), confirming that cleavage‐compatible orientations can persist upon partial release. In the R1 state, antiparallel alignment can still be observed in some configurations (the angle between nts and ts is between 120° and 150°; not exactly 180°), especially those with lower REC2 loop–ts nucleotides distance (Figure 7c). Perpendicular, non‐productive geometries are also observed in the B0 state (as well as across all other conformational states) (Figure 7d). Parallel orientations are observed in released states, like the R1 state (Figure 7e). In the fully released R2 state (Figure 7f), there is no antiparallel alignment, and the ensemble is dominated by non‐productive conformations.

  Together, these representative structures illustrate the progressive loss of catalytically competent alignment as the system transitions from the blocked to fully released states, highlighting the importance of maintaining both proximity and proper orientation for efficient cleavage. Beyond the geometric classification of ts orientations, this analysis also provides additional support for the conformational dynamics of the α7 helix described above. As illustrated by selected structures in Figure 7, α7 motion is not governed by a single pivot point or rigid‐body displacement; instead, it involves distributed, hinge‐like rotations across multiple regions of the helix. Overall, productive ts‐cleavage requires both barrier release and correct strand alignment, both of which are controlled by the same electrostatic gating mechanism.


## CONCLUSIONS
  Extensive sampling by MD has revealed a key regulation mechanism in Cas12j. The present work serves as a mechanistic understanding of Cas12j and a guide for the rational engineering of more efficient Cas12j variants. We go beyond previous experimental work and provide evidence that ts accessibility to the catalytic site is governed by a coordinated gating mechanism involving the α7 helix (steric barrier) and the REC2 loop (electrostatic barrier). Our analysis reveals that the α7 helix regulates ts accessibility to the reaction center through localized conformational motions involving at least two distinct rotational regions rather than a single hinge point. Consistent with this mechanism, the helix movement is coupled to a displacement of the REC2 loop away from the DNA, supporting a dual‐barrier model in which both structural elements cooperatively regulate ts accessibility. Crucially, relief of steric hindrance alone is not sufficient to enable ts‐cleavage. Electrostatic interactions, primarily driven by the positively charged lysine residues within the REC2 loop, can independently stabilize the target‐strand through hydrogen‐bond interactions with the phosphate backbone, thereby restricting its movement toward the catalytic site. We reveal that transition of the ts from a blocked to a released state proceeds through two distinct kinetic pathways. In the first pathway, the system transitions directly from a fully blocked state, in which both the α7 helix and the REC2 loop jointly restrain the ts, to a released state once both steric and electrostatic constraints are simultaneously relieved. In the second, characterized by higher energy barriers, displacement of the α7 helix first removes the steric barrier, while the ts remains electrostatically trapped by interactions with the REC2 loop before eventual release toward the active site. These results indicate that the experimentally observed cleavage kinetics (Pausch et al., 2021) arise primarily from electrostatic retention of the ts by the REC2 loop rather than from steric obstruction by the α7 helix alone. In silico mutational analysis reinforces this mechanistic picture. Alanine substitutions in the REC2 loop weaken electrostatic interactions with the ts and promote its progression toward the active site, consistent with increased double‐strand break activity. Arginine substitutions strengthen electrostatic interactions and enhance stabilization of the ts, thereby increasing energy barriers and favoring nts‐nickase–like behavior. Glutamine substitutions in the REC2 loop weaken electrostatic interactions with the ts without fully disrupting polar contacts, shifting the conformational equilibrium toward released states (toward the active site) while reducing stabilization of blocked configurations. As a result, the associated energy barriers are lowered compared to the cases of mutations of α7 to positively charged residues, favoring ts progression toward the active site and promoting double‐strand break activity, albeit to a lesser extent than alanine substitutions. Moreover, our results reveal that electrostatic interactions govern not only whether the ts is released but also how it is positioned after release. In blocked states, the ts predominantly adopts an antiparallel orientation relative to the nts, which is the geometry required for cleavage. Importantly, our results show that both the strength of electrostatic interactions and the spatial separation between the REC2 loop and the ts govern access to catalytically competent conformations. Strong electrostatic stabilization progressively biases the ts toward non‐productive orientations, while increased REC2 loop–ts nucleotides distances preclude the antiparallel geometry required for efficient cleavage. This combined effect is most evident in REC2‐R mutant, which exhibits impaired alignment and reduced cleavage, consistent with a nickase‐like behavior.

  The present work probed an exhaustive array of mutations on the lysine‐rich segment of the REC2 loop; however, it remains unclear whether all five lysine residues contribute equally to electrostatic stabilization of the ts. Future work will systematically investigate partial substitutions, mutating individual lysines or subsets of residues, in order to determine whether specific positions dominate electrostatic trapping. Such analysis should reveal minimal mutation sets capable of selectively tuning ts‐cleavage kinetics without fully abolishing the electrostatic barrier. Collectively, our findings support a dual‐control model in which steric displacement by the α7 helix and electrostatic interactions mediated by the REC2 loop act cooperatively to regulate ts accessibility to the active site and cleavage. Beyond elucidating the molecular mechanism of Cas12j, this framework provides a rational basis for engineering RNA‐guided endonucleases with tunable kinetics—either enhancing double‐strand break efficiency (alanine and glutamine mutants) or stabilizing nickase activity (REC2‐R)—thereby enabling more precise, predictable, and safer genome‐editing applications. These specific mutational strategies are computational predictions derived from simulation, and we emphasize that experimental validation, for instance through in vitro cleavage kinetics assays, will be required to establish their actual editing outcomes before they can be considered engineering recommendations.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### System setup
  The atomic coordinates for Cas12j were taken from the available experimental structure deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (PDB ID: 7LYT). The experimental structures, in most cases, contain unresolved or missing residues. This is a common limitation due to the presence of regions with high structural flexibility. These absent regions were modeled by employing AlphaFold 3.0 from DeepMind, Maestro Viewer, and PyMOL from the Schrödinger Suite (Abramson et al., 2024). The long C‐terminal protein regions were not modeled due to their low prediction score in AlphaFold 3.0. Another common limitation of experimental structures is the absence of definite protonation states of ionizable residues, such as glutamic acid, aspartic acid, and histidine. Protonation states were assigned based on the local chemical environment of each residue, considering hydrogen‐bonding patterns, electrostatic interactions, and solvent accessibility. Specifically, acidic residues, Asp and Glu, were assigned in their protonated form when located in solvent‐inaccessible environments or when lacking stabilizing interactions with solvent or nearby positively charged groups. Conversely, they were assigned in their deprotonated form when solvent‐exposed or when engaged in favorable electrostatic interactions with surrounding residues. For histidine residues, protonation at the Nδ or Nε position was assigned based on the local hydrogen‐bonding and electrostatic environment. Protonation was placed on the nitrogen capable of forming stabilizing interactions with nearby hydrogen‐bond acceptors or negatively charged groups. When both nitrogens could be favorably stabilized, the histidine residue was assigned in the doubly protonated form. All assignments were further cross‐validated using PDB2PQR (Dolinsky et al., 2007) (as implemented in the APBS software suite). ASP342, ASP427, ASP646 were protonated whereas the rest of the Asp were kept deprotonated. GLU445, GLU657 were protonated whereas the rest of the Glu were kept deprotonated. HIS65, HIS125, HIS641, HIS672, HIS701 were protonated at Nδ. HIS17, HIS109, HIS121, HIS135, HIS187, HIS650 were protonated at Nε. HIS614 was protonated in both Nδ and Nε. The system was constructed by embedding the protein–sgRNA–dsDNA complex within a cubic unit cell. The volume of the simulation box was 13 × 13 × 13 = 2197 nm3. The ternary complex was then hydrated by water molecules that were modeled using the TIP4P potential (Horn et al., 2004). To ensure charge neutrality, counterions were added, along with additional salts to replicate experimental salinity conditions. In total, 50 mM of K+, 150 mM of KCl, and 1.5 mM of MgCl2 were added at neutral pH. The total number of atoms in the cubic box was approximately 280,000.

  The polypeptide was modeled using the AMBER ff14SB force field, RNA was described by ff99bsc0 + χOL3 parameters, and DNA using OL15 parameters (Maier et al., 2015; Zgarbová et al., 2011, 2015). Aqvist parameters were employed for the description of Mg2+ (Aqvist, 1990).


### Classical molecular dynamics simulations
  Full atomistic classical molecular dynamics production simulations were performed using the model described in the previous section following system relaxation and equilibration. System equilibration was done in multiple steps to gradually un‐restrain the structure. First, all heavy atoms of the protein–sgRNA–dsDNA complex were restrained. In the next steps, only the Cα atoms of the protein were restrained. The system was first simulated in the canonical ensemble and then in the isothermal–isobaric ensemble. During equilibration, the temperature was slowly increased from 100 K to the final desired temperature of 310 K based on published protocols in the literature (Daskalakis, 2022). Simulations were carried out using the GROMACS 2024 engine (Berendsen et al., 1995). For the WT, REC2‐A, REC2‐R, REC2‐Q systems 15 independent classical molecular dynamics simulations were run for 1.0 μs each with snapshots saved every 1.0 ns. For the rest of the systems five independent classical molecular dynamics simulations were run for 1.0 μs each with snapshots saved every 1.0 ns. The initial frames of the trajectories (that correspond to the first 100 ns of the simulation) were discarded and only frames corresponding to the plateau region of the Ca root mean square displacement over time were retained. The total accumulated classical molecular dynamics sampling reached 120.0 μs.

  Full atomistic molecular dynamics production simulations were performed based on published protocols in the literature. (Daskalakis, 2022; Daskalakis & Papapetros, 2024). Production simulations were carried out in the NPT ensemble using an isotropic compressibility of4.5 × 10−5 bar−1. A 2.0 fs time step was employed using the leap‐frog integrator to integrate the equations of motion. Non‐bonded interactions were handled using the Verlet cut‐off scheme. Van der Waals forces smoothly shifted to zero between 1.0 and 1.2 nm. Short‐range electrostatic interactions were truncated at 1.2 nm, and, the long‐range electrostatic interactions were computed using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method (Darden et al., 1993; Yeh & Berkowitz, 1999). All covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm allowing for a stable 2.0 fs integration time step (Hess et al., 1997). Temperature coupling was maintained at the desired temperature using the velocity‐rescaling thermostat with a coupling constant of 0.5 ps (Bussi et al., 2007). Pressure was maintained at 1 atm using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat with a coupling constant of 2.0 ps (Nosé & Klein, 1983; Parrinello & Rahman, 1981).

  The number of replicas per system was guided by the degree of conformational dispersion each system displayed in PC1–PC2 space (Figures 3 and S13 and S14). WT and the three REC2 loop variants (REC2‐A, REC2‐R, REC2‐Q) populated a comparatively narrow region of conformational space at five replicas, unlike the α7‐helix variants, which already showed broad dispersion. Because these four anchor our principal mechanistic claims, we have extended sampling to 15 independent 1 μs replicas to test whether the narrow distribution reflected under‐sampling or a genuine mutation‐dependent restriction; the additional replicas revealed a broader distribution, confirming the former. The remaining variants were adequately sampled within five replicas and required no additional trajectories.


### Well‐tempered metadynamics simulations
  Enhanced sampling simulations were performed using well‐tempered metadynamics (Barducci et al., 2008) as implemented in PLUMED interfaced with GROMACS. Two collective variables (CVs) were defined to describe the relative positioning of the α7 helix, REC2 loop, and the ts nucleotides. To define the collective variables, three atom groups were selected. The first group consisted of the Cα atoms of residues 167 and 168 of the α7 helix. The second group included the Cα atoms of residues K/A/R/Q522, K/A/R/Q523, A524, A525, K/A/R/Q526, K/A/R/Q527, and K/A/R/Q528 of the REC2 loop. The third group comprised the phosphorous atom of the phosphate group of the target‐strand nucleotide forming the highest number of hydrogen bonds with residues of the REC2 loop (nucleotide A in Figure 1c). The cores of mass (CoMs) of these three groups were calculated, and two distance‐based collective variables (CVs) were defined. The first CV, d1, corresponded to the distance between the CoM of group 1 and the CoM of group 2. The second CV, d2, corresponded to the distance between the CoM of group 2 and the CoM of group 3. These CVs were chosen to directly capture the steric separation between α7 and the REC2 loop and the progression of the ts relative to the steric gate. Well‐tempered metadynamics were performed by depositing Gaussian bias potentials along both CVs. The Gaussian width was set to 0.25 nm for each CV, and Gaussian hills of initial height at 1.718 kJ/mol, corresponding to 2/3 kBT at 310 K, were deposited every 2000 MD steps. A bias factor of 15 was employed, and all simulations were conducted at a temperature of 310 K. A two‐dimensional grid was used to reconstruct the free energy surface, with boundaries of 0.50–12.50 nm for d1 and 0.50–8.50 nm for d2. For each system one metadynamics simulation of 2.0 μs was performed, resulting in a total enhanced‐sampling time of 32.0 μs. In all cases, convergence was assessed using multiple complementary metrics, including the evolution of the one‐dimensional free energy profiles along each collective variable, the accumulated deposited bias, and the stabilization of the minimum free energy path barriers over simulation time (Figures S24–S39). In α7PN, α7PN‐REC2‐R, α7PN‐REC2‐Q, α7N, α7N‐REC2‐R, and α7P‐REC2‐R, convergence required longer simulation times, likely due to insufficient sampling arising from the relatively low Gaussian height initially employed, which led to inadequate exploration of the conformational space. To address this, additional simulations of 2.0 μs (2.5 in α7Ν) were performed using a threefold increased Gaussian height (2 kBT). This adjustment was necessary to avoid trapping in deep energy minima and to ensure adequate exploration of the full conformational space. The total computational time rises to 32.5 μs. This analysis confirmed that the free energy profiles reached stable values within the simulated time window. Reweighting was performed for the subsequent analysis. New CVs were introduced between groups of atoms. The first group consisted of the Ca atoms of residues 165–170 of the α7 helix. The second group included the backbone atoms of Ca atoms of the REC2 loop. The third group comprised the two non‐bridging oxygen atoms of the phosphate groups of the two target‐strand nucleotides forming hydrogen bonds with the REC2 loop. The cores of mass of these groups were calculated, and two distance‐based CVs were defined. The first CV, d₁, corresponded to the distance between the COM of group 1 and the COM of group 2. The second CV, d2, corresponded to the distance between the COM of group 2 and the COM of group 3. Free energy surfaces were reconstructed using the bias deposited during the original metadynamics simulations. The two‐step strategy of performing metadynamics with compact, computationally efficient CV definitions followed by re‐weighted reconstruction of the FES using backbone‐based groups was adopted for two reasons. First, the use of small Cα‐based groups during metadynamics reduces noise in the CVs and enhances sampling efficiency. Second, it decouples sampling from analysis, allowing projection onto more interpretable descriptors without restricting sampling.


### Principal component analysis
  Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to characterize the dominant collective motions of the protein in the WT, α7PN, α7P, α7N systems. For these four systems, the backbone‐only atoms were considered. All trajectories were first aligned using a least‐squares fit on the Cα atoms to remove global rotational and translational motion and concatenated. The covariance matrix was constructed using Cα atomic coordinates only and subsequently diagonalized using tools implemented in GROMACS to obtain eigenvalues and eigenvectors. The first five principal components (PC1–PC5) were extracted, as they collectively accounted for more than 50% of the total variance (see Appendix S1). For each principal component, 100 structures were interpolated along the corresponding eigenvector to visualize the associated collective motions. Residue‐wise fluctuations along each principal component were computed, and a reduced RMSF profile was constructed to identify the protein regions contributing most strongly to the dominant motions. RMSFREDUCEDi=∑k=15wk·RMSFki where wk corresponds to the normalized eigenvalue weight of principal component k.

  This procedure allowed identification of functionally relevant flexible regions contributing to the dominant collective motions.


### Principal component‐based clustering and structural pathway analysis
  Structurally distinct conformational clusters were identified through density‐based clustering in the reduced conformational space defined by the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) for the lysine‐containing systems (WT, α7PN, α7N, and α7P). The analysis focused specifically on the movements of the α7 helix and the REC2 loop. The PCA projections used for clustering were calculated using only the coordinates of these regions. Clustering was performed in the two‐dimensional space derived from the concatenated backbone trajectory using the density‐based algorithm HDBSCAN (Hierarchical Density‐Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise). Clustering was performed using a minimum cluster size of 225 frames, a minimum samples value of 50, and the Excess of Mass (EOM) method for selecting clusters. Clustering was performed on the entire concatenated trajectory, and the population of each cluster was then calculated separately for each system. Representative structures were selected as the frames with the highest membership probability in each cluster so that they best represent the dominant conformations of that cluster.

  The most populated cluster identified by HDBSCAN was further analyzed to extract a representative conformational pathway.

  A linear regression was fitted to the PC1–PC2 distribution of the dominant cluster: PC2=a·PC1+b One hundred equally spaced points were generated along the regression line in two‐dimensional space and for each point the closest real trajectory frame was identified by minimizing the Euclidean distance. These selected frames were then extracted from the trajectory and concatenated to produce a continuous structural pathway describing the dominant conformational transition of the α7 helix.

  To relate the dominant principal motion to a structural parameter we calculated the distance between the backbone atoms of residues 165–170 of the α7 helix and the backbone atoms of the residues of the REC2 loop for the frames extracted along the pathway. The CoM distance between the two regions was computed using the distance module of GROMACS. The distance was then plotted as a function of PC1 values. A linear regression was performed to quantify the correlation between PC1 and the interdomain separation. The coefficient of determination (R 2) was calculated to evaluate the strength of the linear relationship.


### Dirichlet process lognormal mixture model analysis
  The identification of the distinct conformational populations and the objective determination of transition thresholds in the distance distributions were achieved using a Dirichlet Process Lognormal Mixture Model (Ondicho Nyambega, 2019). This approach allows the data to define the number of underlying components without imposing a fixed number of distributions a priori. Because all analyzed distances are strictly positive and exhibit asymmetric distributions, the data were first log‐transformed and modeled using a Bayesian Gaussian Mixture framework with a Dirichlet Process prior, as implemented in sklearn.mixture.BayesianGaussianMixture. The maximum number of components was set to six and the Dirichlet Process weight concentration prior was set to 0.05 in order to favor sparse solutions and suppress non‐significant components. Model fitting was performed with full covariance matrices and a maximum of 3000 iterations to ensure convergence.

  The probability density function in real space was reconstructed analytically by summing the weighted lognormal components: fix=wi·1x·σi·2·π·exp−lnx−μi2·σi2

  Components with weights below 0.02 were excluded to eliminate statistically insignificant contributions. The resulting total density was normalized to relative frequency and lightly smoothed using a Gaussian filter to facilitate robust identification of maxima and minima. Local maxima and minima were identified using a prominence criterion which allowed us to determine the dominant states and the transition regions. This procedure was applied to the distance distributions between residues 165 and 170 of the α7 helix and the REC2 loop, as well as to the minimum distances between the side‐chain amino groups of LYS, ARG, or GLN residues and the non‐bridging oxygen atoms of the phosphate groups of the ts nucleotides. In both cases, the mixture model identified metastable states and defined physically meaningful cutoffs that separate hydrogen bonded from non‐bonded configurations.


### Hydrogen bond analysis
  Hydrogen bonds between the REC2 loop residues and the ts nucleotides were analyzed using the hydrogen bond analysis tools implemented in GROMACS, following the geometric criteria defined in the GROMACS reference manual.

  A hydrogen bond was considered to be formed when two conditions were simultaneously satisfied:the donor–acceptor distance was ≤0.35 nm.the hydrogen–donor–acceptor angle was ≤30° (equivalent to a donor–hydrogen–acceptor angle ≥150°).

  These criteria ensure that only geometrically well‐defined and energetically favorable hydrogen bonds are counted. For the lysine and arginine systems, hydrogen bonds were evaluated between the terminal amino or guanidinium groups of the side chains and the non‐bridging oxygen atoms of the phosphate groups of the target‐strand nucleotides. For glutamine mutants, hydrogen bonds involving the amide side‐chain group were similarly evaluated. The hydrogen bond analysis was used to validate the structural interpretation of the distance‐based mixture modeling. In particular, the characteristic minimum near 0.45 nm identified in the distance distributions corresponds closely to the geometric threshold separating hydrogen‐bonded from non‐bonded configurations, confirming that the electrostatic stabilization observed in the mixture model reflects genuine hydrogen‐bond formation.


### Free energy surface reconstruction, convergence assessment and kinetic analysis
  The two‐dimensional free energy surfaces were reconstructed from the well‐tempered metadynamics simulations using the PLUMED sum_hills utility with a 200 × 200 grid. The resulting two‐dimensional free energy surfaces files were analyzed using an in‐house python script. Free energies were converted from kJ/mol to kBT units at 310 K and smoothed using a Gaussian filter to reduce numerical noise. Local minima were identified using a neighborhood‐based minimum filter and all distinct minima were detected automatically. Minimum Free Energy Paths (MFEPs) between pairs of minima were computed using a Dijkstra‐based algorithm on the discretized free energy grid (Gallo & Pallottino, 1988). Energy barriers were defined as the difference between the saddle point energy along the MFEP and the energy of the initial minimum.

  Convergence of the well‐tempered metadynamics simulations was assessed by reconstructing the free energy surfaces at progressively increasing simulation times using truncated HILLS files. At each selected time point a new two‐dimensional free energy surface was generated. Convergence was evaluated by monitoring the stability of the free energy differences between the deepest minima as a function of simulation time. The two‐dimensional free energy surface was projected onto one‐dimensional free energy profiles by Boltzmann integration.


### Clustering analysis of ts orientation above the active site
  A total of 2000 frames (2500 in α7N) were extracted from well‐tempered metadynamics trajectories. From this ensemble, only frames corresponding to free energy minima (as identified in Figure 4 with white‐circled regions) were selected for further analysis. Then, to quantify the relative orientation of the ts with respect to the nts, we defined a geometric descriptor based on the angle between two vectors constructed from P atoms of selected nucleotides. For the nts, the vector was defined using the P atoms of the two nucleotides located directly above the catalytic site, corresponding to the pre‐penultimate and penultimate nucleotides. The terminal nucleotide was excluded due to its increased flexibility and reduced interaction with the catalytic core. These nts nucleotides were selected as they remain structurally stable throughout the simulations and thus provide a reliable reference. For the ts, the corresponding vector was defined using the P atoms of two nucleotides (A and B in Figure 1c). The nts vector was defined in the 5′ → 3′ direction, whereas the ts vector was defined in the 3′ → 5′ direction, such that antiparallel strand alignment corresponds to angles approaching 180°. The angle between these two vectors was computed for each conformation. In addition to the angular descriptor, a distance‐based coordinate was defined as the distance between the CoMs of the two P atoms of the nts nucleotides and the two P atoms of the ts nucleotides. This metric captures the relative spatial separation between the two strands in the vicinity of the catalytic site. Clustering of the selected conformations was performed using the k‐means algorithm as implemented in the scikit‐learn library, using the strand orientation angle and the CoM distance as input features. Prior to clustering, the data were standardized using z‐score normalization. Cluster assignments were subsequently used to identify dominant geometric states and to relate strand orientation to conformational regions of the free energy landscape.
