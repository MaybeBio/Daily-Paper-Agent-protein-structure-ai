# Antimicrobial peptides as computational candidates for SARS-CoV-2 papain-like protease inhibition


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-60957-1.


## Introduction
  SARS-CoV-2 infection begins when the virus enters the nasopharynx, establishing itself in epithelial cells that express the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor on the surfaces of nasal and oral mucosal cells. Once inside these cells, newly assembled virions mature in the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) or Golgi apparatus and are then released by exocytosis to infect other cells1.

  During this stage, the nonstructural protein PLpro catalyzes the cleavage of K48-linked polyubiquitin chains, ISG15, and viral polypeptides, thereby triggering viral development2,3. Notably, the SARS-CoV-2 PLpro has slight basicity, with 315 residues and a cysteine content of 3.5%4. This protein is essential to the membrane replication complex (RTC)4,5, which directs the replication, transcription, and maturation of the viral genome and subgenomic mRNAs. Furthermore, the catalytic site of the enzyme contains a classical catalytic triad (Cys111–His272–Asp286), responsible for the catalysis and cleavage of (i) K48-linked polyubiquitin, (ii) ISG15, and (iii) viral polypeptides6.

  Furthermore, PLpro cleaves the ubiquitin-like interferon-stimulated gene 15 (ISG15) protein through extensive hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions7, as well as attenuates type I interferon responses8, inactivates TBK-1, blocks NF-kB signaling, and inhibits the TLR7 signaling pathway by preventing the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of IRF3 to the nucleus, thereby disrupting the activation of type I IFN responses through the Toll-like receptor 3 or retinoic acid-inducible gene 5/melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 pathways4,5.

  In the catalytic triad of SARS-CoV-2 PLpro, Cys111 is a nucleophile, His272 acts as a general acid-base, and Asp286 pairs with His272 to promote the deprotonation of Cys1119. The PLpro-mediated hydrolysis process begins with a hydrogen bond between Asp286 and His272, which positions His272 to facilitate proton removal from Cys111. The generated thiolate then nucleophilically attacks the carbonyl carbon of the substrate peptide bond, forming a tetrahedral intermediate stabilized by the oxyanion hole of the active site. His272, acting as a proton donor, transfers a proton to the substrate’s amino group, releasing the amino terminus. Subsequently, deprotonation of a water molecule by His272 generates a hydroxide, which reacts with the thioester formed between Cys111 and the substrate residue, forming a new tetrahedral intermediate. The decomposition of this intermediate results in the release of the carboxy-terminal fragment, regenerating the cysteine thiol group for a new catalytic cycle10.

  The active site defined by the crystal structure of VIR25111 in complex with SARS-CoV-2 PLpro is formed by a channel of nonpolar amino acids (Gly271, Leu162 and Gly163) that establish hydrogen bonds with the ligand, an S4 pocket (Pro247, Pro248, Tyr264 and Tyr268), an S1’ pocket (Trp106, Asn109, Asn110 and Cys111) and its blocking loop (Gly266–Gly271) that closes after substrate or inhibitor binding9,12. The narrow Gly266–Gly271 binding channel of PLpro, which can only accommodate a linear arrangement of small chemical groups12, is one of the limitations for the search for inhibitors. Among the few existing inhibitors, compounds VIR250 and VIR251 exhibit high selectivity for SARS-PLpro variants and robustly inhibit SARS-CoV-2 PLpro, whereas compound GRL0617 demonstrates a particularly favorable in vitro IC50 of 2.1 ± 0.2 µM7.

  Although computational studies have been developed against SARS-CoV-2 targets such as the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the Spike protein and the major protease (Mpro)13,14, a growing number of studies have employed peptide-based approaches, including antiviral peptides for ORF9b protein deficiency, crucial for viral infection; chimeric peptides derived from ACE2, to target the Spike RBD; and bioactive peptides targeting the Mpro active site15–18. This demonstrates the potential for peptides to interfere with viral entry and proteolytic activity.

  Despite these advances, most computational studies of peptides have paid comparatively little attention to PLpro, particularly in the context of antimicrobial peptide libraries. In this context, PLpro represents a strategically relevant target due to its dual function in viral polyprotein processing and evasion of the host immune response through deubiquitination and deISGylation activities, thus expanding the scope of targets explored in the current literature8.

  Using peptides to develop antiviral drugs against SARS-CoV-2 is a relevant approach, as it blocks viral replication and inhibits dysregulation of signaling cascades in infected cells11. Antiviral peptides (AVPs) have emerged as promising candidates for combating viral infections due to their potent activity, low cytotoxicity, broad-spectrum efficacy, novel mechanisms of action, and low likelihood of inducing resistance19. AVPs can interrupt the replication cycle of enveloped and non-enveloped viruses at multiple stages, including pre-fusion, fusion, and post-entry into the host cell20. They can inhibit viral entry into the host cell by binding to cellular receptors or viral envelope proteins, preventing membrane fusion. Some of these peptides exhibit amphipathic properties, facilitating their interaction with lipid membranes and causing damage and viral inactivation. Within the cell, AVPs interfere with viral replication, affecting processes such as nucleic acid synthesis, viral protein production, or assembly of new viruses. In addition, some modulate the immune response, stimulating antiviral defenses or inducing programmed death of infected cells. Others still act by inhibiting enzymes essential to the virus, such as proteases, or by blocking molecular interactions critical to its viral cycle19,21,22.

  Notably, antimicrobial peptides are enriched in aromatic and hydrophobic residues (Table 1), a compositional signature associated with enhanced membrane interaction, viral envelope destabilization, and increased antiviral potency against enveloped viruses, including coronaviruses23,24. In addition to membrane-associated effects, several antiviral peptides have been shown to directly interact with viral proteins involved in host-cell entry, genome replication, and immune evasion22,25,26. These physicochemical properties distinguish antimicrobial peptides from conventional antiviral inhibitors. While antimicrobial peptides often exert their antiviral effects through membrane-associated interactions, conventional antiviral inhibitors are typically rationally designed to bind specific viral targets, including the RBD of the spike protein, Mpro, ACE2-associated interactions, or PLpro27–32. This distinction suggests a broader mechanism of action, potentially less susceptible to viral escape mutations33.

  The suitability of peptides for PLpro prevention is enhanced by their ability to establish extensive and specific interactions with protein surfaces, potentially improving selectivity compared to small molecule inhibitors, which often exhibit limited potency in non-covalent mechanisms or selectivity issues in covalent designs8,34–36. Although they offer benefits, peptide-based therapies have limitations, including susceptibility to proteolytic degradation, potentially low oral bioavailability, and challenges with systemic administration. Despite this, advances in peptide engineering have improved their pharmacological properties, supporting their role as a complementary strategy to small molecule inhibitors34,35.

  Compared to other targets, such as the Spike protein RBD and Mpro, PLpro exhibits distinct structural and functional features that support peptide-based strategies. The Spike protein RBD primarily mediates viral entry through interaction with ACE2, and peptide inhibitors targeting this interface generally target protein-protein interactions, which are typically large and shallow, posing challenges for high-affinity binding31,37. Similarly, Mpro, a cysteine protease essential for viral replication, has been extensively explored with small-molecule covalent inhibitors due to its well-defined catalytic site, although selectivity and resistance remain limitations32,38. In contrast, PLpro combines proteolytic activity with immune evasion functions (deubiquitination and deISGylation ), involving broader substrate recognition regions that may be more susceptible to peptide-based inhibition3,8.

  In this study, we employed an exploratory in silico workflow to identify peptides from publicly available antimicrobial peptide datasets with predicted binding potential toward SARS-CoV-2 PLpro using protein-peptide docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and binding energy analysis. This approach was designed to support the identification of candidates for subsequent experimental evaluation.


## Materials and methods

### Protein-peptide docking and in silico toxicity
  The SARS-CoV-2 PLpro structure obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 6WX4) corresponds to the wild-type enzyme, as no mutations were reported in the deposited coordinates12. The catalytic site, comprising the conserved catalytic triad Cys111–His272–Asp286, was used as a reference for molecular modeling analyses39. In addition, 6,615 peptide sequences with described antimicrobial activity were filtered and downloaded from the Antimicrobial Peptide Database (APD3), Database of Anticancer Peptides and Proteins (CancerPPD), Database of Antimicrobial Activity and Structure of Peptides (DBAASP), Fermented Food Database (FermFooDb), Mechanism of Action of the Human Microbiome (MAHMI), A structural database of biological peptide-mediated interactions (PepBDB), and A database of FDA-approved therapeutic peptides and proteins (THPdb) for protein-peptide docking40–46. Furthermore, the peptide sequences were submitted to the BioPep pipeline (https://github.com/lbqc-uesb/biopep), where each sequence was subjected to homology searching with BLASTp47 and structural modeling with Modeler48. This step aimed to provide standardized three-dimensional representations of the peptides for use in large-scale structural screening, and should not be interpreted as a prediction of the bound conformation adopted upon interaction with the target.

  Molecular docking experiments were performed in three independent runs using the modeled peptide structures and the SARS-CoV-2 PLpro PDB using the HPEPDOCK server, which performs peptide-protein docking using a hierarchical approach49. The method leverages the peptide’s flexibility through a set of pre-generated conformations, allowing exploration of conformational space during docking with reduced computational cost50. Considering the large number of sequences analyzed (6,615 peptides), this strategy was adopted as a practical first-pass screening step. In addition, in silico peptide toxicity was evaluated using ToxinPred 3.0, a deep learning model that classifies sequences as toxic or non-toxic based on learned patterns51. The most promising PLpro-peptide complexes were selected based on the peptide position within the protein active site, docking energy scores (the more negative, the better), and their toxicity profiles, classified as toxic or non-toxic. The top three selected complexes were opened in LigPlot + v.2.2.852 to generate 2D protein-peptide interaction maps and further investigate their binding mechanisms. Furthermore, PyMOL53 was used to construct and generate the three-dimensional structures of the peptides used in the analyses.

  The structure of PLpro (PDB ID: 6WX4) was prepared in PyMOL by removing crystallographic water molecules, heteroatoms, and co-crystallized ligands, followed by the addition of polar hydrogens. The molecular docking protocol was validated by redocking the co-crystallized inhibitor VIR25111. The similarity between the crystallographic and predicted poses was assessed using DockRMSD, which employs graph-isomorphism-based atom mapping to overcome ambiguities arising from atom numbering and molecular symmetry54. The resulting RMSD value of 1.218 Å demonstrated agreement between the anchored and experimental conformations. Thus, the RMSD below 2.0 Å confirms the reliability of the anchoring protocol for subsequent virtual screening and binding mode analyses54,55. To further reinforce our predictions, the peptidomimetic ligand VIR251, a known inhibitor of SARS-CoV-2 PLpro with reported dose-dependent activity between 2.3 and 300 µM11, and two peptides derived from the bacterial protein azurin (PDB ID: 1E5Z, chain A), were included in our analyses, corresponding to the p18 (Leu50–Gly67) and p28 (Leu50–Asp77) fragments56. The p18 and p28 peptides were previously proposed as in silico PLpro inhibitor candidates, providing a relevant benchmark for the computational approach adopted in the present study.

  Furthermore, the hydrophobicity and aromatic residues of the peptide sequences were analyzed in Python, and the proportions (%) were calculated. Hydrophobicity was based on the Kyte-Doolittle scale, while aromatic residues were defined as F, W, and Y57,58.


### Molecular dynamics simulations and structural stability analysis
  The stability and binding energies of PLpro-peptide complexes were evaluated in triplicate using GROMACS 202459 for a 500-nanosecond (ns) molecular dynamics simulation with a CHARMM36m force field. After the initial parameters were set in CHARMM-GUI60,61, we performed energy minimization using the steepest descent method with a convergence criterion of 1000 kJ·mol⁻¹·nm⁻¹ and 5,000 steps. Subsequently, the system was equilibrated in the NVT ensemble for 125 ps, with the temperature maintained at 303.15 K using a V-rescale thermostat applied separately to the solute (SOLU) and solvent (SOLV) groups. Then, production molecular dynamics were conducted in the NPT (1000 ps) ensemble, employing isotropic pressure control at 1 bar via the C-rescale barostat.

  After the molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, graphs were generated in QtGrace v0.2.6 (https://sourceforge.net/projects/qtgrace/) using triplicate averages, and root mean square deviation (RMSD) and root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analyses were performed to evaluate the flexibility and contribution of each residue in protein-ligand interactions throughout the simulation. Both analyses were compared to the complexes with the APO structure. The hydrogen bond (H-bond) graph was also interpreted to evaluate the formation and stability of these interactions over time, and the radius of gyration (Rg) graph, which measures the average distance of the atoms to the center of mass of the molecule. Furthermore, the radius of gyration (Rg) was calculated along the simulation trajectory and smoothed using a moving average with a window of 500 frames, corresponding to 50 ns based on the trajectory sampling interval (0.1 ns per frame).


### Hydrogen bond occupancy analysis
  In addition to the H-bond plot, hydrogen bond interactions between PLpro and the candidate peptides were evaluated using the MDAnalysis library (version 2.7.0)62,63, the corresponding structure file (.gro), and the trajectory file (.xtc). The analysis, restricted to interactions between site residues and peptide residues, was identified based on geometric criteria: donor-acceptor distance ≤ 3.5 Å and donor-hydrogen-acceptor angle ≥ 150°64. Hydrogen bond occupancy (%) was calculated as the fraction of simulation frames in which at least one hydrogen bond was present between a given pair of residues. For comparative analysis, the maximum occupancy between catalytic triad residues and peptide residues was extracted for each peptide, and complete interaction data for each system are provided in the supporting information (Tables S11–S14).


### MM/PBSA binding free energy calculations
  Estimated binding energies of the complexes were calculated in triplicate using the Molecular Mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area (MMPBSA) approach. For this, we used the gmx_MMPBSA pipeline65. Thus, it was possible to calculate the binding free energy using the interaction entropy (IE) method and the Generalized Born (GB) solvation model. In addition, the gmx_MMPBSA_ana graphical interface allowed visualization and export of graphs, including sets of energy components such as total gas-phase free energy (GGAS) and total solvation free energy (GSOLV), to calculate the entropy and enthalpy involved in the receptor-ligand interaction. For energy calculations and energy decomposition by residue, the active site and peptide residues were considered.

  To account for molecular size-dependent effects and allow for more appropriate comparisons between systems of different sizes, the binding free energies (ΔG), van der Waals contributions (VDWAALS), and electrostatic contributions (EEL) were normalized by the molecular weight of the ligands and expressed in kcal·mol−1·kDa−1 ref.66,67. Absolute values are shown in Figs. 7 and 8, and 9 and in Supplementary Tables S2, S4, S5, S7, S8, and S10. Molecular weights were calculated in kDa using the Biopython library (version 1.87) based on peptide composition, while the molecular weights of salinomycin (SLM) and ouabain (OUB) ligands were obtained from PubChem (CID: 3085092 and 439501, respectively). Residue-level energy decomposition data for all complexes are provided in supplementary Tables S3, S6, and S9.


### Statistical robustness and triplicate strategy
  To increase the statistical robustness of the results, we adopted a triplicate simulation strategy. Studies demonstrate that conclusions based on a single trajectory may not be reproducible, whereas multiple independent replicates provide more reliable estimates and allow quantification of the data variability68,69. Docking results correspond to the average of scores obtained in three independent runs. For molecular dynamics, average values of RMSD, RMSF, Hbond, and Rg were calculated from three independent simulations using a bash script with the GROMACS gmx analyze module. MMPBSA energies were reported as the average of energies obtained from three independent analyses. Results for each replicate run of the molecular docking and MMPBSA analyses are available in supplementary Tables S1–S7.


## Results and discussion

### Protein-peptide docking
  Due to their mechanism of action and biological properties, peptides are emerging as promising candidates in clinical and scientific research; in addition, they are highly selective and effective, with minimal or no toxicity70,71. Peptides exhibit characteristics for PLpro inhibition, meeting the specificities of the PLpro site (Fig. 1a) and exhibiting binding cooperativity across different regions to increase affinity with the substrate and reduce the unbinding rates of inhibitors in the blocking loop11,72. In this study, the antiviral candidate peptides 33,035, 17,047, and 58,620 exhibited docking scores ranging from − 217 to -264 (Table 1), indicating stronger binding affinity than previously reported ligands for PLpro, such as p18, p28, and VIR25111,12,56.

  Toxicity prediction indicated that the peptide 33,035, with the highest affinity, was classified as toxic, whereas 17,047, 58,620, p18, and p28 were predicted to be non-toxic. One way to improve this peptide sequence is to reduce hydrophobicity or modify aromatic residues to mitigate these effects without significant loss of activity73. Such modifications are routinely applied during peptide optimization, resulting in improvements in selectivity, reduced cytotoxicity, and maintenance of biological activity74–77. Consequently, peptide 33,035 can be subjected to additional structural refinement strategies to achieve a more favorable biological efficacy profile. Toxicity analysis was not performed for VIR25111 because the tool used is restricted to canonical peptide sequences in FASTA format. Since VIR251 is a peptidomimetic containing unnatural modifications, its evaluation by this method would be inadequate.


### Molecular dynamics simulations and structural stability analysis
  RMSD analysis was performed at two levels within the same simulation system: (i) the protein main chain, when complexed with different peptides, compared to the APO structure, and (ii) isolated peptides compared to the previously reported p1856.

  The protein-peptide analyses show distinct RMSD contributions of the PLpro protein complexed with different peptides compared to the APO structure (Fig. 2). Quantitative analysis demonstrates that the p18 and pep33035 complexes have structural stability close to that of apo, with favorable perturbations and minimal conformational perturbation, as evidenced by the averages obtained through GROMACS gmx analysis. The pep33035 complex showed an average RMSD of 0.332 ± 0.0379 nm, followed by the comparison pep18 complex with 0.316 ± 0.0482 nm, a very close value (Table 3). The pep17047 and pep58620 complexes presented values of 0.624 ± 0.0538 nm and 0.824 ± 0.048 nm, respectively. Furthermore, the pep58620 complex exhibits values close to 10 angstroms (1 nm) during the equilibrium phase, followed by a stable plateau, indicating a conformational fit69. Similarly, the higher value observed for this complex compared to the others suggests that the conformational changes may be associated with the induced-fit mechanism, characterized by conformational rearrangements induced by the peptide78,79. In addition, conformational rearrangements associated with the pep17047 complex are also observed, but with smaller structural deviations. This interpretation is consistent with the radius of gyration (Rg) results, which remained stable throughout the simulation (Fig. 5), indicating the maintenance of the protein’s overall compactness.

  RMSF analysis in the key site region (100–290) demonstrates a peptide-dependent modulated structural protein dynamic, as evidenced by the residual profile and regional analyses shown in Fig. 4d. RMSF shows that most residues exhibit fluctuations between ~ 0.05–0.15 nm, with localized peaks above 0.30 nm, especially in regions near ~ 225, ~264–272 and residue 286, characterizing regions of high flexibility.

  The average RMSF values shown in Fig. 4a indicate a global reduction in PLPro active site mobility when complexed with pep17047 (0.083 nm) and pep33035 (0.084 nm) compared to APO (0.098 nm). On the other hand, the complex with pep58620 (0.096 nm) shows intermediate fluctuations compared to the peptide p18 (0.101 nm), which exhibits increased flexibility. The percentage change vs. APO analysis reinforces this pattern, with the complexes pep17047 and pep33035 promoting relevant RMSF reductions in the active site, especially at residues 106–111 (− 39% and − 26%) and 264–272 (− 19% and − 14%). In addition, the pep58620 and p18 induce increases in mobility in the 264–272 region (+ 34% and + 20%) (Fig. 4b). The 286 residue shows highly system-dependent behavior, with a significant increase for pep17047 (+ 66%) and a reduction for pep58620 (− 24%) and p18 (− 15%).

  The statistical data in Table 3 demonstrate that, although the overall mean RMSF is lower for 58,620 (0.0828 nm), followed by p18 (0.0902 nm), APO (0.0922 nm), 17,047 (0.0924 nm), and 33,035 (0.0936 nm), this metric does not capture the observed spatial heterogeneity, but presents a global and simplified character of the structural dynamics, not reflecting the local variations observed along the protein. The standard deviation ranges from 0.0338 nm (58620) to 0.0415 nm (33035), indicating lower overall variability for 58,620 and greater heterogeneity for 33,035. The positive skewness across all systems (0.974–1.934) indicates a predominance of stable residuals with occasional high-mobility peaks, more pronounced in 33,035 (1.934), while the high kurtosis for this system (7.571) evidences the presence of extreme fluctuation events. Taken together, these results show that 17,047 and 33,035 promote directed stabilization in key regions, despite local variability, while 58,620 presents average overall stiffness associated with localized destabilization, and p18 exhibits the least favorable profile, with an overall and regional increase in flexibility, highlighting the 264–272 region as determinant for the modulation of structural dynamics.

  The integrated analysis of the Rg and the statistical distribution (boxplot) shows that all systems maintain high structural stability, with values confined to a narrow range (~ 2.35–2.42 nm) and low-amplitude fluctuations, without evidence of relevant conformational transitions or unfolding events (Fig. 5a). The time series demonstrates that the Rg profiles remain stable throughout the trajectory, with small local variations, confirming the structural equilibrium of the complexes. In parallel, the boxplot reveals low dispersion (reduced interquartile ranges) and the presence of punctual outliers, associated with dynamic fluctuations without global impact (Fig. 5b). The comparison between the systems indicates subtle, yet consistent, differences in the degree of compaction: the p18 complex exhibits the lowest Rg values (~ 2.37–2.38 nm), characterizing greater structural compaction, while 33,035 and 17,047 show slightly higher values (~ 2.40–2.41 nm), indicating more expanded conformations; the APO and 58,620 systems show intermediate behavior (~ 2.39–2.40 nm). Despite these variations, the values remain within a narrow range throughout the simulation, indicating that peptide binding is not associated with substantial changes in the overall protein organization, but rather with conformational adjustments of small magnitude. Specifically, the pep58620 complex and the pep17047 complex, which exhibit higher RMSD values (Fig. 2), show differences compared to the APO structure.

  The occupancy analysis, performed considering the catalytic triad, was calculated as the fraction of simulation frames in which at least one interaction between a pair of residues occurred, following a frame-based definition (Table 4)63,80,81. In the pep17047 complex, consistent His272–Leu4 interactions, corresponding to 72–89% of all simulation time, were observed in two of the replicates, and for Asp286–Phe7 interactions, we observed only 11% across all three replicates, indicating stable anchoring at the active site. The pep33035 complex showed moderate, yet reproducible, interactions, particularly Asp286–Leu2 (~ 8–19%) and His272–Tyr5 (~ 18%), suggesting partial stabilization. In contrast, the pep58620 complex exhibited occupancy values for His272–Phe16 (61–95%) and His272–Cys15 (49–89%). Furthermore, the comparison peptide (p18) showed lower and less persistent interactions, with occupancies generally below 25%, reinforcing the enhanced stability of the main candidates. However, these interactions varied significantly across replicates, indicating less consistent binding despite strong transient contacts.

  It is worth noting that occupancy values depend on the simulation configuration, particularly the total simulation time and the geometric definitions used to characterize intermolecular contacts; therefore, direct quantitative comparison with occupancy values reported in other studies is not always appropriate81–83. Thus, the interpretation of interaction persistence in this work was based primarily on the consistency of contacts observed in three independent replicates.

  The combination of hydrogen bond count and occupancy analysis indicates that peptide 17,047 forms the most stable and consistent intermolecular network with catalytic residues, followed by peptide 33,035, while peptide 58,620 shows greater structural variability between simulations. This behavior is consistent with evidence that hydrogen bonds play a central role in stabilizing protein-ligand complexes, with stability often correlated to the number and persistence of these interactions81,84. It is worth noting that hydrogen-bond interactions showed variability across independent simulation replicas, reflecting the dynamic and stochastic nature of molecular dynamics trajectories85–87. Furthermore, differences in specific interactions between residues are expected due to the conformational flexibility and transient nature of hydrogen bonds88.

  In the normalization (Table 5), the electrostatic contributions revealed a distinct trend, with p18 showing the most favorable value (− 331.36 kcal·mol⁻¹·kDa⁻¹), followed by peptides 33,035 (− 213.25 kcal·mol⁻¹·kDa⁻¹) and 17,047 (− 194.00 kcal·mol⁻¹·kDa⁻¹), while OUB (− 94.75 kcal·mol⁻¹·kDa⁻¹) and SLM (70.44 kcal·mol⁻¹·kDa⁻¹) showed significantly less developed or even unfavorable contributions. Even after correction for size, the peptide ligands showed greater efficiency in exploiting electrostatic interactions with PLpro, possibly related to greater conformational flexibility, which allows for more extensive and cooperative interactions at the active site.

  Solvent-related energies also contributed to the stability of the complexes, with polar energies (EGB) resulting from electrostatic solvation and non-polar energies (ESURF) associated with the hydrophobic effects of the solvent. The polar energies of peptide 33,035 (-237.68 kcal/mol), peptide 17,047 (-175.15 kcal/mol), p18 (-240.82 kcal/mol), and the SLM–PLpro complex (-41.1 kcal/mol) are negative, while that of the OUB–PLpro complex (61.9 kcal/mol) is positive. The negative value indicates a more favorable solvent interaction around the complex, providing stabilization. Regarding nonpolar energies, the peptide complexes 33,035, 17,047, and p18 exhibit unfavorable nonpolar contributions of 39.06 kcal/mol, 35.36 kcal/mol, and 25.91 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating significant exposure of hydrophobic surfaces to the solvent, while SLM (-5.7 kcal/mol) and OUB (-4.6 kcal/mol) exhibit favorable nonpolar contributions, being more hydrophobically stable.

  This pronounced exposure of hydrophobic regions to the aqueous environment may negatively impact peptide solubility, as solvent-accessible hydrophobic patches tend to promote aggregation and reduce effective dispersion in physiological media, ultimately compromising bioavailability91. Additionally, such unfavorable non-polar contributions may reflect a higher desolvation penalty upon binding, which is often associated with reduced pharmacokinetic efficiency and limited systemic stability for peptide-based candidates when compared to small-molecule drugs. In contrast, the favorable non-polar contributions observed for SLM and OUB suggest a more favorable hydrophobic solvation profile, a feature commonly correlated with improved membrane permeability91–93. Collectively, these results suggest that although peptides 33,035, 17,047, and p18 benefit from strong electrostatic solvation, their unfavorable hydrophobic exposure may pose challenges for solubility and in vivo bioavailability, highlighting a key limitation relative to the more pharmacokinetically optimized profiles of SLM and OUB.

  The less favorable normalized binding free energy values observed for peptides 33,035 (-6.38 kcal·mol⁻¹·kDa⁻¹) and 17,047 (-4.44 kcal·mol⁻¹·kDa⁻¹) compared to p18 (-13.2 kcal·mol⁻¹·kDa⁻¹), SLM (-63.96 kcal·mol⁻¹·kDa⁻¹) and OUB (-39.68 kcal·mol⁻¹·kDa⁻¹) may be related to the greater conformational flexibility of these peptides (Table 5). During molecular recognition, the restriction of rotational degrees of freedom and the unfavorable entropic contribution reduce the thermodynamic stability of the bond94,95. Consequently, smaller, more rigid molecules like SLM and OUB may exhibit more negative ΔG values due to the lower entropy loss associated with complex formation. Despite this, the complexes formed by peptides 33,035 and 17,047 still showed negative binding free energies, indicating that complex formation occurs spontaneously from a thermodynamic perspective. Additionally, Figs. 7c and 8c, and 9c show that the active site residues of PLpro contributed differently to the binding free energy when complexed with peptides 33,035, 17,047, and p18, particularly the catalytic triad (Cys111, His272, and Asp286); Only the residues in His272 and Asp286 contributed to the interaction with the peptides in peptides 33,035 and 17,047, while in p18 the residues Cys111 and His272 are noted.

  A high contribution from the Arg26 residue is also noted (Fig. 8c), which may be associated with the conformational mobility of this residue in the active site. The flexibility of the arginine side chain likely favors dynamic adaptation to the ligand and neighboring residues, thereby maintaining persistent contacts throughout the molecular dynamics trajectory. This behavior is consistent with previous observations that charged residues exhibit higher and more variable interaction energies, even hindering energy convergence65. Furthermore, the video generated from the molecular dynamics trajectory (accessible at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19899151) confirms this behavior, showing that Arg26 continuously interacts with active-site residues, primarily Asp164, Pro247, Tyr264, and Tyr268. Arg26 from pep17047 is represented in cyan, while the active site residues appear in orange; the protein is in gray, and the peptide residues in green. These structural observations corroborate the energetic results from residue decomposition, indicating the stability and persistence of local interactions.

  Although Cys111 is the nucleophilic residue of the catalytic triad, its limited contribution in the MM/PBSA decomposition does not necessarily imply reduced functional relevance, as this method primarily captures non-covalent interactions and may underestimate residues involved in catalytic or geometrically constrained roles65,67. Previous structural and mechanistic studies of SARS-CoV-2 PLpro indicate that interactions with His272 and Asp286 are critical for catalytic stabilization and can support inhibitory activity even in the absence of dominant energetic contributions from Cys1113,9. In contrast, p18 lacks significant interaction with Asp286 in the MM/PBSA decomposition, which may partially account for its comparatively lower predicted binding efficiency despite favorable Cys111 and His272 contacts. Thus, the preservation of interactions with His272 and Asp286, together with proper positioning within the catalytic pocket, supports the predicted inhibitory potential of peptides 33,035 and 17,047.

  Thus, the energy values (kcal·mol⁻¹·kDa⁻¹) obtained by MMPBSA indicate a stable, energetically favorable interaction between the peptide and PLpro, suggesting inhibitory potential in an intracellular environment. It is worth noting that the primary structural stability analysis was presented in a previous section; the MM/PBSA results are interpreted here as complementary descriptors, not as primary evidence. This approach aligns with the established literature, which highlights the qualitative, rather than quantitative, nature of MM/PBSA predictions95,96.

  In the present study, the selected peptides exhibited predicted binding affinities favorable to PLpro, corroborating their potential as specific inhibitors of PLpro. Furthermore, their physicochemical profiles suggest the retention of functional characteristics commonly associated with antimicrobial peptides (Table 1). This combination may indicate the coexistence of PLpro-targeted inhibition and broader antiviral properties. However, future studies involving enzymatic assays, viral replication models, and mechanistic investigations are needed to determine whether these candidates exhibit both PLpro inhibitory activity and additional antiviral effects.


### Translational considerations: delivery strategies and pharmacokinetic challenges
  PLpro is a proteolytic domain within the non-structural protein nsp3, generated by the translation of polyproteins, subsequently cleaving non-structural proteins and participating in immune evasion4,97. Given the availability of PLpro in the cytoplasm, the association of cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) with the evaluated peptides is an option to cross the plasma membrane and facilitate the cellular transport of the potential inhibitor. Cell-propelled pemphigoids (CPPs) have been investigated as delivery vehicles for peptide-based therapeutics, although their efficacy depends on factors such as peptide sequence, cargo properties, and cell type, and they may raise concerns regarding cytotoxicity and off-target effects98.

  Intranasal administration of (CPP 33035 peptide) may enhance the absorption of this potential inhibitor, since the nasal mucosa has a large surface area and richly vascularized subepithelial tissues that favor molecular absorption, allowing the molecule to be efficiently directed to the systemic circulation99,100. However, despite these advantages, intranasal administration also presents several limitations, including mucociliary clearance, enzymatic degradation in the nasal cavity, and variable absorption101. Although CPP conjugation and intranasal administration represent promising strategies, their translational applicability still needs to be experimentally validated, particularly with respect to pharmacokinetics, safety, and antiviral efficacy in vivo. Furthermore, peptide-based antivirals pose significant challenges in administration, including susceptibility to proteolytic degradation, short plasma half-life, rapid renal elimination, potential immunogenicity, and factors that may limit in vivo efficacy102. To overcome these limitations, strategies such as peptide cyclization, the incorporation of non-natural amino acids, and PEGylation have been proposed to improve stability, bioavailability, and pharmacokinetic profiles35,102.


### Limitations of computational predictions
  Despite the practicality of the computational approaches employed, some limitations must be considered. Molecular docking with HPEPDOCK employs simplified scoring functions and treats receptor flexibility in a limited manner, which may reduce the accuracy of predicted binding modes and interaction specificity49. Although the MM/PBSA method has achieved a favorable balance between computational cost and accuracy, it relies on approximations that can affect reliability. Notably, MM/PBSA frequently overestimates absolute binding free energies, although relative trends may remain consistent with experimental data91. Additional limitations include inadequate handling of conformational entropy and solvent effects, which can introduce systematic errors84. Furthermore, the results depend on simulation parameters such as force field selection and dielectric constants, which can affect reproducibility92. Therefore, affinities derived from MM/PBSA should be interpreted with caution and validated experimentally.


## Conclusions
  In this study, we identified peptide candidates with favorable predicted interaction profiles for SARS-CoV-2 PLpro. Among them, peptide 33,035 showed the strongest computational binding profile, though its predicted toxicity indicates that sequence optimization will be necessary prior to experimental evaluation.

  Peptide 17,047 emerged as the most promising scaffold, demonstrating non-toxic predictions, robust hydrogen bond occupancy with catalytic residues, and stable binding dynamics throughout 500 ns simulations. On the other hand, peptide 33,035 exhibited the strongest computational binding affinity; its predicted toxicity underscores the importance of integrated safety-efficacy screening in early-stage drug discovery. These findings highlight the value of computational toxicity prediction in prioritizing candidates for experimental validation.

  Beyond individual peptide identification, we provided the crucial steps in advancing PLpro inhibitor discovery: (1) a validated computational framework for peptide-based inhibitor screening, (2) mechanistic insights into peptide-PLpro binding modes, and (3) normalized energetic metrics enabling cross-molecular comparisons. In addition, our results establish a foundation for experimental validation studies, including enzymatic assays, antiviral efficacy testing, and structure-activity relationship optimization.

  This work demonstrates the potential of antimicrobial peptide libraries as starting points for antiviral drug discovery, potentially offering dual therapeutic benefits through both specific target inhibition and broad-spectrum antiviral activity. As PLpro remains an underexplored yet strategically important antiviral target, these computational insights may guide future rational design efforts toward addressing current and emerging coronavirus threats.
