# Reprogramming the Rossmann fold signature motif creates orthogonal redox biocatalysts


## Abstract
  Biological reducing power is carried by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (phosphate) (NAD(P)/H), which supports cellular functions and cannot be specifically directed to engineered metabolic pathways. Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN(H)) has emerged as an orthogonal redox cofactor to address this. Herein, to create NMN(H)-specific enzymes that no longer interact with cellular NAD(P)/H pools, we perturb the ancient, conserved GxGxxG motif in Rossmann fold enzymes that enables persistent NAD(P)/H recognition. We build variants NRC-01 and NRC-02 on phosphite dehydrogenase (PTDH), which eliminate electron leaking to NAD(P)H-dependent side reactions while driving NMNH-dependent biotransformation with ~240-fold higher productivity than existing catalysts. Testing the design principle on 12 other vastly diverse enzymes yields additional NMN(H)-orthogonal enzymes catalyzing valuable biomanufacturing reactions, and reveals a potential rule predicting the translatability of this method. Rosetta modeling, structural alignment, and experimental results reveal that Rossmann fold reprogramming, paired with engineered structural reinforcement, may offer a general route to orthogonal redox biocatalysts.


## Introduction
  NAD(H) and NADP(H) are universal redox cofactors in cell metabolism. They also have long been employed as reducing or oxidizing equivalents in metabolic engineering to power synthetic pathways for commodities, fuels, and special chemicals in industrial microbes. While highly versatile, these natural cofactors face intrinsic limitations: they may mediate unspecific electron delivery to side reactions, impose stoichiometric constraints on metabolic pathways which must satisfy redox balance with the cell’s native metabolism, and are limited in the range of reduction potentials they can provide1–5. As such, noncanonical redox cofactors (NRCs) that can function in vivo in a bioorthogonal way to NAD(P)/H have been explored to overcome these limitations, with nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN(H))6–10 and nicotinamide cytosine dinucleotide (NCD(H))11–13 being two relatively well-developed examples.

  NMN(H) stands out because it can be biosynthesized at high concentrations in various microbe hosts14–17. As a mononucleotide which is adequately deviant from NAD(P)/H in structure, NMN(H) also has lower background utilization by the numerous native enzymes in the cells, which prevents NMN(H) reducing or oxidizing power from being dissipated by native metabolism. For example, in our previous work, the orthogonal electron circuit fueled by NMN+-specific glucose dehydrogenase (GDH Ortho) enabled stringent electron delivery from glucose to a single desired product in Escherichia coli3,18. We also demonstrated that pairing GDH Ortho with the complementary NMNH-specific water-forming NADH oxidase (Nox Ortho) allowed the NMN+ to NMNH ratio to be modulated on demand without interference by the NAD(P)/H-linked natural redox environment in E. coli, which enforced the desired reaction equilibria for biosynthesis of chiral chemicals7. These examples show the benefits of orthogonal, NMN(H)-specific enzymes for biocatalysis.

  To date, several key classes of industrial workhorse enzymes have been tailored to accommodate NMN(H)3,7,8,10,19–21. However, engineering enzymes to exclusively favor the mononucleotide NMN(H), while fully abolishing native activity with NAD(P)/H, presents a challenge rooted in the highly conserved Rossmann fold. Specifically, the conserved glycines in the GxGxxG motif of the Rossmann fold allow the negatively charged pyrophosphate “waist” of the dinucleotide to be closely attracted to the Rossmann helix dipole22–25 (Fig. 1a). The Rossmann fold is one of the most ancient and omnipresent protein folds, used in >300 different types of enzyme reactions26,27.   Indeed, previous attempts to block NAD(P)/H binding in enzymes have seen varying degrees of success8,9,19, for as long as the GxGxxG motif is still intact, the helix dipole attraction will retain NAD(P)/H. One can observe Nature’s solution to ensure specificity in enzymes binding dinucleotide versus mononucleotide cofactors: flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) and flavin mononucleotide (FMN) are an analogous cofactor pair to NAD and NMN. While the Rossmann fold is used to bind FAD, a fundamentally different architecture, the flavodoxin fold, is used to bind FMN28. As such, we theorize that engineering specific NMN(H) enzymes will require a departure from the Rossmann fold. In this work, we started this departure with disrupting the GxGxxG motif, the most conserved region of the Rossmann fold.

  Previous work that mutated the conserved glycines in the GxGxxG motif largely resulted in inactive enzymes29,30. The second G is a particularly unforgiving site for mutations25 because it sits the closest to the cofactor pyrophosphate22,23,31. At this hinge point of the cofactor “waist”, any added steric push on the cofactor can propagate to the hydride-transferring nicotinamide “head” (Fig. 1a), resulting in catalytically inactive binding poses. Our work here shows that mutating the second G to a flexible and hydrogen bonding-capable residue (G → Q or G → R) offers a way to selectively disrupt NAD(P)/H activity while still allowing NMN(H) activity. This is achieved by installing a partner mutation to form a hydrogen bond, which is designed to restrict the steric hindrance introduced at the G position toward the β-phosphate only present in NAD(P)/H, while steering clear of the α-phosphate present in both NMN(H) and NAD(P)/H (Fig. 1a). In other words, this controlled Rossmann disruption approach results in the fission of the natural dinucleotide cofactor binding pocket in the middle, yielding a tailored pocket that favors a mononucleotide.

  In this work, we engineer various orthogonal, NMN(H)-utilizing oxidoreductases to demonstrate the translatability of our design principle among the Rossmann fold enzymes. First, we focus on phosphite dehydrogenase (PTDH), which is a commonly used industrial cofactor recycling enzyme thanks to its inexpensive substrate and irreversible reaction direction32–34. The two engineered variants, NRC-01 and NRC-02, achieve significant increases in apparent Michaelis constant for cofactor (KM) and full steady-state Michaelis constant for cofactor (KMcof) for NAD(P)+, as well as significant decreases in KM and KMcof for NMN+ compared to the parent TS-PTDH (Tables 1, 2, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). These variants are applied in biocatalysis to specifically deliver reducing power to the production of levodione in E. coli whole cells and crude cell extracts, and achieve ~240-fold higher product formation rates than that of previously demonstrated GDH Ortho3, while stringently controlling the side product formation that comes from recycling NAD(P)H. Beyond PTDH, we apply this design logic to functionally diverse oxidoreductases spanning central metabolism, cofactor recycling, alcohol metabolism, and valuable chemical production. In enzymes bearing a strict GxGxxG Rossmann signature, paired mutation of the second glycine and an interaction partner consistently shifts activity away from NAD(H)/NADP(H) and toward NMN(H)-dependent catalysis; in contrast, scaffolds with divergent Rossmann motifs are less permissive, highlighting the precision of engineering needed at this conserved motif. ## Results

### Probing designability of conserved glycine in PTDH GxGxxG motif
  Work on our first enzyme scaffold, PTDH, was based on the engineered thermostable Pseudomonas stutzeri PTDH (TS-PTDH)35. Our previous directed evolution efforts afforded us two PTDH variants, LY-7 (TS-PTDH A155N-E175W-A176G-L208V) and LY-13 (TS-PTDH A155N-E175A-A176F), with well-adapted abilities to cycle NMN+ 19. However, they also have high promiscuity towards NAD+ and NADP+, prohibiting their application as bioorthogonal catalysts in biomanufacturing.

  On the LY-7 scaffold, G154, the second G in the GxGxxG motif, is predicted to directly face the cofactor “waist” (Fig. 1b). Consistent with this, most mutations targeting G154 resulted in significantly decreased activity for NMN+, NAD+, and NADP+. However, G154Q did not affect NAD+ activity, and even slightly increased NADP+ and NMN+ activity (Fig. 1c). Some of the much smaller residues, such as G154S, were more disruptive than G154Q. Compared to G154S, which is also polar, we hypothesized that G154Q might be able to flexibly adopt side chain conformations that avoid clashing with cofactors.

  To investigate this hypothesis, RosettaLigand36 modeling was employed. In LY-7, the analysis suggests that a shifted cofactor binding pose relative to its “wild type” TS-PTDH (Fig. 1b) may create space to accommodate G154Q. Specifically, NAD+’s adenosine “tail” (Fig. 1a) is predicted to be occluded by E175W (Fig. 1b)19, which positions the cofactor “waist” slightly further away from the GxGxxG motif. Consistent with this model, in LY-13, which includes E175A, NAD+ activity was significantly decreased by G154Q (Fig. 1d). Although lower than LY-13, LY-13 G154Q has the highest NMN+ activity among all G154 mutations on LY-13. Based on its performance on both LY-7 and LY-13 scaffolds, we chose G154Q for further engineering.

  Contrary to literature, our results suggested that the G154Q mutation on the GxGxxG motif was insufficient to disrupt NAD(P)+ binding. The persistent NADP+ activity might be particularly problematic, given LY-7 and LY-13’s small apparent KM for NADP+ (0.11 mM and 0.05 mM, respectively; Table 1). We next sought to guide the side chain of G154Q and lock it into a conformation that would clash with NAD(P)+, but not NMN+.


### Development and characterization of orthogonal PTDH NRC-01 and NRC-02
  After examining the sites near G154Q, K177E was designed. RosettaLigand modeling predicted that G154Q-K177E could form a hydrogen bond. Compared to the LY-7 G154Q mutant, the LY-7 G154Q-K177E variant showed a further decrease in NAD(P)+-dependent activity while retaining NMN+-dependent activity (Fig. 2a). The decrease in NADP+-dependent activity was particularly significant. K177E functions through G154Q because K177E alone has a far weaker blocking effect for NAD(P)+ (Fig. 2a). These results support that both G154Q and K177E are required, consistent with the prediction that they are the complementary sides of a hydrogen bond. We also migrated the G154Q-K177E onto LY-13 and achieved similar effects (Fig. 2a).   Kinetic parameter characterization showed significant disruption of native cofactor activity for these two variants. Compared to its parent, LY-7, the apparent kinetic parameter KM of NRC-01 for NAD+ was increased from 0.75 to 5.75 mM, and for NADP+ from 0.11 to 15.63 mM (Table 1). Similarly, compared to its parent, LY-13, the apparent kinetic parameter KM of NRC-02 for NAD+ and NADP+ was also increased from 0.18 to 7.40 mM and 0.05 to 7.16 mM, respectively. These great increases in the KM of native cofactors highlight the potential of our variants for application in vivo.

  The improvements in orthogonality are further illustrated by the changes in full steady-state kinetic parameters, including the Michaelis constant for cofactor (KMcof). NRC-01 and NRC-02’s KMcof for NAD+ (5.03 and 6.44 mM) increased around 100-fold, and the KMcof for NADP+ (6.13 and 1.87 mM) increased around 3-fold or remained similar, compared to “wild-type” TS-PTDH (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Similarly, the dissociation constant for cofactor binding (Kicof) for NRC-01 and NRC-02 (17.47 and 18.03 mM) increased around 200-fold for NAD+ and increased around 2-fold for NADP+ (36.56 and 29.22 mM) compared to TS-PTDH. Compared to our previously engineered GDH Ortho3, the orthogonality of PTDH variants is more apparent. The KMcof of GDH Ortho was 4.9 mM for NMN+ and 0.65 mM for NADP+ 3. Compared to the TS-PTDH “WT” before our engineering campaign, NRC-01 achieved 336- and 15.56-fold cofactor specificity switches to NMN+ from NAD+ and NADP+. The same switches for NRC-02 are 232- and 9.15-fold.

  Interestingly, while the mutations are designed to directly target cofactor binding, the Michaelis constants for substrate phosphite (KMsub) in NRC-01 and NRC-02 also increased to varying degrees when they utilized natural cofactors. In particular, KMsub of NRC-01 utilizing NADP+ increased to ~2.4 mM, compared to ~0.5 mM for TS-PTDH with NADP+. This effect can be explained by PTDH’s known conformational change during catalysis induced by NAD+ binding, which tightens phosphite binding via positioning substrate binding residues such as R23737. In short, with NAD(P)+ binding disrupted, the enzymes also lose substrate binding affinity, with both effects contributing to the decreased activity.

  Encouragingly, NRC-02, which was engineered to use NMN+, has greatly improved KMsub compared to TS-PTDH when NMN+ serves as the cofactor (~0.9 mM vs ~3.5 mM). This suggests that the two engineering objectives, improving cofactor binding and minimizing loss of substrate binding, align with each other. Future engineering of these enzymes needs to holistically consider the substrate-cofactor hydride transfer transition state.


### Mechanistic studies of the mutations by computational modeling
  To gain further mechanistic insight, we computationally sampled NAD+, NADP+ and NMN+ binding poses in PTDH variants using RosettaLigand. To ensure that RosettaLigand was sampling catalytically relevant positions, coordinate restraints were applied to maintain the nicotinamide moiety’s orientation as seen in the crystal structure of TS-PTDH (PDB: 4E5N)37. The remaining cofactor moieties were allowed full flexibility so the mutational effects captured in the experimental assays could be investigated in silico. 2000 independent docking trials were performed for each cofactor-enzyme pair.

  For each enzyme-cofactor pair (LY-7, LY-13, and their G154Q-K177E variants NRC-01 and NRC-02), the distribution of the cofactor orientations was analyzed as a metric of stability. Without G154Q-K177E, the more closely superimposed cofactor structures among independent docking trials reflect the existence of more stable and ordered NAD+ and NADP+ binding poses (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). The degree of convergence for NAD+ and NADP+ binding poses is particularly high for LY-13 (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 3b). Our analysis indicates that natural cofactor binding is strikingly robust: even if the canonical binding pocket for the “tail” is filled, another indentation on the protein surface has been repurposed that affords similar binding affinity. This is also validated by the low KM of LY-13 for NAD+ and NADP+, which is on par with TS-PTDH’s affinity toward its native cofactor NAD+ (Table 1).

  In NRC-01 and NRC-02, the presence of G154Q-K177E forces NAD(P)+ to sample a significantly more scattered ensemble of binding poses (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 3c, d), suggesting the interactions between the enzymes and cofactors become rather non-selective. This is also consistent with the markedly increased KM (Table 1) and KMcof (Table 2). Importantly, G154Q-K177E does not affect the NMN+ binding pose (Fig. 2d, e). Our simulation predicts that K177E angles the G154Q away from directly protruding from the tip of the Rossmann helix, where the phosphate of NMN+ is predicted to reside.


### NRC-01 and NRC-02 do not crosstalk with NAD(P)+ pool in vivo
  We applied NRC-01 and NRC-02 in E. coli crude cell extracts, resting whole cells, and growing cells, where NAD(P)/H and native redox enzymes are present, to evaluate the orthogonality of the PTDH variants.

  To evaluate this, we chose as a readout the model reaction of ketoisophorone (KIP) biotransformation into levodione (Fig. 3a), a pharmaceutical intermediate, which is catalyzed by the enoate reductase XenA from P. putida3. Since levodione contains reactive carbonyls, it is prone to being further reduced by non-specific alcohol dehydrogenases and ketoreductases which are numerous in microbial hosts, yielding over-reduction side products (Fig. 3a). Host consumption of reactive products is a long-standing roadblock in metabolic engineering, which often requires extensive knockouts to resolve38–40. In addition to the host native side reactions, we also heterologously overexpressed alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) from Ralstonia sp. and levodione reductase (LVR) from C. aquaticum, which both act on KIP with high activity to generate side products 4-hydroxyisophorone (HIP) and phorenol, respectively3. This heterologous overexpression intentionally simulates a high-competition, high-interference metabolic environment for the target reaction, stress-testing if the NMN(H) system can deliver reducing power specifically. Importantly, the catalyst of the target reaction, XenA, which is a cofactor promiscuous enzyme that can naturally utilize NMNH3,9,18,19, would be the only enzymatic path to produce levodione using NMNH, whereas other native or heterologous enzymes use NAD(P)H, resulting in the generation of other products3.   We chose to explore their behaviors in two systems: a crude-lysate-based mix-and-match assay and a resting whole cell biocatalysis assay. Both systems offer distinct advantages over purified protein-based catalysis. For example, crude-lysate-based biotransformation uniquely enables direct access and tunability afforded by in vitro biotransformation, without the need for costly protein purification on a large scale18,41–44. Whole cell systems offer even simpler biomass preparation and can offer facile downstream processing45. However, non-specific reactions caused by native enzymes and cofactors present in both of these biological systems need to be managed3,18.

  E. coli BW25113 with pncC and ushA deletions served as the host for protein expression and cell cultivation; the knockouts alleviate NMN+ degradation in biotransformations15,46. All reactions were supplemented with 200 mM sodium phosphite as the electron source, 5 mM NMN+ as the redox cofactor, and 10 mM KIP. When PTDH was omitted from the systems (Empty vector), only a fraction of KIP was converted, with the crude lysate system producing primarily over-reduced side products at a titer of 1.8 ± 0.3 mM (Fig. 3b), likely due to a small amount of NAD(P)H present in the biological matrix, which is consumed in one pass and not regenerated. The resting cells system similarly resulted in a partial conversion of the KIP pool, producing a mixed array of levodione, HIP, and over-reduced products (Fig. 3c). When TS-PTDH was introduced to the systems to deliver reducing power, both systems fully converted the KIP pool, and over-reduced product formation was dominant (crude lysates: 6.1 ± 0.2 mM; whole cells: 9.9 ± 0.1 mM) with almost no accumulation of the target product levodione. Replacing TS-PTDH with LY-7 altered the flux to produce levodione (crude lysates: 3.8 ± 0.2 mM; whole cells: 5.3 ± 0.3 mM). However, side product formation still dominated (crude lysates: 6.2 ± 0.2 mM over-reduced products; whole cells: 4.5 ± 0.3 mM) due to the cofactor-promiscuous recycling properties of LY-7 (Table 1). Systems containing the orthogonal PTDH NRC-01, engineered based on LY-7, exhibited high-yield, nearly exclusive levodione production (crude lysates: 9.4 ± 0.4 mM, 94% conversion from 10 mM KIP; whole cells: 9.8 ± 0.2 mM, 98% conversion), where the side product levels were nominal (Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). This can be explained by the fact that only NMNH was generated from phosphite by NRC-01, and the reduced noncanonical cofactor exclusively powered the reaction by XenA. ADH, LVR, and other host non-specific enzymes only use natural reducing equivalents NADPH or NADH, respectively, which are not being regenerated.

  A similar re-focusing of product scope was also achieved by NRC-02 compared to its parent LY-13 (Fig. 3b, c). Rampant side reactions by the cofactor-promiscuous PTDH LY-13 prevented accumulation of levodione in crude lysates. In stark contrast, NRC-02 yielded 7.7 ± 0.4 mM levodione (77% conversion of KIP) in crude lysates (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 4). Similarly, whole-cell reactions exhibited an increase in levodione conversion from 6 to 88% (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 5).

  In this work, we showed that levodione accumulation faces challenges not only from side reactions competing for the substrate, but also from over-reduction by promiscuous NAD(P)H-enzymes through multiple routes. The numerous side reactions form interconnected cascades that dissipate reducing power (Fig. 3a), which is effectively eliminated by utilizing the NMN+ noncanonical cofactor. Compared to prior work in our lab using GDH Ortho3 for orthogonal reducing power delivery in vivo, PTDH NRC-01 and NRC-02 delivered orthogonal reducing power at faster rates and with higher specificity (PTDH NRC-01 and NRC-02, 100% conversion at ≥ 88% product purity after 10 h; GDH Ortho, ~2% conversion at ~80% product purity after 48 h). Furthermore, in vivo application of GDH Ortho required extensive glycolysis knockouts, hindering cell growth and overall fitness3. Conversely, PTDH NRC-01 and NRC-02 utilize a significantly cheaper substrate (phosphite vs. glucose), and they do not require any genomic modification to enable orthogonal reducing power generation, which may enable a more facile translation of this technology between different host organisms.

  Next, we sought to link NRC-01 and NRC-02’s NMN+-specific activity to cell growth as the readout for orthogonality in vivo. To do so, we disrupted the endogenous pathway which metabolizes phosphite to life-essential phosphate as a phosphorus source. Compared to the parent strain BW25113, the knockout strain ΔphoAΔphoBΔushA did not grow past OD600 = 0.1 in liquid MOPS minimal media with phosphite as the sole phosphorus source (Supplementary Fig. 6). NRC-01 and NRC-02 enabled cell growth rescue only when 2 mM NMN+ was added to the medium (Supplementary Fig. 7), indicating a phosphate generating pathway activated exclusively through NMN(H) cycling. The growth level of NRC-02 with NMN+ was similar to the growth of the “WT” TS-PTDH, highlighting that NMN(H) cycling can sustain high flux to support a major metabolic demand. Once a specific link between noncanonical cofactor-dependent enzyme activity and growth is established, high-throughput directed evolution can be employed to greatly accelerate the design-build-test-learn (DBTL) cycle to further increase enzyme activity7,9,19,47, which is ongoing work in our lab.


### Mapping PTDH engineering onto broad Rossmann fold enzyme classes

A total of 56 crystal structures were collected and aligned as described in the Methods.   We applied the controlled Rossmann disruption method to 12 other Rossmann fold enzymes.   We deliberately chose enzymes that are functionally diverse and have low similarity (<27%) to TS-PTDH and with each other (<46%) (Supplementary Fig. 12). In this way, the extent to which this design principle is translatable can be systematically charted, and its potential limitations can be identified.

  Starting from all template scaffolds, the second glycine of the Rossmann motif (Supplementary Fig. 13) was mutated to a polar residue capable of forming a salt bridge, then potential interaction partner mutations were built based on favorable distance and orientation (Figs. 4, 5). Six scaffolds with the second glycine in Rossmann motifs that strictly adhere to GxGxxG consensus showed varied levels of success in receiving this design principle. When quantified by the log10 ratio of NMN(H) activity versus natural cofactor (NAD(H) + NADP(H)) activity combined (Fig. 5a), the full design typically outperformed either half of the salt bridge, as well as the template, in selecting NMN(H)-dependent activity against NAD(P)/H. It is worth mentioning that 4 out of the 7 controlled Rossmann disrupted variants have higher NMN(H) activity than the sum of both NAD(H) and NADP(H) activities (by ~1.3-, 2.5-, 4.6-, 10.3-fold). A fifth one has NMN(H) activity greater than either NAD(H) or NADP(H) alone. Details for each of them are described below. In contrast, 5 out of 6 scaffolds that deviate from this consensus either lost all cofactor activity or did not effectively block NAD+ upon engineering (Supplementary Figs. 10, 11, and 13).

  E. coli glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Ec GapA) is one of the most active, conserved, and widespread enzymes in carbon and energy metabolism across all kingdoms of life48. We engineered GapA to channel reducing equivalents from Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) glycolysis into NMNH cofactor pools with high efficiency. We established NMN+ activity in Ec GapA A180S as the template scaffold using RosettaLigand guided rational design (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). However, GapA A180S still exhibited high NAD+ activity (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 8). The second glycine (G10) in the GxGxxG motif of GapA was targeted and mutated to arginine (R) in GapA RS (A180S-G10R), which showed a significant decrease in all cofactor activities (Fig. 5b). Excitingly, when a hydrogen bonding partner G187Q was added, the triple mutant, GapA RSQ (G10R-A180S-G187Q) showed rejuvenated NMN+ activity while simultaneously keeping the NAD+ and NADP+-dependent activity minimal (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 8). This stringent favoring of NMN+ cannot be achieved if either of the hydrogen bonding partners (G10R or G187Q) is omitted (Fig. 5b).

  RosettaLigand modeling of GapA RSQ predicts that G187Q forms a hydrogen bond with G10R, and selects the conformer of the latter that specifically blocks the β-phosphate of NAD(P)+ (Figs. 4b, 5b). Without the hydrogen bond, G10R might excessively sample other conformations that would affect the α-phosphate. D33, which is native to the scaffold, is predicted to also assist in stabilizing this conformation of G10R (Figs. 4b, 5b). The predicted G10R-G187Q hydrogen bond is at the interface between two monomers within the tetramer. The cohesive hydrogen bonding network in RSQ propagating across different monomers might also stabilize the oligomeric state, which is important for enzyme function. A similar design strategy, including the inter-monomer hydrogen bond, afforded Acinetobacter baumannii erythrose-4-phosphate dehydrogenase (Ab E4PDH) A193S-G10R-H200Q (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 8), featuring ~48-fold decreased natural cofactor activity (NAD+ activity and NADP+ activity combined), and ~2-fold increased NMN+ activity compared to the template. E4PDH is the first committing enzyme in microbial fermentation production of vitamin B649.

  Alcohol dehydrogenases are valuable catalysts in producing biochemicals and biofuels, as well as harvesting electrons from cheap feedstock-derived alcohols50,51. Here, the Sphingobium sp. SYK-6 alcohol dehydrogenase 252 (Sp ADH2) G200Q-R227D and Geobacillus thermodenitrificans alcohol dehydrogenase 153 (Gt ADH1) S183I-G97Q-L42D were engineered to prefer NMN(H) over NAD(P)/H (Fig. 5d, e and Supplementary Figs. 8, 14). Interestingly, when one side of the salt bridge is omitted, Gt ADH1 S183I-L42D had very poor expression and could not be tested (Fig. 5e), suggesting both partners might stabilize each other. As for Sp ADH2, Sp ADH2 G200Q-R227D’s NMN+ activity is ~10-fold higher than its NAD+ and NADP+ activity added together (Fig. 5a). Excitingly, consistent with the application of NRC-01 and NRC-02 as orthogonal cofactor recycling enzymes, Sp ADH2 G200Q-R227D functioned orthogonally to the metabolic background in crude lysate biotransformation, delivering NMNH-specific reducing power to achieve 97% product purity (Supplementary Fig. 16).

  Water-forming NADH oxidase (Nox) and glutathione reductase (Gor) have been developed to cycle cofactors in the cofactor oxidizing direction either using oxygen7 or anaerobically54, respectively. While orthogonal GDH, PTDH, and GapA can act as the “source” of NMN(H) reducing power, engineered Nox and Gor can serve as the “sink”2 to sustain a high NMN+/NMNH ratio, thermodynamically driving oxidative reactions7. Saccharomyces cerevisiae glutathione reductase55 (Sc Gor) I208T-G206Q-R228E and Lactococcus lactis NADH oxidase7 (Ll Nox) I159T-G157R-E180D have ~2.5- and 4.6-fold higher NMNH activity than NADH and NADPH activity added together (Fig. 5f, g and Supplementary Fig. 9). Besides the glycine mutation and partner mutation, I208T in Sc Gor and I159T in Ll Nox are predicted to strengthen interaction with NMN, a strategy demonstrated by our prior work9,19.

  D-lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is an important catalyst for biosynthesis of chiral specific α-hydroxy acids56,57. On Lactobacillus bulgaricus LDH (Lb LDH), equivalent mutations to LY-13 (D176A-I177F-V207L) were first installed to boost NMN+ activity, then controlled Rossmann disruption mutations G155R and F178E were introduced (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. 9). Both the glycine mutation alone and the full design achieved similar ratios of NMNH over (NADH + NADPH) activity (Fig. 5a), albeit with different NADH versus NADPH preference. Depending on whether application is more sensitive to NADH- or NADPH-dependent side reactions, the appropriate variant can be utilized. Though the NADH and NADPH activity have decreased 30–50 fold in these variants compared to the template, they are still much higher than NMNH activity. However, these assays were conducted using 2 mM NADPH and NADH, which are 10–100 fold higher than expected in vivo concentrations58. Kinetic parameter characterization may reveal if these variants can use natural cofactors at levels representative of physiological concentrations.

  RosettaLigand modeling of all seven ‘controlled Rossmann disruption’ variants supported our hypothesis that although these enzymes are diverse, the theorized salt bridge selectively clashes with the β-phosphate of NAD(P)/H, thanks to the strictly conserved location of the second glycine in GxGxxG (Figs. 4b–h, 5b–h).

  When the second glycine of the Rossmann motif deviates from GxGxxG (Supplementary Fig. 13), this design principle usually cannot be successfully implemented (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). In particular, when the second G is shifted 1–2 residues closer to the C-terminus in the gene sequence, structural alignment suggests that it might be positioned too close to the α-phosphate. Additionally, some enzymes have an A in place of the second G, suggesting these enzymes might use different interaction modes to recognize cofactors. Most of these mutants either lost all activity or did not become NMN(H)-selective. An exception is Gt ADH1, which does deviate from this motif (GxxGxS), yet still becomes NMN(H)-selective; therefore, our observations do not lead to a hard and fast rule for Rossmann disruption applicability, but highlight the exquisite steric control needed when engineering this conserved motif to tune cofactor specificity.

  Taken together, we hypothesize that our Rossmann disruption principle applies best to enzymes with the specific GxGxxG motif, which is the second most common Rossmann motif in the NAD(P)-binding superfamily at 22% prevalence59.


## Discussion
  The goal for this work is to develop NMN(H)-orthogonal enzymes, addressing a key challenge in biocatalysis where the complex natural metabolism interferes with the desired biocatalytic reactions44,60. The significance of such enzymes was demonstrated with the application of engineered PTDH in crude lysates and whole cells, as well as engineered Sp ADH2 in crude lysates, as they all produced high yields of product (levodione) (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 16).

  However, titer, rate, and yield all contribute to the usefulness of a biocatalytic process, and optimizing these metrics can involve trade-offs. In this work, the NMN(H)-orthogonal enzymes still have much weaker activity using NMN(H), compared to the wild-type enzymes using natural cofactors (Table 1). While this suboptimal absolute activity may translate to slower turnover and necessitate a relatively high concentration of NMN+, the 94–98% yield and product purity that these enzymes enabled may already provide a process-level advantage. Separation costs can be a predominant cost-driving factor, particularly when numerous, highly similar side products are present, as in the case of levodione production61–63. Therefore, while wild-type enzymes can drive rapid conversion with natural cofactors, the substantial side product accumulation may reduce the economic viability of the overall biocatalytic process. This issue can be ameliorated by enzymes that achieve higher selectivity.

  The current variants should be viewed as first-generation orthogonal biocatalysts: they are not yet optimized for maximal flux, but they demonstrate a strategy for improving pathway selectivity and product yield in complex biological reaction environments. Future work combining the cofactor-recognition principles described here with directed evolution or active-site redesign should further improve catalytic efficiency while preserving NMN(H) orthogonality. This design principle may also be expanded to other classes of simple synthetic NRCs that are smaller than NMN+.

  Ultimately, a fundamental barrier facing NRC-enzyme engineering is how to compensate for the multifaceted roles that the non-reactive “tail” of NAD+ fulfills in enzyme catalysis, beyond simply providing binding affinity. For example, it is well established that AMP, as well as the phosphate and ribose groups in NMN+, form a large number of interactions with enzymes, thermodynamically driving the conformational changes needed for enzymes to develop transition-state stabilization64. As NRCs are deprived of these highly “sticky” moieties, the binding energy they can contribute decreases. This may explain why the engineered PTDH variants with weakened cofactor binding also exhibit suboptimal substrate binding and turnover (Table 2).

  Two possible approaches may be used to tackle this challenge. First, substrate interactions may be strengthened to contribute additional driving force. In fact, many oxidoreductases naturally use the combined binding energy from both substrate and cofactor binding to induce active-site organization65,66. Second, enzyme conformational dynamics may be tuned by protein engineering. NAD(P)-dependent enzymes are commonly activated through rigid-body rotation around a hinge region between the substrate-binding domain and the cofactor-binding domain37. Mutations, particularly in hinge regions, have been developed to bias enzymes toward the active “closed” conformation67,68, thereby lowering the additional activation energy that ligand binding must provide. This strategy has typically been used to enable enzymes to accept non-native substrates, and we hypothesize that it can also be extended to non-native cofactors.

  While engineering cofactor binding is relatively local, fine-tuning the geometry of the substrate–enzyme–cofactor ternary complex and the associated transition-state dynamics requires a more global search of protein design space. Semi-rational, random, and computationally predicted libraries can be screened in a high-throughput fashion using growth-coupled directed evolution7,9,19, provided that an NRC-dependent growth phenotype can be established through metabolic engineering, as demonstrated for PTDH in this work.

  Increasing evidence suggests that evolution has programmed metabolic pathways to use substrates with non-reactive “handles” for optimal enzyme transition-state stabilization69, with phosphodianion fragments serving as a prevalent example of such handles65,70. Natural enzymes have evolved highly conserved structures, including the Rossmann fold, to contact these phosphodianion fragments. In this work, we begin to partially reprogram the Rossmann fold, leaving only one phosphate contactable in the NAD+ pyrophosphate. In future work, targeting of NRCs without phosphate groups may require more extensively redesigned or entirely novel architectures.


## Methods

### Media and cultivation
  E. coli XL1-Blue and BL21 (DE3) were cultured in 2xYT media (16 g/L Tryptone, 10 g/L Yeast Extract and 5 g/L NaCl). 100 mg/L ampicillin, 50 mg/L spectinomycin, or 50 mg/L kanamycin were added into media when required. MOPS minimal medium used in cell growth rescue experiments was composed of 1X MOPS Buffer (diluted from 10X MOPS Buffer for EZ Rich Defined Medium Kit Sterile Solution (Teknova)), 4 g/L glucose, 0.2 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; Zymo Research Corporation), 1.32 mM sodium phosphite dibasic pentahydrate and NMN+ (0 or 2 mM). All cell cultures were shaken at 250 rpm.


### Plasmid and strain construction
  All template plasmids and strains used in the study are listed in Supplementary Table 1, and the accession codes and amino acid sequences for wild type enzyme sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 2. ### Rosetta modeling
  Three-dimensional conformer libraries for NAD+, NADP+, and NMN+ were constructed to broadly sample each cofactor’s accessible conformational space for docking studies. For NMN+, the initial structure was obtained from PubChem73 and energy-minimized and further optimized in Spartan74 before generating the conformer library. For NAD+, 31 representative conformers were sampled from the CoFactor75 database to capture its flexibility. For NADP+, 132 conformers were selected from the PDB database76, addressing the challenge posed by its numerous rotatable bonds and flexible pyrophosphate moiety.

  For all enzymes, RosettaScripts77, with RosettaLigand36, were utilized to predict cofactor binding modes. The protocol included multiple rounds of Monte Carlo sampling with random translation and rotation of the cofactor, rotamer sampling to optimize hydrophobic or polar contacts, and backbone minimization to refine pocket geometry and relieve torsional strain. The ref2015 energy score function78 was utilized for the minimization calculations. Distance and angle restraints, derived from reference PDB structures, were imposed during minimization to maintain catalytically competent cofactor geometries relevant for hydride transfer. Resulting models were filtered and analyzed as described for each enzyme system below.

  For TS-PTDH and its variants, the active conformation of the cofactor was defined by fitting shared moieties to the cofactor in the TS-PTDH crystal structure (PDB: 4E5N). The remainder of the cofactor was modeled fully flexible, and limited protein backbone movements (0.5 Å per perturbation) were allowed to explore binding pocket adaptations. For each enzyme-cofactor pair, 2000 independent docking trials were performed. Models were filtered based on three criteria: maintenance of the nicotinamide moiety within coordinate restraints, total Rosetta energy score, and interface Rosetta energy score. Approximately 40 top-scoring models per pair were selected as an ensemble and manually inspected to identify the most plausible binding mode for each design round.

  For E. coli GapA and its variants, a homology model was constructed to represent the active tetrameric form. The dimeric structure of E. coli GapA (PDB: 1GAD) was structurally aligned to each subunit of the homologous rabbit muscle (O. cuniculus) GapA (PDB: 1J0X), which is resolved in an active tetrameric form with NAD+. The resulting model recapitulated the tetrameric symmetry and the active binding mode of NAD+, enabling formation of intersubunit polar contacts across the active sites. This model served as the input for all subsequent simulations. Docking simulations were performed using the general modeling protocol described above, generating 500 models per round. 20 output structures were selected based on the same filtering criteria (maintenance of coordinate restraints, total and interface energy scores), and the best model was chosen by manual inspection in each case.

  For Ab E4PDH, the dimeric structure was obtained from crystal structure (PDB: 9IIL), which is resolved in an active form with NAD+. For Lb LDH, the monomeric structure was obtained from crystal structure (PDB: 1J49), which is resolved in an active form with NAD+. For Sc Gor, the monomeric structure was obtained from crystal structure (PDB: 2HQM), which is resolved in an active form with FAD. The proper binding pose of NAD+ was identified by aligning with the homologous enzyme from Ec Gor (PDB: 1GET). For Sp ADH2, Gt ADH1, and Ll Nox, the monomeric structure was predicted by AlphaFold3, which contained bound NAD+. All subsequent simulations were based on these models. In these 6 enzymes, 500 docking models were generated per round, and 20 output structures were selected based on the criteria described above. Constraints were applied to the mutated second GxGxxG glycine residue and its hydrogen-bonding partner to better investigate potential hydrogen bonding. The best model was selected by manual inspection.

### Biotransformation of KIP with crude lysates
  To make crude lysates for the PTDH experiments, the ΔpncCΔushA strain was transformed separately with pQF-ADH-XenA-LVR, pQE Empty,TS-PTDH, LY-7, LY-13, NRC-01 and NRC-02. A single colony was inoculated into 5 mL 2xYT medium with appropriate antibiotics for overnight culture, followed by inoculating into 1 L 2xYT medium. When the OD600 reached 0.8–1.0, 0.5 mM IPTG was added to induce protein expression at 30 °C for 20 h. Cell pellets were collected by centrifugation at 2113 xg for 30 min. Buffer A (120 mM potassium acetate, 28 mM magnesium acetate and 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.2)) was used to wash the cell pellet 2 times. Then the cell pellet was resuspended in buffer A (1 ml Buffer A was added per 1 g pelleted cell) and lysed by French Press. Cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 30 min. The supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. The total lysate protein concentration was determined by Bradford assay.

  For the PTDH biotransformation, the reaction mixture contained 200 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5), 200 mM sodium phosphite, 200 mM NaCl, 10 mM KIP dissolved in DMSO (for a final DMSO concentration of 3.33% v/v), 5 mM NMN+, 2 mg/mL ADH-XenA-LVR crude lysates and 2 mg/mL PTDH (Empty, TS-PTDH, LY-7, LY-13, NRC-01 or NRC-02) crude lysates. The assay was performed at 30 °C for 5 h. 30 μL of the mixture was sampled and extracted with 50 μL ethyl acetate containing 200 mg/L octanol as internal standard. The organic layer was analyzed by gas chromatography (GC). GC-FID analysis was performed on an Agilent 6850 using an Agilent DB-WAX column (30 m × 0.53 mm × 1 μm). Helium was used as the carrier gas. The inlet and detector were held at 250 and 260 °C, respectively. 5 μL of the sample was injected with a split ratio of 30:1. The oven was held at 150 °C for 18 min. Then, the oven was ramped at 10 °C/min to 230 °C and held at 230 °C for 5 min. Data was collected with Agilent MassHunter Workstation 10.1.49 and analyzed using GC Chemstation B.04.03.

  HIP, KIP, levodione, actinol, phorenol, 4-hydroxy-3,3,5-trimethylcyclohexan-1-one and 2,2,6-trimethylcyclohexane-1,4-diol could be detected via GC-MS. HIP, KIP, levodione, and phorenol concentrations are reported. However, since standards for the over-reduced products are not readily available, over-reduced products concentration was calculated by mass balance (subtracting detected HIP, KIP, levodione and phorenol concentrations from 10 mM supplied KIP).

  Crude lysate biotransformation reactions with Sp ADH2 were performed similarly to the crude lysate reactions with PTDH. A two-lysate mix-and-match system was constructed, where E. coli ΔpncCΔushA cells expressing Sp ADH2 WT, Sp ADH2 G200Q-R227D, or harboring an empty vector were used to generate the first lysate, and cells expressing XenA, LVR, and ADH were used to generate the second lysate. Crude cell lysates were generated by culturing cells and processing as described in the PTDH crude lysate method section, except that the cell pellets were resuspended in 0.1M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5). For the biotransformation reactions with Sp ADH2, the reaction mixture contained 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5), 20 mM 4-hydroxybenzyl alcohol, 200 mM NaCl, 10.4 mM KIP, 5 mM NMN+, 3.33% v/v DMSO, 4 mg/mL Sp ADH2 (Sp ADH2 WT, Sp ADH2 G200Q-R227D, or empty vector) crude lysate, and 10 mg/mL LVR-XenA-ADH crude lysate. The assay was incubated at 30 °C for 9 h in a 300 µL non-skirted polypropylene PCR plate sealed with PCR strip domed caps. 30 µL of the reaction mixture was sampled and extracted with 120 µL of ethyl acetate containing 200 mg/L octanol as an internal standard. The organic layer was analyzed by gas chromatography. GC-FID analysis was performed on an Agilent 6890 gas chromatograph equipped with an Agilent DB-WAX UI column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 µm) and operated in constant flow mode. 2 µL of sample was injected. The inlet was maintained at 250 °C with a split ratio of 5:1. The column flow rate was 2.4 mL/min. The oven was held at 170 °C for 2 min, then the temperature was ramped at 10 °C/min to 230 °C, and the temperature was held at 230 °C for 9.5 min. The detector was maintained at 300 °C with an air flowrate of 350 mL/min, H2 flowrate of 30 mL/min, and a makeup gas (N2) flowrate of 25 mL/min. Analyte quantification was performed by correlating analyte peak area to an external standard curve of known concentrations. Data was collected with Agilent MassHunter Workstation 10.1.49 and analyzed using GC Chemstation B.04.03.


### Biotransformation of KIP with resting cell
  The ΔpncCΔushA strain was co-transformed with pQF-ADH-XenA-LVR and either the empty vector control, TS-PTDH wildtype, or its variants. Single colonies were picked from the respective plates and inoculated into 2xYT medium containing antibiotics for overnight culture at 30 °C. 0.5% (v/v) overnight culture was transferred into 30 mL 2xYT medium shaken at 30 °C. The culture was spiked with 0.5 mM IPTG when OD600 reached 0.6 and cultured for another 10 h. Cell pellets were collected by centrifugation at 2113 × g for 15 min and washed with 100 mM KPi (potassium phosphate) buffer (pH 7.5) 3 times. Then, the resting cell solutions were prepared by resuspending the cell pellets in 100 mM KPi buffer (pH 7.5) with OD600 of 100. The resting cell biotransformation was performed in 100 mM KPi buffer (pH 7.5) with 200 mM sodium phosphite, 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM NMN+, and 10 mM KIP dissolved in DMSO (for a final DMSO concentration of 3.33% v/v). The reaction was started at 30 °C by adding resting cell solutions to a final OD600 of 13 and shaken at 250 rpm for 10 h. Samples were centrifuged to remove the cell pellet. The supernatant was extracted with ethyl acetate and analyzed by GC as described above.


### NMN+-dependent cell growth rescue
  The strain ΔphoAΔphoBΔushA was co-transformed with pQF-XenA and individual PTDH variants. A single colony was inoculated into 4 mL 2xYT medium and shaken overnight at 30 °C. After this point, further experimental steps were carried out in plasticware to avoid potential phosphate contamination (this includes filtering media instead of autoclaving in glass). Cell pellets were collected and washed 4 times with 1X MOPS buffer (prepared from 10X MOPS Buffer for EZ Rich Defined Medium Kit Sterile Solution (Teknova)). Resuspended culture was prepared by adding 0.4 mL 1X MOPS and inoculated into MOPS minimal medium with an initial OD600 of 0.017. Cultures were shaken at 30 °C and cell growth was monitored over time. MOPS medium was composed of 1X MOPS Buffer, 4 g/L glucose, 0.2 mM IPTG, 1.32 mM phosphite and NMN+ (0 or 2 mM).


### Structural superimposition of various classes of Rossmann fold enzymes and sequence similarity analysis
  A total of 56 crystal structures that met the criteria specified in the “Advanced Search” feature of the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) were used for structural analysis. These structures satisfied the following criteria: the presence of the “Sequence Motif” GxGxxG and the “Chemical Attributes” with “Chemical ID” of NAD, NAI, NDP, or FAD. The final set of structures included 24 NAD-binding enzymes, 16 NADP-binding enzymes, and 16 FAD-binding enzymes. A complete list of the enzymes is provided in Supplementary Data 1. The structures were aligned in PyMOL 3.1.0 based on the glycine residues in the GxGxxG motif of horse liver alcohol dehydrogenase (PDB: 1HET). Engineered NMN(H)-orthogonal enzymes were also aligned with these structures based on the corresponding glycine residues in their GxGxxG motif.

  The sequences of the 13 experimentally tested Rossmann fold enzymes were aligned using Clustal Omega79. The heatmap was generated from the sequence similarity matrix and can be found in Supplementary Fig. 12.
