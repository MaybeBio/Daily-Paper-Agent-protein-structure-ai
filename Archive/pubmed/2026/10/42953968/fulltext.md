# Metal-ion coordination drives active-site remodeling for backsplicing in circularly permuted group II introns


## Abstract
  Circularly permuted (CP) group II introns are self-splicing ribozymes that undergo a sequential branching and backsplicing reaction to yield branched introns and circular exons (circRNAs). While the initial branching step is structurally characterized, the mechanistic principles that govern the subsequent large-scale active-site remodeling and stringent metal-ion specificity required for backsplicing remain poorly understood. Here, we present a series of high-resolution cryo-EM structures of the CP group II intron from Gluconacetobacter diazotrophicus PAL5 (Gdi-1), capturing the ribozyme at discrete stages along the branching -to- backsplicing pathway under distinct ionic conditions. Comparative analyses under K+/Mg2+, K+/Ca2+, and Cs+/Mg2+ conditions demonstrate that productive backsplicing relies on ion-specific coordination networks that govern 3′ splice site (3′SS) docking and catalytic center formation, revealing the precise metal-ion-coordinated conformational rearrangements and dynamic splice-site exchange mechanisms. Crucially, we identify a previously unrecognized, highly selective monovalent cation-binding site, K3, that structurally couples the γ–γ′ interaction to reassembly of the heteronuclear four-metal core, thereby promoting productive second-step active-site formation. Together, these findings establish a structural basis for metal-ion-dependent active-site remodeling during group II intron catalysis and provide mechanistic insights into metal-ion selectivity in circRNA biogenesis.


## Introduction
  Group II introns are self-splicing ribozymes broadly classified into three subclasses, IIA, IIB, and IIC, based on conserved structural motifs and distinct exon recognition mechanisms [1–3]. A specialized subgroup, termed circularly permuted (CP) group II introns, has been identified in diverse bacterial lineages [4, 5], and one of them from Comamonas testosteroni KF-1 (Cte-1) has recently been characterized using cryo-EM [6, 7]. These CP group II introns exhibit a unique domain arrangement in which the p1 and p2 subdomains of Domain IV (D4), together with Domain V (D5), Domain VI (D6), and the 3′ exon, are repositioned to the 5′ end, whereas the remainder of D4 is located at the 3′ terminus (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S4 A). As a result, the exon region remains sequestered within the central ribozyme core. Recognition of the 5′ exon relies on a single IBS-EBS interaction, whereas the 3′ exon is positioned through the δ-δ′ interaction with the auxiliary sequence (AUX) (Supplementary Fig. S4 A and C). Located immediately upstream of the EBS region, AUX contributes additional tertiary interactions that reinforce exon binding, analogous to the auxiliary role of maturase in canonical group II introns [8] or Prp8 in the spliceosome [9]. Although CP group II introns retain the two-step transesterification self-splicing pathway, their unique domain permutation produces both a branched product (BP or T-large) and circular exons (circRNAs) through sequential branching and backsplicing reactions (Fig. 1A).

  In the first transesterification step, the conserved bulged adenosine within D6 attacks the 5′ splice site (5′SS) through its 2′ hydroxyl, generating a branched intermediate and recapitulating the canonical chemistry of spliceosome branching during eukaryotic pre-mRNA splicing [10]. Efficient branch formation requires precise positioning of D6 to present the branch-point adenosine (bpA) in a catalytically competent orientation [11, 12]. Structural studies across distinct group II introns have progressively revealed that D6 adopts a branching-competent “up” conformation stabilized by specific tertiary interactions with D1, as well as by interactions with the intron-encoded protein [8]. Within D6, internal base-pairing interactions further constrain the bpA, orienting its 2′-OH for nucleophilic attack on the 5′SS scissile phosphate [13]. Concurrently, cooperative metal-ion coordination organizes the reactive groups within the catalytic center to support the first-step reaction.

  Following branching catalysis, extensive remodeling of the ribozyme is required to establish a catalytically competent active site for the second transesterification, wherein the liberated 3′-OH of 5′ exon executes a nucleophilic attack on the 3′SS [14]. Structural studies across diverse group II introns have established that progression toward the second step is accompanied by extensive rearrangement of D6 and coordinated substrate repositioning within the catalytic core [6–8]. Notably, crystallographic trapping of an Oi5eD1-5 group IIC intron under Li+/Mg2+ or Na+/Mg2+ conditions captures a distinct toggled conformation, implicating metal-ion coordination as a critical determinant of post-first-step remodeling [15, 16]. Comparative analyses of pre-2S and post-2S states, together with SHAPE-MaP chemical probing experiments, further suggested that assembly of the second-step active site requires formation of the γ–γ′ tertiary interaction and reorganization of the catalytic triplex [17–19]. These studies highlight the highly dynamic nature of second-step activation, pointing to an intimate interplay between RNA tertiary conformational transitions and localized metal-ion coordination networks. However, the precise mechanisms by which these processes are cooperatively coupled to establish a catalytically competent active center and promote productive second-step reaction remain fundamentally unresolved.

  Here, we report a comprehensive set of high-resolution cryo-EM structures of the CP group II intron from Gluconacetobacter diazotrophicus PAL5 (Gdi-1), capturing the ribozyme at discrete stages along the branching-to-backsplicing pathway under distinct cationic conditions. These structures reveal how the dynamic remodeling of RNA tertiary interactions and ion-specific coordination networks cooperatively drive assembly of the productive second-step catalytic center. Importantly, we identify a previously unrecognized monovalent cation-binding site, K3, that promotes the γ–γ′ interaction, mechanically coupling productive 3′SS docking to the functional reassembly of the catalytic heteronuclear metal-ion core network. Together, these findings establish a structural basis for metal-ion-dependent active-site remodeling during backsplicing step for circRNA biogenesis and provide mechanistic insight into the coupling between metal-ion selectivity and active-site assembly in group II intron catalysis.


## Materials and methods

### RNA in vitro transcription and purification
  The Gdi-1 CP group II intron sequence (GenBank: NC_010124.1) was cloned into pUC-57 and amplified with primers for both wild-type and mutant RNAs (Supplementary Table S1). In vitro transcription (IVT) was carried out at 37°C for 1 h in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9), 0.01% Triton X-100, 12 mM MgCl2, 2 mM spermidine, 10 mM DTT, 5 mM each NTP, 1 U/μl RNase inhibitor, 10 U/μl T7 RNA polymerase, and 3 μM DNA template to prevent self-splicing [20]. The reaction was mixed with 2× denaturing loading buffer (95% formamide, 0.025% SDS, 10 mM EDTA, 0.025% xylene cyanol, 0.025% bromophenol blue), resolved on a 4% (19:1 acrylamide:bis) 8 M urea PAGE at 15 W for 3 h, and visualized under 254 nm UV. RNA bands were excised and eluted overnight at 4°C in RNase-free water containing 300 mM NaOAc and 50 mM EDTA, filtered through 0.22 μm membranes, precipitated with isopropanol, washed twice with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in RNase-free water. Integrity was confirmed by denaturing 6% urea PAGE, concentration measured by Nanodrop, and samples stored at −80°C.


### Gdi-1 CP group II intron in vitro self-splicing assay
  Purified wild-type and mutant Gdi-1 pre-RNA at a final concentration of 0.05 μM was annealed by heating to 80°C for 1 min, then gradually cooled to 37°C (0.5°C/s) in 40 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 100 mM KCl or CsCl. For self-splicing assays, MgCl2 or CaCl2 was added to 5, 20, or 100 mM and reactions incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Splicing-kinetics assays were performed at 37°C in 5 mM MgCl2 for the indicated times. Reactions were quenched with an equal volume of denaturing buffer (100 mM EDTA, 8 M urea, 0.1% SDS, 0.025% xylene cyanol, 0.025% bromophenol blue) and resolved on 6% (19:1 acrylamide:bis) 8 M urea gels at 15 W for 110 min. Gels were stained with 1× SYBR Gold and imaged on a Typhoon FLA 9500. Circular RNA (circRNA) and branched intermediate (BI) bands were quantified by ImageJ, normalized to the wild-type (5 mM MgCl2) control, and statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism v10.0.


### Cryo-EM sample preparation
  0.5 µM RNA was annealed as described above, incubated with 20 mM MgCl2 or CaCl2 at 37°C for 30 min in a 1.5 ml reaction volume. Samples were immediately chilled on ice and concentrated to ∼15 µM using pre-chilled 100 kDa-cutoff centrifugal filters (Millipore). RNA integrity was verified by denaturing 6% (19:1 acrylamide:bis) 8 M urea PAGE. Concentrated RNA was supplemented with 0.01% n-octyl-β-D-glucopyranoside (β-OG) and applied to glow-discharged Quantifoil® R1.2/1.3 Cu 300-mesh + 2nmC grids. Grids were blotted for 1.5 s at 4°C (100% humidity) and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific).


### Cryo-EM data collection and image processing
  All datasets were acquired using a Thermo Fisher Krios G4 cryo–electron microscope operating at 300 kV under identical microscope settings. The instrument was equipped with a Falcon 4i summit direct detector (Thermo Fisher), and automated data collection was performed via EPU and SerialEM software using the beam-image shift method. Microscope magnification was at ×130 000 (corresponding to a calibrated sampling of 0.959 Å per pixel). Each movie covered a defocus range from −1.0 μm to −2.0 μm. Movie stacks were dose-fractionated into 40 frames, with a total exposure dose of ∼50 e−/Å2.

  For Gdi-1 full-length RNA self-splicing in K+/Ca2+ conditions, 10 548 movie stacks were collected and motion–corrected using patch motion correction. After Patch CTF estimation, 10 465 micrographs were processed using Blob picking and template picking for automatic particle selection. A total of 323 703 particles (box size 256 × 256 pixels) were extracted and subjected to two rounds of reference-free 2D classification in cryoSPARC v4 [21, 22]. Subsequently, 261 597 were selected for ab initio reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement to generate an initial model and discard low-quality particles. All viable particles then underwent multi-round 3D classification, which resulted in 181 104 particles and 26 740 particles corresponding to the pre-branching (K+/Ca2+) and pre-backsplicing (K+/Ca2+) states, respectively. High–resolution maps were then obtained through homogeneous refinement, non–uniform refinement and CTF/local refinement (Supplementary Fig. S1).

  For Gdi-1 full-length RNA self-splicing in K+/Mg2+ conditions, 10 693 movie stacks were collected and processed as mentioned above. After 2D classification, 953 586 particles were subjected to ab initio reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement, resulting in an initial model containing 831 770 particles. The initial 3D classification was performed primarily based on the up and down conformations of D6. To ensure that each class contained homogeneous, high-quality particles representing a single conformational state, a focused mask covering the central density region was subsequently generated. Multiple rounds of focused 3D classification further separated distinct conformational populations, ultimately yielding 259 891 particles corresponding to the post-branching (K+/Mg2+) state, 331 267 particles corresponding to the pre-backsplicing (K+/Mg2+) state, and 72 743 particles corresponding to the Branched Product (K+/Mg2+) state (Supplementary Fig. S2).

  For Gdi-1 full-length RNA self-splicing under Cs+/Mg2+ conditions, 10 130 movie stacks were collected and processed as described above. A total of 712 875 particles were extracted and subjected to 2D and 3D classification, yielding three major structural states corresponding to the post-branching (Cs+/Mg2+), pre-backsplicing (Cs+/Mg2+), and branched product (Cs+/Mg2+) states (Supplementary Fig. S3).

  Subsequently, local resolution estimation was conducted. The final maps were unsharpened, in case of overfitting, and employed for subsequent structural analyses. The reported resolutions above are based on the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) 0.143 criterion. All the visualization and evaluation of 3D density maps were performed with UCSF Chimera [23] and ChimeraX [24]. Detailed cryo-EM data processing workflows are outlined as indicated in Supplementary Figs S1–S3.


### Cryo-EM model building and refinement
  De novo atomic models were first built for the Gdi-1 structures obtained under K+/Mg2+ conditions using conserved secondary structural features, including D5 and IBS-EBS interactions. All cryo-EM maps were sharpened using EMReady [25] prior to model building. The resulting models were used as templates for model building of the remaining structural states and ionic conditions. For poorly resolved regions, RNA 3D structure predictions [26, 27] were used to guide model construction, followed by manual adjustment in Coot [28]. All models were refined against the corresponding cryo-EM maps using Phenix.real_space_refine [29]. The refined models yielded CCmask values ranging from 0.79 to 0.89 (Supplementary Table S2 and S3). Metal-ion coordination geometry was restrained according to established ionic radii and coordination distances [30], with target distances of 2.13 Å for Mg2+, 2.30 Å for Ca2+, 2.90 Å for K+, and 3.20 Å for Cs+. Final models were validated by MolProbity [31].


### Figure preparation
  Figures, illustrations in the article were drawn using PyMol, ChimeraX, GraphPad Prism v.10.0 and Adobe Illustrator 2024.


## Results

### Ion-dependent structural states of the Gdi-1 CP group II intron
  Previous studies demonstrated that CP group II introns represent a distinct subgroup of group II introns that undergo ion-catalyzed self-splicing through sequential folding, branching, and backsplicing. The branching and backsplicing reactions correspond to the first and second transesterification steps (1S and 2S) of canonical group II intron splicing (Fig. 1A) and share the same catalytic mechanism [5, 7]. Notably, distinct combinations of monovalent and divalent ions promote self-splicing to different stages of the reaction, with the backsplicing step (2S) showing particularly strong ion dependence. In vitro self-splicing assays of the Gdi-1 CP group II intron revealed different degrees of self-splicing under K+/Ca2+, K+/Mg2+, and Cs+/Mg2+ conditions (Fig. 1A and B). K+/Mg2+ supported efficient completion of both splicing steps, with substantial precursor consumption, minimal accumulation of the branched intermediate (BI), and robust formation of branched product (BP) and circular exon (C). In contrast, K+/Ca2+ permitted branching but strongly impaired subsequent backsplicing, as indicated by substantial BI accumulation without detectable BP or circular exon formation. Cs+/Mg2+ supported completion of both steps but resulted in greater BI accumulation and lower product levels than K+/Mg2+. Time-course analysis further revealed transient BI accumulation and rapid circRNA formation under K+/Mg2+ conditions, whereas sustained BI accumulation and delayed circRNA formation were observed under Cs+/Mg2+ conditions (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Together, these results indicate that Cs+ can substitute for K+ to support completion of the pathway, but slows progression relative to K+/Mg2+ due to its larger ionic radius.

  To investigate how ionic conditions influence CP group II intron self-splicing, we solved cryo-EM structures of full-length Gdi-1 pre-RNA under K+/Ca2+, K+/Mg2+, and Cs+/Mg2+ conditions. Under each ionic condition, two major conformations distinguished by D6 orientation were observed. The intron adopted a compact architecture stabilized by extensive tertiary interactions, with its secondary structure and major long-range interactions summarized in Fig. 1C. To facilitate structural analysis [32], post-branching (D6-up) and pre-backsplicing (D6-down) states were annotated according to the Leontis-Westhof RNA interaction nomenclature [33], with all interactions supported by well-resolved cryo-EM densities (Supplementary Fig. S4A and C). Focused classification and refinement resolved five distinct structures within the branched intermediate stage (Fig. 1D), encompassing post-branching states under K+/Mg2+ (2.58 Å) and Cs+/Mg2+ (2.42 Å) conditions, and pre-backsplicing states under K+/Ca2+ (3.20 Å), K+/Mg2+ (2.53 Å), and Cs+/Mg2+ (2.59 Å) conditions. In addition, we resolved a pre-branching state under K+/Ca2+ (3.00 Å) and branched product states under both K+/Mg2+ (2.86 Å) and Cs+/Mg2+ (2.82 Å), revealing that distinct ionic environments drive different structural states during self-splicing. Under K+/Mg2+ conditions, the reaction rapidly proceeds to completion, allowing us to capture productive post-branching and pre-backsplicing states. K+/Ca2+ supports branch formation but stalls prior to backsplicing, allowing us to capture productive pre-branching and stalled pre-backsplicing states. Cs+/Mg2+ slows the overall reaction while still allowing us to capture productive states. The use of cesium also provides increased confidence in assigning monovalent metal-ion positions within the cryo-EM maps, which is similar to previous crystallographic studies using thallium and ytterbium [16].


### Functional roles of the 5′ and 3′ portions of D4 in Gdi-1 CP group II intron
  CP group II introns rely on extensive tertiary interactions to stabilize folding and promote efficient self-splicing [6, 7]. Beyond previously identified conserved core contacts, our analyses reveal structural roles for the two separated portions of D4 generated by circular permutation. In Gdi-1, the split D4 comprises a transposed 5′ portion containing the p1 and p2 hairpins [5, 34] and a remaining 3′ portion. Structural analysis identified kissing-loop interactions between D4p1 and D2, as well as between D4p2 and D3 in Gdi-1 CP group II intron (Supplementary Fig. S5E and F), which collectively stabilize the global ribozyme architecture. Interestingly, although the sequences in D4p1 and D4p2 involved in kissing-loop formation are exactly the same, in vitro splicing assays showed that the p2/p1 sequence-swap construct, together with the Δp1 and Δp1/p2 truncation variants, exhibited reduced self-splicing activity (Supplementary Fig. S5H), indicating that the stem lengths and spatial organization of p1 and p2 are critical for assembly of the catalytically competent conformation.

  The extended 3′ terminus of CP group II introns forms part of D4 but lacks the protein-coding region found in many bacterial group II introns, in which D4 contains an open reading frame encoding the intron-encoded protein (IEP; maturase) involved in splicing and lariat retrotransposition [35–39]. Structural analysis revealed an extensive ω–ω′ tertiary interaction in which nucleotides G831-G839 in the 3′D4 pair with U50-U58 located between 5′ D4 and D5. This arrangement recapitulates the D4 stem architecture observed in canonical group II introns, indicating that the U50-U58 segment likewise belongs to the split D4 (Supplementary Fig. S5D). In vitro self-splicing assays showed that disruption of the ω–ω′ interaction markedly reduced splicing activity, whereas constructs retaining this interaction remained fully active (Supplementary Fig. S5G), indicating that the ω–ω′ interaction contributes to stabilization of D5. In addition, low-threshold cryo-EM density maps revealed that the unmodeled D4 extension associates with the exon region before exon release but becomes disordered following backsplicing (Supplementary Fig. S5A and B), suggesting a possible role in product release. Together, these results establish essential structural roles for both terminal regions in supporting CP group II intron self-splicing.


### Structural transition from the post-branching to the pre-backsplicing intermediate
  We determined two consecutive intermediate states following the branching reaction. The first intermediate was captured under both K+/Mg2+ and Cs+/Mg2+ conditions and corresponds to a previously uncharacterized post-branching state of the CP group II intron (Fig. 1D). In this structure, the phosphodiester bond between U208 and G209 at the 5′SS is cleaved, accompanied by formation of a 2′–5′ phosphodiester linkage between the branch point adenosine A128 (bpA) and G209. Both features are clearly resolved in the cryo-EM density map (Fig. 2C). In addition, G209 forms a trans Watson–Crick/Watson–Crick G–G noncanonical base pair with G555(AUX), while the nucleobases of bpA and G210 insert into the D6 minor groove lined by the G102-C126 base pair and the adjacent C103-U125 mismatch, forming the conserved π–π′ interaction (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Together, these contacts position G209, G210, and G555 in an approximately collinear geometry (Fig. 2B and C). This structural feature is also observed in the pre-branching state of Gdi-1 (Supplementary Fig. S7A) and in the previously reported Cte-1 structure [7], indicating that the D6-up architecture persists transiently after the first transesterification reaction. A similar retention of branch-site organization has been reported for the yeast spliceosomal Complex C immediately after branching [40].

  The subsequent intermediate corresponds to the pre-backsplicing state and was captured under all three ionic conditions (K+/Ca2+, K+/Mg2+, and Cs+/Mg2+) (Fig. 1D). The covalent 2′–5′ phosphodiester linkage (branched bond) between bpA and G209 is retained despite extensive remodeling of the catalytic architecture associated with the downward repositioning of D6 (Fig. 2A and D). During this transition, bpA undergoes an ∼20 Å clockwise rotation, carrying the covalently linked G209 away from the catalytic center as the 3′SS is docked into the active site (Supplementary Fig. S6A). bpA, G209, and G210 establish a stacking arrangement, compensating for the energetic penalty arising from dissociation of their pre-existing mutual anchoring interactions formed within the branching conformation [27]. Within this rearranged architecture, the first nucleotide of the 3′ exon, C135 (IBS3), is recognized by G555 through a Watson–Crick base pair, forming the conserved δ–δ′ interaction. In contrast, the adjacent U134 at the 3′ terminus of D6 orients oppositely and forms a stable Watson–Crick base pair with A722 of the D2 and D3 junction (J2/3) under both K+/Mg2+ and Cs+/Mg2+ conditions (Fig. 2E and F), thereby constraining the 3′SS into a highly distorted, buckled conformation directed toward the catalytic core.

  Despite extensive remodeling of D6 and the catalytic center during the transition from the post-branching to the pre-backsplicing state, the exon-recognition architecture remains essentially unchanged (Fig. 2G). The 5′ exon is anchored through IBS that pair with EBS and AUX elements located within terminal loops of D1, forming a stable recognition platform. At the distal end of this platform, IBS, EBS, and AUX nucleotides assemble into a three-layer triple helix (Fig. 2B, E and Supplementary Fig. S6E, G), whereas the proximal region is maintained primarily through EBS-IBS base complementarity. This organization positions G555 directly beneath the terminal EBS-IBS base pair, enabling its sequential engagement with G209 in the post-branching state and C135 in the pre-backsplicing state. Consistent with the conserved 5′ exon anchoring mode observed in spliceosome C and C* complexes [40, 41], the 3′-OH of the 5′ exon retains an almost identical spatial location between the two states, remaining poised in a catalytically favorable geometry for the second transesterification reaction.

  Structural superposition revealed modest positional shifts of D1p9 and D1p11 during the transition between the post-branching and pre-backsplicing conformations, associated with the alternative positioning of D6 in the two states (Fig. 2G and Supplementary Fig. S6C). In the D6-up state, A460 of D1p11 extends outward to form an A-hook insertion motif that engages the terminal GAAA tetraloop of D6. Specifically, the A460 nucleobase inserts between A114 and A115, establishes a non-Watson–Crick contact with the sugar edge of G112, and participates in a four-nucleotide stacking platform composed of A113, A460, A114, and A115 (Fig. 2H). This conserved tertiary interaction, termed η-η′up, is characteristic of CP group II introns and contributes critically to branching catalysis. Upon downward flipping of D6, the D1p9 subdomain reorients toward the minor groove of the D6 helix, allowing its GAAA tetraloop to dock through canonical GNRA tetraloop-receptor interactions [42]. A pronounced U-turn spanning G382-A385 positions the terminal adenosines to form hydrogen-bonding interactions with three consecutive G-C base pairs in the minor groove, thereby establishing the η-η′down interaction (Fig. 2I). Together, these observations suggest that distinct D6 conformations in CP group II introns are stabilized by alternative RNA tertiary interactions. We generated mutants disrupting η-η′up (Δ457–460) and η-η′down (Δ382–385). Both mutants exhibited reduced splicing efficiency relative to the wild-type (WT) intron (Supplementary Fig. S6D and F). Disruption of η-η′up nearly abolished BI and circular exon formation, diverting the precursor RNA toward a hydrolytic pathway [43]. In contrast, disruption of η-η′down resulted in substantial BI accumulation and reduced circRNA production, consistent with impaired progression to backsplicing. As illustrated in the proposed model (Supplementary Fig. S6B), these findings support coordinated roles for η-η′up and η-η′down in splice-site exchange at the catalytic core.


### Conserved peripheral scaffold surrounding the catalytic core
  In vitro splicing assays demonstrated that distinct combinations of monovalent and divalent ions support progression through different stages of the Gdi-1 branching pathway. Having characterized the structural transitions between the post-branching and pre-backsplicing intermediates, we next examined the metal-ion architecture surrounding the catalytic center. Comparison of six structures representing the pre-branching (Fig. 3A), post-branching (Fig. 3B and C) and pre-backsplicing states (Fig. 3D–F) revealed that the peripheral RNA framework remains largely conserved despite substantial local remodeling of the catalytic core accompanying D6 repositioning. Consistent with canonical group II introns, the catalytic center is organized around the conserved two-nucleotide bulge (2-nt bulge) and catalytic triad of D5, with J2/3 nucleotides contributing to the formation of the catalytic triplex [44]. Positioned above the catalytic core, the phosphodiester backbone of G210-G211 within the 5′SS region adopts an extended conformation, while G211–G213 together with A432 and U433 form an arch-like framework (Fig. 3A–F). Despite substantial rearrangement of G209 and G210 during the transition from branching to pre-backsplicing, this peripheral architecture remains largely preserved. Within this peripheral architecture, nucleotides A432–U437 adopt a sharply kinked backbone and establish tertiary contacts with distinct regions of the intron, closely resembling the Z-anchor previously described in group IIC introns [45, 46]. G435 and C436 form hydrogen-bonding contacts with C212 and G211, respectively, constituting the ε-ε′ interaction, whereas U434 forms a base triple with the C70-G81 pair in the catalytic core, establishing the λ–λ′ interaction (Supplementary Fig. S7A and B). A432 stacks with G211-G213 and forms a noncanonical Hoogsteen-edge interaction with U437. Together, these interactions stabilize the peripheral scaffold surrounding the catalytic core.

  The conserved peripheral RNA scaffold is further stabilized by metal ions. Two divalent metal-ion sites, M3 and M5, are consistently observed across the six structures (Supplementary Table S4). M3 is coordinated by the phosphate groups of A432, U433, and U207 of the 5′ exon through a triangular arrangement of non-bridging oxygens, contributing to the stable positioning of the 5′ exon at the catalytic center. M5 is coordinated by the phosphate groups of G435 and C436, forming a phosphate-bound metal clamp that stabilizes the sharply kinked backbone of the nucleotides A432-U437 (Supplementary Fig. S7A and B). A monovalent-ion site (K2/Cs2) is located adjacent to the D1 5′SS region, where it remains associated with the phosphate groups of G211-G213 despite subtle changes in coordination geometry among catalytic states, consistent with a structural role in stabilizing the negatively charged RNA backbone around the 5′SS [16, 39]. In the K+/Mg2+ pre-backsplicing state, an additional metal ion, M4, establishes contacts with phosphate groups of G211 and C212, potentially complementing K2 in stabilizing the remodeled D1 5′SS backbone (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. S8A–C). Such local variations in metal-ion coordination occur without disrupting the overall peripheral framework surrounding the active site, consistent with subtle metal-ion rearrangements observed during self-splicing of the Tetrahymena group I intron [47]. Together, these conserved RNA interactions and metal-ion sites maintain a stable peripheral scaffold during remodeling of the catalytic core (Fig. 3G–I).


### State-dependent metal-ion coordination during branching reaction
  Previous structural studies demonstrated that the two-metal-ion catalytic center, together with the evolutionarily conserved second-shell K1 and K2 cations [48], originated in group II introns and has been retained in the spliceosome [49], providing a unified framework for understanding metal-ion coordination during branching catalysis. We next examined the organization of metal ions within the catalytic center in the pre-branching state. D6 adopts an upward conformation that positions the bpA adjacent to the active site (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Catalytic metal M1 precisely positions the nucleophilic 2′-OH of bpA within activation distance (2.2 Å) of the scissile phosphate between U208 and G209, priming it for nucleophilic attack. M1 is further stabilized through inner-sphere coordination with the catalytic triad. The U208-G209 scissile phosphate bridges M1 and M2 through its pro-Rp oxygen, whereas the 3′-bridging oxygen directly contacts M2. M2 is additionally coordinated by the 2-nt bulge, thereby stabilizing the geometry of the scissile phosphate during branching (Fig. 4B and G). Two monovalent ions, K1 and K2, are positioned adjacent to the catalytic metals. K1 coordinates phosphate oxygens from A61, G62, and C84, together with the O6 of G723, establishing an electrostatic network surrounding the catalytic center, whereas K2 occupies a more peripheral position through interactions with G213 and A722, contributing to stabilization of the sharply kinked 5′ splice junction. Together, K1, K2, M1, and M2 constitute a heteronuclear four-metal core that establishes a branching-competent active-site arrangement (Fig. 4A, G and Supplementary Fig. S7E). Notably, the arrangement of these four ions closely resembles that observed in the crystallographic pre-1S hydrolytic structure of the O. iheyensis group IIC intron (PDB: 4FAQ) (Fig. 4I) [16], revealing a conserved heteronuclear metal-ion architecture among group II introns.

  Following branch formation, the overall architecture of the catalytic center remains largely preserved. K1 and K2 occupy positions nearly identical to those observed in the pre-branching state, whereas M1 is no longer detected. M2 remains coordinated to the newly generated U208 3′-OH, with two additional coordinating water molecules resolved in the local density (Fig. 4D, E and H). Formation of the 2′–5′ branched linkage is accompanied by local displacement of the branch-point region away from the catalytic center, disrupting the coordination geometry required for M1 binding and resulting in its release. Thus, the catalytic metal-ion network undergoes selective remodeling, while the remaining ions maintain an essentially unchanged coordination network. The post-branching state captured under Cs+/Mg2+ conditions exhibits ion-binding patterns highly similar to those observed in the K+/Mg2+ state (Supplementary Fig. S7C, D, F and G), further supporting the conservation of the metal-ion binding mode in the post-branching state. Schematic representations of the pre- and post-branching coordination networks are shown in Fig. 4C and F. The local structural quality underlying these active-site interpretations was further assessed by local-resolution and atomic Q-score analyses (Supplementary Fig. S9).


### Reassembly of the heteronuclear four-metal core during transition to the pre-backsplicing state
  Following partial disassembly of the heteronuclear four-metal core after branching, we next investigated how the catalytic metal network is reassembled during transition to the pre-backsplicing state under distinct ionic conditions (K+/Ca2+, K+/Mg2+, and Cs+/Mg2+). Local-resolution and atomic Q-score analyses of the three pre-backsplicing catalytic centers are shown in Supplementary Fig. S9. K1 is retained in all conformational states except under K+/Ca2+ conditions (Supplementary Table S4), where only weak residual density is observed at lower map thresholds, insufficient to support confident ion assignment (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. S8D). The absence of a stably resolved K1 density is consistent with previous proposals that protonation of the first pyrimidine in the catalytic triad following branching triggers transient K1 release during assembly of the second-step active site [50]. Under both K+/Mg2+ and Cs+/Mg2+ conditions (Fig. 5B and C), K1 reoccupies its conserved site through coordination with A61, G62, C84, and G723, thereby restoring the electrostatic environment of the catalytic chamber and supporting formation of the M1-binding pocket.

  Beyond K1, we identified an additional density near the catalytic center that is observed exclusively in pre-backsplicing intermediates. Located between C84 of the 2-nt bulge, G210 in the 5′SS region, and U134 of the 3′SS, this density resides immediately adjacent to both K1 and M1, extending the ion-coordination network toward the docked 3′SS region. The corresponding distances to C84 (OP1), G210 (O6), and U134 (O2 and O2′) range from 2.8 to 3.1 Å (Fig. 5B), consistent with assignment as a monovalent ion. Notably, a similar monovalent-ion site was previously observed in the O.iD1-5 structure, where its proximity to functionally important elements of the catalytic core led to the proposal that it might participate in the second step of splicing [51]. Although its role could not be directly assessed in the previous study, the close positional correspondence with the site identified here prompted us to designate this putative second-step specific monovalent-ion site as K3. To further verify the identity of this site, we determined the pre-backsplicing structure under Cs+/Mg2+ conditions. A strong density was observed at the equivalent position, where a Cs+ ion (Cs3) occupies the K3 site and engages the same set of ligands observed in the K+/Mg2+ structure, albeit with longer coordination distances consistent with the larger ionic radius of Cs+ (Fig. 5C). The preservation of this binding site under ion substitution provides independent structural support for the existence of K3. In contrast, no corresponding density is detected in the K+/Ca2+ structure (Fig. 5A), indicating that occupancy of the K3 site is associated with backsplicing-competent active-site organization.

  Previous studies have established that both transesterification reactions utilize a common catalytic core and metal-binding platform [8]. Given the distinct monovalent-ion binding patterns observed under different ionic conditions, we next compared the occupancy and coordination of the catalytic divalent ions in the three pre-backsplicing structures. Comparison of the K+/Ca2+ and K+/Mg2+ structures revealed a striking difference at the M2 site. In both structures, M1 occupies the conserved catalytic position and is coordinated by A61 and G62 of the catalytic triad together with C84 of the 2-nt bulge. M1 simultaneously engages the scissile phosphate between U134 (3′SS) and C135 (3′ exon), thereby stabilizing the distorted phosphate geometry required for catalysis (Fig. 5D and E). A notable difference is observed in the coordination of the C84 bulged phosphate. In the K+/Ca2+ structure, both of its non-bridging oxygens coordinate M1, whereas in the K+/Mg2+ structure they coordinate M1 and M2, respectively. A well-defined density for M2 is observed only in the K+/Mg2+ structure. M2 forms inner-sphere contacts with the non-bridging oxygens of C82 and C84 and is positioned between the nucleophilic 3′-OH of U208 and the pro-Rp oxygen of the U134-C135 scissile phosphate, positioning M2 to facilitate nucleophile activation and leaving-group stabilization. Together with two additional water molecules, these ligands complete a canonical six-coordinate octahedral geometry around M2 [52]. No corresponding density is detected under K+/Ca2+ conditions, indicating failure to establish a catalytically competent M2 site. Given the distinct coordination properties of Mg2+ and Ca2+, the inability to stabilize M2 likely underlies the failure of Ca2+ to support backsplicing.

  Unexpectedly, no unambiguous density corresponding to M1 is observed in the Cs+/Mg2+ structure; however, the structure retains a properly docked 3′SS and a catalytically competent M2 site whose coordination geometry closely matches that observed under K+/Mg2+ conditions (Fig. 5F). Notably, the K3 site is occupied by Cs3 in this structure. Given its close proximity to the docked 3′SS, occupancy of the K3 site by Cs+ is likely sufficient to maintain productive 3′SS docking despite the absence of a stably bound M1 ion. The larger ionic radius of Cs+ may alter the local metal-ion organization around the catalytic center and thereby reduce the stability of M1 occupancy. This altered active-site organization may be related to prolonged accumulation of pre-backsplicing intermediates and slower backsplicing under Cs+/Mg2+ conditions in vitro (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S4B). These findings align with previous studies on RNA-associated cations [52, 53]. Together, these structures demonstrate that reassembly of the pre-backsplicing heteronuclear four-metal core is highly sensitive to the identities of both monovalent and divalent ions. Distinct ion-binding patterns generate alternative active-site arrangements (Supplementary Fig. S8E–J), providing a structural basis for understanding how metal-ion coordination regulates backsplicing competence.


### K3 promotes active-site assembly through stabilization of the γ–γ′ interaction during backsplicing
  We next compared the local organization of the docked 3′SS among the three pre-backsplicing structures under distinct ionic conditions. Viewed together, the monovalent and divalent ions form an integrated coordination network with the surrounding RNA, linking the catalytic core to the docked 3′SS (Fig. 6A–C). The most prominent differences are concentrated around the γ–γ′ interaction and the adjacent G209/G210 platform in the 5′SS region, which together position the docked 3′SS within the catalytic center. Under K+/Mg2+ and Cs+/Mg2+ conditions, U134 and A722 adopt a coplanar geometry with close hydrogen-bonding contacts characteristic of the conserved γ–γ′ interaction. G210 and G209 stack above and below the U134-A722 base pair, respectively, forming a clamp-like stacking platform, while a trans Watson–Crick/sugar-edge interaction between G209 and A133 further stabilizes the docked 3′SS. K3 (or Cs3) resides immediately adjacent to this interaction network, directly coordinating U134 of the γ–γ′ interaction and G210 of the adjacent stacking platform (Fig. 6E and F). In contrast, in the K+/Ca2+ structure, the absence of a stably resolved K3 density is accompanied by altered geometry of the same RNA elements. U134 and A722 deviate from the coplanar arrangement and exhibit less favorable hydrogen-bonding geometry, while rearrangement of G209 and G210 weakens the compact stacking architecture around this region. G209 also deviates from A133, further perturbing the local organization of the γ–γ′ region (Fig. 6D).

  Comparison of the complete active sites further shows that these differences in 3′SS organization are coupled to catalytic metal binding (Fig. 6A–C). Notably, the K3 coordination network extends from U134 and G210 in the γ–γ′ region to the C84 bulged phosphate, a shared ligand for catalytic metals M1 and M2. Within this network, G210 adopts a syn conformation [12, 54] and forms hydrogen-bonding interactions with U83, thereby positioning the neighboring C84 phosphate to support assembly of the catalytic metal-binding platform. Superposition of the three active sites shows that, in the K+/Ca2+ structure, the absence of K3 weakens positioning of G210 and displaces the U83–C84 region. The C84 bulged phosphate is displaced from its optimal geometry for metal coordination, disrupting stabilization of the M2 site (Fig. 6G), consistent with previous observations that mutations of the catalytic triad abolish catalytic metal binding in group II introns [55]. In all three structures, the phosphate between U134 of the 3′SS and C135 of the 3′ exon adopts a distorted conformation. Further comparison of the structural elements flanking the distorted scissile phosphate shows that C135 forms the conserved δ-δ′ interaction with G555 of AUX across the three ionic conditions (Supplementary Fig. S10A). However, under K+/Ca2+ conditions, the distorted scissile phosphate is markedly displaced away from the catalytic center (Fig. 6H). We therefore propose that K3-associated stabilization of the γ–γ′ interaction promotes catalytic metal assembly and productive positioning of the scissile phosphate during backsplicing.

  Notably, the γ–γ′ interaction and the associated G209/G210 stacking platform remain intact following exon ligation (Fig. 7C), suggesting that this architecture is maintained throughout the second transesterification reaction. To further assess the functional importance of the γ–γ′ interaction during backsplicing, we designed disruptive substitutions at U134 and A722 together with compensatory mutations restoring base pairing. Quantification of circRNA production after 10 min of self-splicing at 5 mM Mg2+ showed that disruption of the γ–γ′ interaction markedly reduced circRNA formation under all conditions tested, whereas compensatory mutations restored activity to near wild-type levels (Fig. 6I). In particular, the U134A-A722U(AU) mutant exhibited activity indistinguishable from wild-type. Consistent with these endpoint measurements, kinetic analyses revealed that disruptive mutants accumulated elevated levels of the branching intermediate and produced little circRNA product, whereas compensatory mutants restored progressive conversion of the branching intermediate into circular RNA (Supplementary Fig. S11). Together, these structural and biochemical data demonstrate that formation of the γ–γ′ interaction is required for productive assembly of the second-step active site, thereby promoting efficient backsplicing.


### Sequential active-site remodeling couples branching to backsplicing
  The structures presented here reveal the sequential structural events that couple branching to backsplicing through coordinated remodeling of RNA tertiary interactions and the catalytic metal-ion network (Fig. 7 and Video 1). Following completion of the first transesterification reaction, formation of the 2′–5′ branched bond is accompanied by release of the catalytic metal ion M1, whereas M2 remains associated with the active center. In this post-branching state, the bpA remains embedded within the catalytic pocket and the 3′SS has not yet been recruited (Fig. 7A). Subsequent displacement of bpA from the active center reorganizes the G209/G210 platform, permitting docking of the 3′SS into the catalytic center (Fig. 7B). This conformational transition brings the 3′ exon into proximity of the catalytic core, while formation of the δ-δ′ interaction guides the 3′SS toward the active site, establishing an initial recognition state for the second transesterification reaction. Organization of the γ–γ′ region further stabilizes productive docking of the scissile phosphate for the second transesterification reaction. Concomitantly, the K3 coordination network is established and the heteronuclear four-metal core is reassembled, generating a catalytically competent second-step active site in which M1 and M2 occupy their functional catalytic positions.

  Backsplicing subsequently proceeds through nucleophilic attack of the activated 3′-OH on the scissile phosphate, resulting in exon ligation and circRNA formation. Following release of the circular exon, the branched product retains a largely intact catalytic architecture, including preservation of the γ–γ′ region, the associated monovalent ions K1 and K2, and catalytic metal M1, whereas M2 is released from the active site (Fig. 7C). This sequential exchange of catalytic metal occupancy mirrors the two transesterification reactions, with release of M1 following branching and release of M2 following exon ligation. The persistence of the γ–γ′ region together with the associated monovalent-ion network indicates that this region remains an integral part of the catalytic architecture beyond its role in 3′SS docking.


## Discussion
  In this study, we determined eight cryo-EM structures representing consecutive catalytic states of the Gdi-1 CP group II intron under distinct ionic conditions. Together, these structures reveal that transition from branching to backsplicing is accompanied by coordinated remodeling of RNA tertiary interactions and the underlying metal-ion organization, which together maintain the ribozyme’s catalytic competence. Integration of these structural states supports a model (Fig. 7D) in which completion of branching is followed by a splice-site exchange that remodels the catalytic center for backsplicing. This transition involves displacement of bpA from the active site and recruitment of the 3′SS through the δ-δ′ interaction, followed by organization of the γ–γ′ region and reassembly of the heteronuclear four-metal core to establish a catalytically competent second-step active site. Within this process, monovalent ions organize the catalytic architecture, whereas divalent ions directly mediate catalysis, together supporting productive backsplicing. Following exon ligation, the intrinsic reversibility of transesterification may permit the reverse reaction, allowing the pre-backsplicing and exon-ligated states to exist in dynamic equilibrium.

  A striking finding emerging from our structures is that the two sequential transesterification reactions exhibit markedly different requirements for divalent cations. The initial branching reaction can proceed under K+/Ca2+ conditions, with both catalytic divalent ions accommodated within a relatively expanded active site. The M1-M2 distance reaches 4.5 Å in this state (Fig. 4B), exceeding the optimal spacing (<3.9 Å) established for two-metal-ion catalysis in group I introns and canonical group II introns [45, 56]. In contrast, upon transition to the Mg2+-dependent backsplicing reaction, the catalytic center undergoes pronounced compaction, with the M1-M2 distance contracting to 3.8 Å, within the optimal range for two-metal-ion catalysis (Fig. 5E). Given its smaller ionic radius and higher charge density, Mg2+ is better suited than Ca2+ to support the inner-sphere coordination required within such a compact catalytic center [57, 58], whereas the greater hydration propensity of Ca2+ can increase local hydration and relax the metal-coordination network [59]. In addition, molecular dynamics (MD) free-energy simulations of the pre-second-step catalytic center of the Pylaiella littoralis group IIB intron revealed a strong thermodynamic preference for Mg2+ over Ca2+, with thermodynamic integration yielding a relative binding free-energy difference (ΔΔG) of approximately 13 kcal mol−1. Quantum chemical calculations showed that the interaction network in the first coordination sphere of Ca2+ at the M2 metal-binding site associated with the 5′ exon nucleophile differs from that of Mg2+, and substitution of Mg2+ by Ca2+ replaces metal-ion-RNA phosphate coordination with metal-ion-water coordination in the MD structure [60]. In contrast, the interaction network in the first coordination sphere of the M1 site was independent of the identity of the divalent metal ion (Mg2+ or Ca2+). A similar site-specific difference is observed in the Gdi-1 pre-backsplicing structure under K+/Ca2+ conditions, in which Ca2+ at the M1 site remains accommodated, whereas Ca2+ at the M2 site, the metal site that coordinates the nucleophilic 3′-OH of the 5′ exon U208 under K+/Mg2+ conditions, is not stably resolved.

  Beyond divalent-ion coordination, monovalent ions also contribute to organization of the second-step active center. Notably, K3 stabilizes the γ–γ′ interaction and establishes a local environment favorable for productive Mg2+ coordination. Such interdependence between K+ and Mg2+ binding has also been observed in other RNA contexts [52, 61]. The importance of ion-dependent active-site remodeling is further supported by previous observations that replacement of K+ by smaller monovalent ions such as Na+ or Li+ preferentially traps group IIC introns in the toggled conformation [16]. Consistently, pharmacological perturbation of this conformational transition preferentially inhibits the second step of splicing in the O. iheyensis group IIC intron [62]. Together, these observations underscore the importance of coordinated ion organization and catalytic-center remodeling for second-step progression. Structural analyses of Gdi-1 backsplicing (K+/Ca2+), the truncated Cte-1 mutant backsplicing (U537ins, K+/Ca2+; PDB: 8 × 9L), and Group IIC pre-2S (NH4+/Ca2+; PDB: 8T2R) indicate that none adopts a fully optimized RNA architecture for second-step catalysis, even though M2 is modeled in the latter two structures (Supplementary Fig. S10C–E). Although the δ-δ′ interaction is formed in these structures, the γ–γ′ interaction adopts distorted geometries. Mutational analysis further highlights the functional importance of the γ–γ′ interaction, as its disruption has a substantially stronger effect on splicing efficiency than disruption of δ-δ′ (Supplementary Fig. S10B). Together, these observations indicate that coordinated formation of both δ-δ′ and γ–γ′ interactions flanking the scissile phosphate is critical for accurate 3′SS docking [63], with K+-dependent organization of the γ–γ′ region being particularly important for assembly of the heteronuclear four-metal core in the second-step active center.

  CP group II introns complete the splicing cycle through exon circularization, generating circRNAs with potential biological and biotechnological significance. CircRNAs function as microRNA sponges, protein decoys, and regulatory elements in eukaryotes and have attracted considerable interest because of their exceptional stability [64, 65]. Recently, a redesigned group IIC intron derived from the Clostridium tetani surface layer protein (ctSLP) was engineered to mimic CP group II intron architecture and produce circRNAs exhibiting stability and translational efficiency comparable to linear mRNAs [66]. Our structures from two phylogenetically distinct circularizing ribozymes provide a mechanistic framework for understanding how active-site remodeling and exon organization promote RNA circularization. These findings provide a structural foundation for future efforts aimed at engineering circularizable ribozymes for synthetic biology and RNA-based applications.
