# Pose Reproduction,
Cross Docking, Database Enrichment,
and Reverse Docking Benchmarks for DOCK6, Vina, and AutoDock 4 with
Emphasis on Approved Drugs


## Abstract
  High-quality test sets to evaluate docking software help the community understand strengths and weaknesses of different methods and programs. In this work, we introduce SB2025, a calculation-ready set of 1,244 protein-ligand complexes derived from the PDB, curated using literature-based ligand protonation states, and freely available in DOCK6, AutoDock Vina, and AutoDock 4 (AD4) formats. SB2025 includes 322 unique proteins, 17 major protein classes, and ligands with a wide range of properties and flexibilities, making it a broad and challenging test set. FARMA2025, a specific subset of SB2025, contains 165 approved drugs bound to their pharmacological targets and closely mirrors the distribution of FDA drug target classes for small molecules through 2023 (r 2 = 0.72). Comprehensive benchmarking was subsequently performed to evaluate pose reproduction (PR), cross-docking (CD), database enrichment (DE), and reverse docking (RD) under standardized conditions. Averaged PR success rate across triplicate runs for DOCK6 was slightly improved compared to Vina (followed by AD4) using both the large SB2025 test set and the more drug-focused FARMA2025 test set. Conversely, CD matrix success rates for AD4 were more favorable than DOCK6 or Vina across 11 protein families. The programs all yielded substantial early DE; however, the number of active ligands in common between the programs within the top 1% of the database, with one exception, was surprisingly low. Challenging RD tests using FARMA2025, which gauge the ability to correctly rank the cognate structure best, indicate that generating the correct ligand pose is necessary but not sufficient. SB2025, FARMA2025, and example scripts and protocols for benchmarking are available at ringo.ams.stonybrook.edu (downloads) and github.com/rizzolab.


## Introduction
  Molecular docking is a powerful and well-established −   computational approach employed in the early-stages of drug discovery to aid in the identification of promising lead compounds. The fundamental process involves generating 3D binding geometries (termed poses) between a candidate ligand (typically a small organic molecule) and a drug target of interest (typically a protein and often called receptor) and scoring the complementarity (favorability) between the two species. When a large library of candidate ligands is evaluated, the process is known as virtual screening. The seminal docking program called DOCK, developed by Kuntz and co-workers in 1982, primarily scored complementarity using a shape matching algorithm. Subsequent versions of DOCK (discussed in Allen et al.), and programs developed by other research groups including AutoDock, AutoDock Vina, CDOCKER, eHITS, FlexX, FRED, Glide, ,  GOLD, ,  ICM, MOE, rDock, and Surflex, among others, have employed a wide variety of methods to assess complementarity.

  Successful lead identification using structure-based approaches such as docking necessarily requires sufficiently accurate sampling and scoring. If native-like binding geometries cannot be generated and/or ranked appropriately among the many solutions produced during the calculations, then the results will not likely translate to the real world. Thus, when possible, most developers make significant efforts to benchmark their software against experimental data. In our own work, we developed a large curated set of 780 protein-ligand systems (SB2010 test set), subsequently expanded to 1043 protein-ligand systems (SB2012 test set), as part of our laboratories ongoing development efforts. We employed SB2012 to show that the accuracy of DOCK, in terms of pose reproduction success (best-scoring dock poses within 2 Å of the experimental structures), systematically improved in going from version 4.0.2 (51.4%) to 5.4 (65.2%) to 6.7 (73.3%). Figure  a–c outlines the three most common experiments typically employed for benchmarking which include pose reproduction (PR), cross docking (CD), and database enrichment (DE). A fourth experiment (Figure  d) pursued in the present work involves reverse docking (RD). Together, these four computational experiments broadly assess geometric accuracy (PR, CD) and rank accuracy (DE, RD).

  In this present work, for comparison with DOCK6.12, we expand our benchmarking to include the widely used programs AutoDock 4 - Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm (LGA) and AutoDock Vina. These specific programs were chosen because they are actively supported, are free for academic use, have well documented protocols, and employ a docking protocol that includes “on-the-fly” ligand sampling as opposed to approaches that use pre-generated conformers which are then rigidly docked (i.e., DOCK 3 approach). It is important to emphasize that our objective was not to show that any one docking program is superior but rather to explore if any of the programs yielded improved outcomes for different tasks or systems. As DOCK developers we are eager to identify areas in which we can improve. We acknowledge that no single program, sampling method or scoring function is likely to perform best under all conditions. ,  We are also keenly aware that comparing different programs is fraught with potential pitfalls. For example, we have years of experience using DOCK6, compared to other programs, and therefore will be more comfortable in terms of preparing the systems for simulation, analyzing the results, and troubleshooting. Nevertheless, we believe that benchmarking is important and (hopefully) will yield useful information if the calculations are performed carefully, and under well-controlled conditions, which we attempted to do. The intent was to use the same protocols established by the developers of their respective programs, as these would most likely be employed by prospective users for real-world projects. We would additionally note that as the docking outcomes are fundamentally linked to the different sampling/scoring schemes employed by each program, this is an important component of the benchmarking in a practical sense for the end user.

  As a baseline for comparison, we performed a literature search to identify previously reported benchmarks that focused primarily on AutoDock Vina (hereafter referred to as Vina), AutoDock 4-LGA (hereafter referred to as AD4), and DOCK6. As shown in Table  , the initial publication associated with the release of Vina yielded a PR success rate of 78% compared to 49% using AD4 across 190 systems (Table   row i). A second study, which also included DOCK6, yielded PR success rates of 64% for Vina, 55% for AD4, and 58% for DOCK6 across 228 systems (Table   row ii). A third larger study, employing 2002 complexes, yielded PR success rates of 49% for Vina, 37% for AD4, and 44% for DOCK6 (Table   row iii). A fourth study, focused specifically on 710 metalloproteins, reported PR rates of 43% for Vina, 23% for AD4, and 38% for DOCK6 (Table   row iv). A fifth study, which reported CD success rates for a set of 130 protein systems, yielded 40% for Vina, 35% for AD4, and 40% for DOCK6 (Table   row v). The varying results in Table   strongly suggest that docking success rates are highly dependent on which specific systems were employed for benchmarking (43–78% for Vina, 23–55% for AD4, 38–58% for DOCK6). The results also suggest that the trend in PR accuracy for these three programs is Vina > DOCK6 > AD4.

  While the results in Table   are valuable, and establish points for comparison of these three programs, additional factors are important to consider. For example, for docking to be useful both pose accuracy (Figure  a,b) and score ranking (Figure  c,d) should be high, but of the five studies in Table  , only iii and iv examined both. Further, although study iii presented docking outcomes for six protein classes, many important drug target classes were not examined. Additionally, although studies i, ii and iii evaluated successes as a function of ligand flexibility, they did not explore the impact of other properties (ligand or receptor based). Other parameters such as partial atomic charge, ligand sampling (box size, exhaustiveness) should be explored. An additional observation, based on our experience as developers, is that the success rates reported in Table   for DOCK6 appear low (38–58%). Given that study ii employed a 6–12 potential for the docking grids, instead of the recommended 6–9 potential, and study iii employed non-standard grid generation protocols, more comprehensive benchmarking is warranted.

  An additionally important component of benchmarking is the use of test sets that are large, comprised of targets with significant diversity, and have unbiased composition. An examination of the literature shows that most benchmarking studies from recent years focused primarily on specific classes of targets such as metalloproteins, peptides, nucleic acids, and antibiotics. With regards to the studies in Table  , for the three programs under investigation, most of the test sets were rather limited in size (N = 130–710 systems) with the exception of study iii (N = 2002). However, study iii eliminated systems containing organic cofactors or metal ions thereby neglecting an important and challenging area of chemical space. In terms of reasonable size, the present work introduces the SB2025 docking test set (1,244 systems), which updates and extends our previous test sets (SB2010 and SB2012). In terms of reasonable diversity, SB2025 contains 251 distinct proteins, as defined using UniProt Names, overall representing the 17 most common protein classes among drug targets. SB2025 also contains another class termed “other” which contains an additional 71 distinct proteins that do not fit into any of the 17 main classifications (251 + 71= 322 distinct proteins total). In terms of the ligands, SB2025 is also reasonably diverse, as defined by their large range of flexibilities, molecular weights, and cLog Ps (see Results). Finally, although diversity is important, some redundancy of proteins is equally important to assess docking robustness as well as facilitating CD experiments on aligned proteins. And, to more explicitly deal with potential composition bias, we also introduce a new test set based on Food and Drug Administration, European Medicines Agency, and Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency compounds which are Approved as drugs with a Related Mechanism of Action (termed FARMA2025). This latter set, which we believe be the first of its kind, is comprised solely of approved drugs, co-crystallized with their primary target, in proportions relative to the number of compounds approved under each major protein class.

  The primary objectives of this study are three-fold: (I) Introduce FARMA2025 and the expanded SB2025 docking test and compare their composition to FDA approved targets, (II) benchmark pose reproduction in terms of RMSD calculation protocols, parameters affecting ligand sampling, charge models, docking test set composition, and protein class accuracy, (III) benchmark cross docking, database enrichment, and reverse docking. Additional contributions of this work include downloadable versions of expanded test sets with simulation-ready input files, tutorials on structure preparation should users want to add their own systems, and analysis tools. The work also establishes benchmarks for DOCK6 version 12 for which a new simplex ramping feature was added to increase energy minimization efficiency.


## Theoretical Methods

### SB2025 Test Set Criteria
  The SB2025 test set was expanded to 1244 systems by adding 128 new structures containing approved drugs, 33 new antibiotic targets comprised of LpxC proteins, 76 systems from the DUD-E database missing in our earlier SB2012 test set, and several structures for smaller protein families (Glutamate AMPA, Dopamine D3). The actual system preparation steps required for docking are summarized below in Computational Details. As a general rule, protein-ligand complexes from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) were included in our test sets only if they met the following criteria: (1) the PDB structure resolution was <3.5 Å (2.1 Å average), (2) the ligands were non-covalent, (3) the ligands did not contain uncommon elements, (4) the proteins did not contain unmodelled regions in contact with the ligand, (5) the complex could be assigned standard force field parameters and be energy-minimized with the program AMBER, and (6) the ligand retained a negative (i.e., favorable) energy score and low root mean square deviation (RMSD) to the original X-ray pose (≤2.0 Å) after a second energy minimization with the program DOCK6.

  As new protein-ligand systems were added, and complexes already existing in SB2012 were reconstructed for SB2025, we introduced additional steps to maintain and improve test set integrity, including bookkeeping scripts and visual checks. The bookkeeping scripts logged the addition of heavy atoms to incomplete residues, the deletion of dual occupancy atoms, and which nonstandard residues were mutated to a similar standard sidechain (i.e., selenomethionine to methionine). If the heavy atom count was not balanced for a particular system, this indicated an unaccounted change had taken place. Visualization of representative systems was also employed to spot-check the integrity of: (1) disulfide bond assignment, (2) histidine protonation states when chelating Zn, (3) incorporation of Heme or (4) other organic cofactors, (5) incorporation of phosphotyrosine, (6) proper placement of TER keywords for PDB files, and (7) defined mutations for nonstandard residues. Adoption of the above helps eliminate errors due to formatting and/or other software changes since our original SB2010/SB2012 processing scripts/protocols were implemented. Overall 35 systems in SB2012 were not propagated to SB2025 for various reasons, for example, entry 1A8I had a nonstandard residue (LLP) near the ligand not sufficiently similar to a standard residue, entries 1S2A and 1T40 had a NADP cofactor which failed to converge during ligand partial charge assignment, and entries 1EC9, 1LMC, 1LMO, 1LMP, 1LMQ, and 3DFR had poor ligand geometries according to PDB Structure Validation Reports.

  It should be noted that 26 structures introduced into SB2025 were determined by Electron Microscopy (EM), all other structures were determined by X-ray crystallography. Membrane proteins, such as G-Protein Coupled Receptor (GPCR), Ion Channels, and Transporters, among others, can be difficult to crystallize, but due to improvements in EM many difficult to crystallize structures can now be solved. All of the 26 EM structures added to SB2025 were better than 3.3 Å resolution (2.96 Å average). While precise atomic detail may not emerge until higher resolution, atomic level structure-based design and modeling projects is possible using EM structures of similar resolution as incorporated here. −       Given that local resolutions in EM structures can be lower than the overall resolution, particularly for ligands, we aimed to include structures only where ligands could be unambiguously assigned or had high electron density. Although we believe all of the structures in SB2025 are suitable for benchmarking, users should employ their own discretion if any are used for specific production modeling projects.


### FARMA2025 Test Set Criteria
  The FARMA2025 test set, comprised of 165 approved drugs in complex with their “primary” protein target, is a subset of SB2025. During the construction of FARMA2025, we examined the frequency with which approved drugs target different “protein classes” (see section below) to help create a test set which reflected these populations. Potential systems for FARMA2025 were identified using the online database DrugCentral.org (https://drugcentral.org) which provided a list of all drugs approved by either the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), European Medicines Agency (EMA), and Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA). The International Nonproprietary Name (INN) name of drugs available through the “SMILES strings and InChI file” link on DrugCentral.org was used as input to further search the database to determine the drug target associated with the “mechanism of action” (MOA), if any. The INN and target associated with each drug were subsequently used to query the PDB to assess if publicly available complexes were available. More specifically, with regards to “targets”, DrugCentral.org contains a Bioactivity Summary section for each drug with quantitative data compiled from multiple databases such as CheEMBLDb, DrugMatrix, and to a lesser extent scientific literature or drug labels. For FARMA2025, we restricted the targets for a given drug to be those associated with the MOA which is defined by DrugCentral.org as the biomolecule (in the present work all proteins) with which the drug directly binds to exert the therapeutic effect (termed the “drug target”). Thus, FARMA2025 will contain the primary mechanistic target for each drug and not investigational targets or other off-target proteins. The intent was to create a representative database containing therapeutically relevant protein-ligand binding interactions. With regards to “MOA”, although DrugCentral.org indicates whether a drug acts as, for example, an inhibitor, agonist, antagonist, etc, we did not in the present work make decisions to include or exclude systems based on specific MOA “type”. An analysis of the 165 systems in FARMA2025 yielded the following breakdown: inhibitor = 102, agonist = 28, antagonist = 17, modulator = 7, blocker = 4, positive allosteric modulator = 3, binding agent = 1, activator = 1, gating inhibitor = 1, partial agonist = 1). It would be worthwhile for future updates to FARMA to consider adding less represented MOA types.

  Priority was also placed on incorporating wildtype structures from the most relevant species. In some cases, orthologs with highly similar active sites were employed. For six systems (7JVQ, 7CMU, 5KZC, 4IAQ, 6DRY, 8IRT) the protein was a related subtype of the actual drug target. For example, 7JVQ contains apomorphine bound to the D1a Dopamine Receptor, but the actual mechanistic target is the D2 Dopamine Receptor. For some ligands in the test set, the active form of the compound is different than the approved form (i.e., prodrugs). For example, the approved drug Tamiflu is a prodrug but the metabolite oseltamivir carboxylate is the more relevant form for molecular modeling. To help determine which systems under consideration for inclusion in the new test sets were active metabolites of approved prodrugs we searched the Chemical Entities of Biological Interest (ChEBI) for the pro-drug code 50266. From the search, the only relevant matches identified were the aforementioned compound oseltamivir carboxylate and simvastatin acid.


### Protein Classification for Entries in SB2025 and Docking Subsets
  To facilitate analysis of the composition of SB2025 and FARMA2025 we wanted a simple, concise, and consistent classification system for grouping proteins. Although every PDB entry has an associated “Classification” assignment, in our experience, identical proteins may be classed differently depending upon the particular entry being examined. For example, epidermal growth factor receptor may be labeled as a Kinase or Transferase, and HIV protease may be listed as a Protease, Hydrolase, or Viral Protein. Further, although they have very dissimilar functions, proteins such as 7-Transmembrane receptors, and Ion Channels, can both be listed under the broad category of Membrane Protein.

  Santos et al. recently reported a comprehensive curation of FDA approved drugs and their targets, and assigned each drug to a specific mechanistic protein class or classes. Drug/target relationships were primarily obtained from prescribing information or the literature. Starting from 4,631 entries, which the authors provided as Supplementary Information Table S2 in ref  (hereafter referred to in this work as STS2), the majority of drugs could be grouped into 18 “primary” protein classes and 1 “other” category. Their analysis employed 1,591 approved drugs (small molecule and biologics) which targeted 667 human proteins, 189 pathogen-derived proteins, and 37 other biomolecules (893 total). The list of protein classes included: (1) 7TM1, (2) Nuclear Receptor, (3) Other, (4) VGIC, (5) Reductase, (6) LGIC, (7) Transporter electrochemical, (8) Kinase, (9) Protease, (10) Hydrolase, (11) NTPase, (12) Transferase, (13) 7TM2, (14) Lyase, (15) Isomerase, (16) Phosphodiesterase, (17) Cytochrome p450, (18) 7TM3, and (19) Epigenetic Eraser.

  It is important to note that the classifications in STS2 were originally derived from those specified in the Chemical European Molecular Biology Laboratory (ChEMBL) database. ChEMBL currently has records for about 10,000 protein targets but unfortunately does not contain entries for many of the structures deposited in the PDB from which our docking test sets are sourced. In an attempt to classify all entries in SB2025 (N = 1,244), using the same 19 protein classification categories employed by Santos et al., we developed a straightforward classification procedure as outlined in Supporting Information Theoretical Methods (SI), Protein classification for entries in SB2025 and docking subsets (SI) section (Figure S1, Table_S1.pdf, and accompanying text). This procedure provided a standardized classification convention for our docking test set, and as shown further below, simplified comparisons with the prior Santos et al. study. Lastly, as a point of clarification, it is important to note that in this work we make the distinction between “protein class” and “protein family”, the latter being a highly homologous set of experimental structures containing the same protein aligned to a common reference frame and employed in CD experiments (see Methods).


### Protein Classification for Approved Drug Targets
  An important outcome from the prior Santos et al. study was analysis of the prevalence of “Human” protein classes most frequently targeted by approved drugs. To create a dataset more relevant for the present work focused on small molecule docking, we excluded targets associated with approved “biologics” (N = 194 drugs). We also added non-Human (pathogenic) proteins targeted by small molecules (N = 211 drugs) which were original excluded in the Santos et al. analysis although they were in fact listed in STS2. Finally, an updated dataset was created by adding in protein targets associated with small molecules (N = 178 drugs) approved by the FDA (https://www.fda.gov) for the time period 06/2015 to 08/2023. Drug/target relationships for drugs approved in this time period were obtained from DrugCentral.org. We refer to this updated group as the FDA 2023 dataset. The most common small molecule drugs approved for the updated time period targeted Kinases (27.4%). ## Computational Details

### File Preparation for Energy Minimization and Docking
  Detailed protocols for taking raw PDB files and preparing them for docking with DOCK6 closely followed the steps outlined in previous publications ,  The processed files created for DOCK6 were then used as inputs to create files for Vina and AD4. Briefly, the processes can be organized into five steps comprising: (1) initial PDB processing, (2) restrained energy minimization, (3) DOCK6 input file preparation, (4) Vina input file preparation, and (5) AD4 input file preparation. The specific tasks associated with each of these steps are summarized in Supporting Information Computational Details (SI), File preparation for energy minimization and docking (SI) section (see Figure S2 and accompanying text). It is important to note that all of the 3D files used for docking a given system were derived from the same set of input coordinates.


### Program Versions, Sampling and Scoring Functions
  The three docking programs discussed in this work use different sampling and scoring algorithms and interested readers are advised to consult the developer’s publications for more in-depth information. All DOCK6 calculations employed version 6.12 (abbreviated DOCK6), all AutoDock Vina calculations employed version 1.1.2 (abbreviated Vina), and all AutoDock calculations employed version 4.2.6 (abbreviated AD4). Briefly, in terms of sampling, DOCK6 employs the “anchor & grow” procedure in which a ligand to be docked is segmented about its rotatable bonds, and the resulting fragments are treated as “anchors” which are then oriented into different regions of the binding site. For each oriented anchor, the algorithm proceeds to regrow different ligand poses in layer-by-layer fashion which involves ligand dihedral angle sampling and energy minimization at each stage of growth. The Vina program employs an “Iterated Local Search global optimizer” which uses iterative steps of mutation and optimization to sample rigid body and internal degrees of freedom (dihedral angles) of the ligand with each step being accepted or rejected using a Metropolis criterion. The AD4 sampling method employed in this work was the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm (LGA) in which a given molecule is considered to have 7 + N genes. Three of the genes represent a ligand’s translation from the origin, four genes representing a quaternion of the ligand’s orientation, and N genes are related to the dihedral angle sampled about each rotatable bond. New candidate geometries are produced using crossover (gene swapping between parent molecules) followed by mutation and energy minimization.

  In terms of scoring, all DOCK6 calculations used the default scoring function consisting of intermolecular van der Waals (VDW) + electrostatic (ES) energy. All Vina calculations used the default scoring function comprised of gauss1 + gauss2 + repulsion + hydrophobic + H-bonding + conformational entropy. All AD4 calculations used the default scoring function comprised of VDW + ES + H-bonding + desolvation + conformational entropy. Additional setup considerations applicable to all three programs are discussed in Supporting Information Computational Details (SI), Other docking considerations (SI) section) along with representative PR input files for DOCK6 (Table S2), Vina (Table S3), and AD4 (Table S4).


### Pose Reproduction (PR) Protocols
  PR experiments evaluate the ability of a program to reproduce experimentally observed ligand poses and historically is arguably the most widely used benchmarking metric to assess docking programs. Briefly, docking outcomes are considered a “success” if the ligand RMSD (discussed further below) between the best scored pose and the original X-ray pose is ≤2.0 Å, a “scoring failure” when the best scored pose is >2.0 Å but a pose exists that is ≤2.0 Å, and a “sampling failure” if none of poses generated were ≤2.0 Å. For a group of docking results, as outlined in Mukerjee et al., the percent of successes, scoring failures, and sampling failures add to 100%. As presented in Results, we initially investigated use of different protocols to determine the most appropriate conditions to be used for each program through examination of (1) ligand sampling (search space size and exhaustiveness), (2) RMSD calculation models, and (3) different ligand partial atomic charge models.

  At the suggestion of a reviewer we employed PoseBusters analysis, using the pip-installable Python package, to verify the physical validity of docked ligand geometries. Based on 20 different criteria, which included both inter- and intra-molecular descriptors, all three docking programs yielded physically reasonable poses for the overwhelming majority of the 1,244 systems in the SB2025 test set, thereby providing confidence in the three-dimensional geometries analyzed in this work. See Supporting Information Computational Details (SI), Pose reproduction protocols (SI) section for PoseBuster results (Figure S3 and accompanying text), as well as additional PR protocols.


### Cross Docking (CD) Protocols
  In CD, each structure in a related group of proteins are aligned in 3D space to one representative family member (the reference) which yields a matrix of dockable protein-ligand pairings, where the x-axis represents each protein structure in the family, and the y-axis represents ligand poses. Diagonal elements in the CD matrix represent “true” protein-ligand cognate pairs each originating from the same experimental complex, and are analogous to standard PR results, while the off-diagonal elements represent “theoretical” protein-ligand cognate pairs. Prior to cross docking, each protein-ligand pair (true or theoretical) was energy minimized using DOCK6 in conjunction with a harmonic restraint to tether the coordinates to the aligned ligand geometries. Energy minimized poses resulting in unfavorable (positive) intermolecular scores, or RMSD values >2 Å from the aligned ligand pose, were considered “incompatible” pairing given that docking would not be expected to be able to adopt the same pose and thus were excluded from docking and not numerically included in the outcomes. For simplicity, incompatible pairings determined by DOCK6 for each CD family were applied to each program. To visualize CD results, heat maps are employed with each matrix element being colored coded by docking outcome (blue = success, green = scoring failure, red = sampling failure, grey = incompatible paring). Table   outlines the eleven CD protein families used in this work, which were taken from more than thirty families available in SB2025, ten of which represent a common drug target protein class as described above. We also elected to select CD families which had an equivalent match in terms of the DUD-E database so that DE experiments (discussed next section) could be run on a congruous set. Additional CD protocols employed in this work are provided in Supporting Information Computational Details (SI), Cross docking protocols (SI) section.


### Database Enrichment (DE) Protocols
  DE experiments assess the ability of program to dock and rank molecules with known activity (actives) relative to a group of property-matched molecules assumed to have no activity (decoys). The active and decoy sets associated employed in this work, associated with a specific protein target, were taken from the DUD-E database. Based on rank, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves can be generated by plotting the true positive rate (TPR) versus false positive rate (FPR) for the docked/ranked results. In general, good DE occurs when the known actives are ranked more favorably than the decoys and the corresponding area under the ROC curve (AUC) will be large. In contrast, random DE yields ROC curves which lie along the diagonal and poor DE yields ROC curves with lower AUCs. Additional DE protocols employed in this work are provided in Supporting Information Computational Details (SI), Database enrichment protocols (SI) section.


### Reverse Docking (RD) Protocols
  In RD a ligand is docked to multiple proteins, including its target, and ranked by score. For each ligand in this experiment, target rank was assessed using three levels of classification: (i) cognate rank, (ii) family rank, and (iii) class rank. “Cognate rank” can be used to quantify how favorably the specific protein structure (i.e., coordinates) in which a given ligand was co-crystallized with ranks. For example, when the drug Aricept taken from PDB code 1EVE is docked to all structures in FARMA 2025, what is the rank of the docking score for the 1EVE protein structure? “Family rank” provides an arguably more useful definition of RD outcomes because it accounts for the possibility of redundant targets. For example, FARMA2025 contains three different acetylcholinesterase structures (PDB 1EVE, 1W76 and 2ACK), and whichever of these three structures Aricept ranks best in, is the rank that will be counted for the system’s family rank. Finally, “class rank” provides a more promiscuous definition of docking outcomes. Acetylcholinesterase is a hydrolase, so the most favorably ranked of any hydrolase in the test set is considered. Evaluation of class rank is important because drugs may show affinity for other proteins in the class (e.g., kinases) or across related subtypes (e.g., FARMA2025 contains five subtypes of the 5-Hydroxytryptamine receptor). In summary, cognate rank is the most precise definition of target recognition, class rank is the most promiscuous, and family rank is in between. As a corollary to docking success, we also computed for RD the “cognate first rank” (CFR) rate, which measured what percent of drugs accurately rank their cognate protein structure best. From the CFR, we also compute whether or not the predicted ligand poses match the X-ray poses (termed pose accurate-cognate first rank, PA-CFR).


### RMSD Calculations
  An important metric for assessing PR and CD accuracy is ligand RMSD relative to a known reference. For consistency, DOCK6 was used to compute all ligand RMSD values using one of three algorithms: (1) Standard heavy atom RMSD (RMSDs) based on atomic order, (2) symmetry corrected RMSD using the Hungarian algorithm (RMSDh), and (3) a minimum RMSD approach (RMSDm) based on the method reported in Trott et al.. It should be noted that the DOCK6 RMSDm implementation only allows matches if atoms pairs also have identical Sybyl atom types which is consistent with the RMSDh algorithm. For Vina and AD4, pdbqt files for docked poses were converted to mol2 format using OpenBabel version 2.4.1 and atom types were converted to the Sybyl atom types used by DOCK6 such that all programs had RMSD calculated using the same atom types.

## Results and Discussion

### Results Part I. Composition of FARMA2025 and SB2025 Compared
to Other Docking Test Sets

#### Test Set Ligand Composition
  As outlined in Theoretical Methods, we overhauled and updated our SB2012 docking benchmark test set to include more clinically relevant protein-ligand systems. The new set called SB2025 includes 1,244 ready-to-dock systems for which a subset of 165 systems, termed FARMA2025, contains approved drugs. For comparison, this work also examined results corresponding to the smaller but widely used ,,  Astex Diverse set (hereafter referred to as Astex2007) which is a subset of SB2025. The downloadable Table_S5.pdf in Supporting Information Results and Discussion (SI), Test set ligand composition (SI) section, indicates which specific PDB codes are associated with which specific dataset (SB2025, FARMA2025, SB2012, Astex2007).

### Results Part II. Pose Reproduction (PR) Benchmarks

#### PR Outcomes Varying Parameters Affecting Ligand Sampling
  Table   shows results from a set of docking experiments to gauge outcomes as a function of small or large sampling space (box size) and low or high sampling exhaustiveness. To gauge reproducibility, all of the PR experiments in this study were run in triplicate using different random seeds. These tests employed the SB2025 database, which, as a point of reference, yields average volumes of 3,439 and 39,530 Å3, for small and large sampling spaces, respectively. We particularly wanted to examine the effects of using the standard DOCK6 large box size protocol on Vina and AD4 outcomes, and vice versa, given the considerable differences in size. The PR outcomes here are presented in terms of docking success, scoring failures, and sampling failures (see Methods). This initial experiment employed the DOCK6 Hungarian RMSD, but different RMSD calculations were subsequently explored (see section “PR Outcomes Using Different RMSD Calculation Methods” below).

  In terms of sampling “space”, DOCK6 yielded much worse PR success rates using small (53–55%) vs large (72–73%) docking grids (Table  ) which coincide with a larger number of sampling failures. In contrast, both Vina and AD4 yield improved PR success using small vs large search spaces with concomitant lower sampling failure rates. For DOCK6, if a ligand atom falls outside of the grid during docking the calculation terminates, which for smaller search spaces under the present conditions will contribute to the poorer performance observed (incomplete dockings = 213 small vs 3 large). Additionally, DOCK6 employs docking spheres for anchor orientation which by design will bias sampling towards relevant cavities and binding site regions which will help to focus the search despite use of a larger grid. Vina and AD4 do not employ spheres, thus focusing the search space appears to be helped by using smaller docking grids.

  In terms of sampling “exhaustiveness”, using a large search space with DOCK6 and a small search space with Vina and AD4, none of the programs showed meaningful differences in PR success rates using low vs high parameter settings. A similar observation was previously reported for AD4 when comparing runs using 10 vs 50 LGA. For DOCK6, although high exhaustiveness led to more sampling failures (7.9 low vs 8.5 high, large box size) the difference was small and similar in magnitude to the standard deviations and thus likely not significant. Further, as only the top 20 scored poses are retained for analysis for DOCK6 (Pose Reproduction (PR) protocols (SI)) it would be possible to retain more decoy poses even with increased sampling. In contrast to DOCK6, use of higher exhaustiveness led to a reduction in the number of sampling failures for Vina (9.3 low vs 5.7 high, small box size) and AD4 (21.1 low vs 12.1 high, small box size) as expected. Overall, given that the PR success rates changed little using higher exhaustiveness settings despite being more computationally expensive, unless otherwise specified, all DOCK6 runs employed default parameters corresponding to a low-exhaust + large-size state (termed low-ex_large-size) and all Vina and AD4 runs employed default parameters corresponding a low-exhaust + small-size (termed low-ex_small-size) state. Finally, the small standard deviations for the PR outcomes shown in Table   using SB2025 suggests good reproducibility, and although use of additional random seeds could be desirable if resources allow, a single seed is likely sufficient to capture representative PR statistics.

  It should be noted that explicit docking times are not presented in this work because it was not a focus, and run-time speed is highly dependent on HPC architecture, run-time resource usage, and cluster variability, among other factors (for example compiler options). DOCK6 also has additional overhead in terms of the time required to pre-compute the protein energy grids, and if desired, ligand partial charges which make direct timing comparisons difficult. A potential concern with regards to timings raised by a reviewer was that the larger search space used by DOCK6 might decrease efficiency compared to the smaller search space used by Vina and AD4. However, with the present protocols DOCK6 timings using SB2025 with small (1.5 cpu-minutes/system) vs large (1.7 cpu-minutes/system) docking grids were similar.


#### PR Outcomes Using Different RMSD Calculation Methods
  As noted in Methods, there are three different methods in DOCK6 for computing RMSD between predicted and native poses (RMSDs, RMSDh, and RMSDm). Figure   compares PR success rates for a test case of docking calculations using the SB2025 database using all three RMSD methods, which, as expected, followed the order RMSDs < RMSDh < RMSDm across all three programs. Importantly, DOCK6 and Vina showed similar performance which indicates RMSDh is not biased towards DOCK6. Given the fact that symmetry is important for benchmarking, and use RMSDm may overinflate docking success, we feel that the use of the RMSDh calculation method is a more appropriate choice for benchmarking and is used for the remainder of this manuscript.


#### PR Outcomes Using Different Partial Atomic Charge Models
  The partial atomic charge model used to assign charges to ligands will also affect docking outcomes. Historically, we have employed the AM1-BCC semi-empirical method when performing benchmarking, or when studying a limited sets of ligands. ,  In contrast, AD4 developers typically recommend use of Gasteiger ligand charges. Although the semiempirical AM1-BCC model is generally considered to be a more accurate than the empirical Gasteiger method, as shown in Figure  , there is a slight increase in AD4 docking performance using Gasteiger charges. The AD4 program uses an empirically weighted scoring function which was fit using Gasteiger charges which likely explains why this model is preferred. In contrast, DOCK6 shows a slight improvement using AM1-BCC, as opposed to the Gasteiger charges which would be more consistent with DOCK’s use of a simple physics-based Lennard Jones plus Coulombic energy function. Readers should note that Vina does not make use of ligand charges. Based on these partial charge model tests, in conjunction with the “sampling space” and “sampling exhaustiveness” results shown above in Table  , for the remainder of this manuscript, all DOCK6 calculations employed the “low-ex_large-size_bcc” protocol, all AD4 calculations employed the “low-ex_small-size_gast” protocol, and all Vina calculations employed the “low-ex_small-size_nocharge” protocol.


#### PR Outcomes Using Four Docking Test Sets
  After determining which of the tested protocols work best for each program, we also investigated how use of different sized test sets might affect PR outcomes. Again, across the different seeds, there was little variability observed in terms of PR success (blue), score failure (green), and sample failure (red), suggesting good reproducibility. Readers should note that the sum of these three percentages will be 100%. Figure  a plots results for the largest dataset SB2025 (N = 1244) for which success trends and standard deviations follow DOCK6 (74.6 ± 0.8%) > Vina (71.0 ± 0.4%) > AD4 (65.8 ± 0.4%). PR outcomes in Figure  b which used the new FARMA2025 database (N = 165), indicate DOCK6 was marginally better than Vina and both performed better relative to AD4. Specifically, the results were DOCK6 (78.9 ± 0.4%) > Vina (76.0 ± 0.9%) > AD4 (67.3 ± 1.6%). Importantly, the standard deviations were low despite the smaller sample size. For the even smaller, but widely used Astex2007 dataset (Figure  c), all programs performed similarly, DOCK6 (80.9 ± 1.4%) ≈ Vina (80.5 ± 0.0%) ≈ AD4 (79.7 ± 0.7%), and again the standard deviations were low. Interestingly, all three programs yielded their respective highest success rates using the Astex2007 set of all of the PR tests in Figure  . Readers should note that the current Astex2007 PR results are based on 82 out of 85 systems. Systems not included were 1R58 which contained a manganese ion near the ligand which did not pass our preparation setup protocol, 1T40 which contained a cofactor which failed the energy minimization step during partial charge assignment, and IT9B which contained a binding site cofactor with incomplete electron density. Outcomes using the 227 protein-ligand cognate structures taken from the 11 CD protein families (Figure  d) yielded DOCK6 (64.4 ± 2.0%) ≈ Vina (60.9 ± 1.3%) > AD4 (54.8 ± 2.4%). The small increases in standard deviations observed with this particular data set are likely a function of the large number (N=63) of relatively flexible ligands in the HIV protease CD family (#RB = 20 on avg).

  The results in Figure   indicate that PR outcomes are clearly dependent on which dataset is employed for benchmarking, and highlights the need to examine multiple datasets. In general, based on our own experiences, we would recommend including a large and reasonably diverse dataset such as SB2025. And, we envision that the new FARMA2025 dataset (conveniently a subset of SB2025) will help users gauge how their protocols perform on an unbiased group of approved drugs with their targets. We would additionally emphasize that the DOCK6 PR success rates obtained here (64–81%, Figure  ), compared to the unexpectedly low rates reported by other groups noted in the Introduction (38–58%, Table  ), are in accord with prior studies from our lab (63.8%, 72.5%, 73.3%).

  One reviewer commented that docking may be more challenging for novel or underexplored proteins and ligands compared to more well-studied targets and approved drugs (ie FARMA2025). To assess if using approved vs non-approved ligands/targets might impact docking outcomes, we examined PR success rates for two additional subsets of SB2025: (a) 1,008 systems where the ligand was not an approved drug (termed ligNA), and (b) 560 systems where the protein was not an approved drug target (termed proNA, using the inverse of “drug-target” as defined in Theoretical Methods, FARMA2025 test set criteria). The hypothesis being these subsets may reflect less well-studied systems. Interestingly, PR successes using SB2025 (DOCK6 = 74.6 ± 0.8%, Vina = 71.0 ± 0.4%, AD4 = 65.8 ± 0.4%, Figure  a) were essentially identical to using the ligNA subset (DOCK6 = 73.6 ± 0.8%, Vina = 70.4 ± 1.0%, AD4 = 64.8 ± 0.2%). And, although PR successes using the 560 proNA subset was somewhat reduced for DOCK6 and AD4, the differences were relatively small at 4–5% (DOCK6 = 70.5 ± 0.9%, Vina = 70.5 ± 1.5%, AD4 = 60.7 ± 1.1%). Together, the results suggest that docking outcomes using approved vs non-approved ligands or targets under the current conditions are similar.

### Results Part III. Cross Docking (CD), Database Enrichment (DE),
and Reverse Docking (RD)

#### CD Heatmaps
  CD experiments can help to assess if ligand poses can be accurately predicted using less than ideal protein conformations, a common situation in real-world screening campaigns. The procedure involves docking all ligands to all proteins for a group comprised of identical or nearly identical proteins co-crystallized with different ligands, or under different conditions, that were aligned to a common reference frame. Given the low standard deviations observed over triplicate PR experiments and different sized datasets, all CD calculations were run using a single seed due to their inherent similarity to PR. As seen visually in the CD heatmaps in Figure  , for some families, the three programs perform similarly in terms of success (blue), scoring failures (green), or sampling failures (red) with regards to similar patterns of matrix coverage (Table   quantifies the outcomes). In other cases, there are more dramatic differences. For example, Era for which AD4 shows more sampling failures (red cells), HMG-CoA for which DOCK6 shows more sampling failures, CAII for which AD4 interestingly yields much more success (blue cells), and HSP90 for which Vina shows almost all scoring failures. The average CD outcomes are reported in Table   as the average of “per-family” outcomes, so that this metric would not be dominated by the much larger HIV Protease family. Thus, the average cognate success rate reported here will be slightly different from those in Figure  d calculated across the 227 systems simultaneously. Interestingly while AD4 has the lowest average cognate success rate (54.9%) compared to DOCK and Vina (61.6 and 59.8%), it has the highest matrix success rate (46.4% vs. 43.7% and 43.2%).


#### CD Failures for HSP90 Rescued by Including Structural Waters
  A particularly interesting observation visible in the HSP90 cross docking heatmaps (Figure  , green), and quantified in Table  , is the considerably higher percentage of scoring failures using Vina (77.7%) compared to DOCK6 (37.6%) or AD4 (28.0%). The HSP90 family contains twelve-related ligands which form a congeneric series and one additional ligand not in the series (PDB code 2BSM). In the HSP90 binding site, the original X-ray structures for ligands from the congeneric series contain four conserved water molecules (Figure  ) which form an H-bonding network important for ligand recognition. Interestingly, only two of these four waters appear in 2BSM structure, which is not in the congeneric series. Suspecting that the absence of binding site waters in the docking setups, deleted by default in the system preparation protocols, could contribute to the high scoring failure rate for Vina (Figure  , green cells) and high sampling failure rate for DOCK6 and AD4 (Figure  , red cells), a set of docking files were prepared with binding site waters included. Notably, with binding site waters added, all of the CD matrix success rates increased as shown in Figure  . Quantitatively, Vina success increased from 19.7 to 90.3%, DOCK6 increased from 21.0 to 32.5%, and AD4 increased from 13.4 to 32.5%.

  Related to this discussion is the poor success rates observed using DOCK6 for the CAII family (Table  , 27.4%). As discussed previously in Mukherjee et al., poor CD success rates using DOCK6 for this family could be traced to relatively high sampling failures as seen in the present work (Table  , 32.3%) which could be significantly improved when crystallographic waters were included in the calculations. Given this fact, the much higher success rate obtained using AD4 for CAII without crystallographic waters, compared to the other two programs, is unusual (Table  , DOCK6 = 27.4%, Vina = 17.2%, AD4 = 59.3%). Future studies could examine additional CD families with and without structural waters, as well as the impact on DE outcomes. The choice to include or not include structural waters in real world applications is a dilemma for molecular modeling. On one hand, while including structural waters might benefit benchmarking experiments as shown above, virtual screening without explicit waters facilitates identification of ligands that can make direct interactions with the protein site being targeted, which may improve specificity.


#### DE Outcomes
  DE experiments evaluate how well a program can rank-order molecules known to have biological activity (termed actives) versus those without activity (termed decoys). Figure   shows ROC plot for 11 systems using DUD-E active and decoys while Table   provides more details including total number of molecules docked, number of actives recovered at 1% of the database (DB), and AUC analysis. To evaluate DE reproducibility, we ran the calculations in duplicate. As was observed for PR outcomes, duplicate DE outcomes were very similar as quantified here by high r 2 values (Pearsons correlation squared) computed from runA vs runB results over all 11 systems and programs, and similar averages for each program. For simplicity, only the results using 1 random seed (runA) are discussed below. For completeness, statistics using the second random seed (runB) are provided in Table  . On average, in terms of “overall” enrichment, the three programs yield larger AUC values at 100% FPR compared to random (DOCK6 = 6445.5, Vina = 6349.7, AD4 = 5492.8, random = 5000.0) with DOCK6 and Vina yielding similar outcomes. More importantly, in terms of average “early” enrichment (a more practical metric to assess virtual screening impact) all three programs yielded large AUC values compared to random at 1% FPR (DOCK6 = 9.1, Vina = 4.0, AD4 = 5.3, random = 0.5). The results in Table   highlight that a given program may yield enhanced performance relative to the others tested depending on the protein. Enhanced early enrichment (AUC@1%FPR) using DOCK6 was observed with proteins D3DR, HIV RT, EGFR, AChase, CAII, HSP90, and HIV Pro. Enhanced early enrichment for Vina was observed with protein PDase, and for AD4 with proteins ERa and HMG-CoA.

  From another perspective, the number of actives recovered at 1% divided by the number of molecules at 1% × 100 yields a “theoretical” hit rate (THR). For example, for system D3DR, 1% of the database equates to 350 molecules which is a reasonable number to purchase and experimentally test. The number of actives recovered at 1% for this system using DOCK6 yields a better THR of 21.4% (75/350) compared to 1.4% (5/350) for Vina, or 2.3% (8/350) for AD4. On the other hand, for system ERa, the DOCK6 program yields a worse THR of 39.7% compared with both Vina (52.8%) and AD4 (90.7%). The THR of 90.7% for AD4 is the highest value for this metric obtained for any of the programs for any of the systems examined. Interestingly, of all the programs, DOCK6 typically had the highest proportion of “non-dockings”, with 1.6 and 1.9% of actives and decoys, respectively, failing to dock. Non-dockings were much lower for Vina and AD4 being ∼0.2% of both actives and decoys.

  We also investigated to what extent the same exact actives would be prioritized by the different programs in terms of being in the top 1% of the DB (Table  , #Actives@1% DB column). Strikingly, with only one exception, most actives appear to be prioritized by only a single program (Figure   orange, green or purple), rather than being found at the intersection of two (Figure   yellow, blue, red) or all three (Figure  , gray). The system with the most consensus was ERa for which the same 36 active molecules (gray) were identified from the total number of unique 252 actives prioritized across all programs. These results may be related to the relatively high CD matrix success rate seen for this family shown in Table   (72.4–78.0%). We initially hypothesized that Vina and AD4 would have substantial overlap (Figure  , blue) primarily because both programs used the same smaller search space, but this does not appear to be the case. Again, the sole exception was for ERa, for which the Vina and AD4 consensus was 63 actives (Figure  , blue). Overall, DOCK6 and AD4 showed the least overlap of any pair of programs (Figure  , red). Taken together, the results in this section reinforce the notion that no single program performs best across all ligand chemotypes or receptors. While the Venn diagrams suggest that some form of “consensus” docking could be used to help maximize the recovery of actives, from a practical standpoint, the purchase and testing of up to 3 times the number of compounds (for the present scenario, if each program led to a completely different group of compounds) may not always be feasible.


#### DE and Box Size
  Interestingly, an examination of “early” enrichment (Table  , AUC@1%FPR DOCK6 = 5.7, Vina = 0.3, AD4 = 0.4) vs “overall” enrichment (Table  , AUC@100%FPR DOCK6 = 6712.6, Vina = 5747.6, AD4 = 5106.8) results for the D3DR family showed that DOCK6 yielded enhanced enrichment compared to Vina or AD4 (particularly for early enrichment), despite the fact that all three programs showed relatively good cross docking pose accuracy in terms of matrix success (Table  , DOCK6 = 76.9%, Vina = 61.5%, AD4 = 69.2%) and diagonal success (Table  , DOCK6 = 100.0%%, Vina = 100.0%, AD4 = 75.0%). To explore this observation in greater detail, we examined the pose ensembles for docked active molecules as shown in Figure  a–c. As noted earlier, DE simulations were run using a larger search space for DOCK6 (large box size) and a smaller search space for Vina and AD4 (small box size) because these protocols produced the highest pose reproduction outcomes for each program. As shown in Figure  , the resulting poses for Vina (Figure  b) and AD4 (Figure  c) appear to be butted up against the search box boundaries compared to DOCK6 (Figure  d).

  To investigate if using a larger search space might improve Vina and AD4 enrichment results for D3DR, we re-ran the simulations using the same larger box size as used for the DOCK6 calculations. Under these conditions, docked pose ensembles for Vina (Figure  e) and AD4 (Figure  f) visually appear more similar to DOCK6 (Figure  d) and both the early (AUC@1%FPR Vina = 0.3 small to 1.9 large, AD4 = 0.4 small to 0.9 large) and overall (AUC@100%FPR Vina = 5747.6 small to 7070.3 large, AD4 = 5106.8 small to 6763.4 large) enrichment statistics improved. We also re-ran DE for the remaining 10 proteins to evaluate if using Vina or AD4 with larger boxes would show similar improvement. However, for 4 out of 11 cases using Vina, and for 6 out of 11 using AD4, calculations with large vs small boxes actually lead to a reduction in performance of at least 20% in terms of early enrichment (Table S6 AUC@1%FPR large, red cells). And, the only protein systems using Vina or AD4 which showed improvement with larger boxes of at least 20% or greater were D3DR as discussed above, and AChase (Table S6 AUC@1%FPR large, green cells). On average, across all 11 systems, the AUC@1%FPR did not improve using larger boxes (Table S6 AUC@1%FPR average, Vina 4.0 small to 3.6 large, AD4 5.3 small to 2.4 large).

  Similarly, in terms of the overall enrichment, the only two systems which showed greater than 20% improvement using large vs small boxes (Table S6 AUC@100%FPR large, green cells) were D3DR (Vina) and AChase (AD4). Here, across all 11 systems the average AUC@100%FPR using large boxes marginally increased for Vina (Table S6 AUC@100%FPR average 6349.7 small to 6377.3 large) but were decreased for AD4 (5492.8 small to 5333.0 large). In contrast to the early enrichment results, only two HMG CoA (AD4) and HSP 90 (Vina) showed 20% or more degradation in overall enrichment using large vs small box sizes (Table S6 AUC@100%FPR large, red cells). Taken together, the DE and PR results underline the importance of choosing an appropriate box size for virtual screening and the inherent challenges given the fact that box size changes can lead to improvements in one benchmarking metric while decreasing another on a case-by-case basis. It is also important to emphasize that the box sizes employed in the calculations are determined based on the size of the crystallized ligand in the protein binding site. An examination of MW of the cognate ligands used to define the boxes in this section revealed that for 4 of 11 systems, including the D3DR and AChase proteins discussed above, the cognate MW was smaller by 20% or greater than the average MW of the ensemble of actives (Table S6, light blue cells). Thus, an alternative protocol to generate box sizes used for enrichment benchmarking (and more practically real-world VS projects) could involve using multiple protein-ligand X-ray structures (if available) to better define the search space. Stated another way, if a small ligand is used to generate a small bounding box for docking, then larger ligands may not be able to be properly docked in the site.


#### RD
  RD experiments seeks to identify “protein targets” for a given ligand and can be particularly useful in a drug repurposing context (see for example discussions in Gao et al. describing the Potential Drug Target Database). Given that approved drugs often undergo medicinal chemistry optimization to reduce off target effects, the drugs in FARMA2025 are expected to have more favorable scores when docked to their own cognate structure, rather than other proteins in the set. Figure   plots RD outcomes in terms of three specific metrics: cognate rank, family rank, and class rank (see Computational Details for definitions). Table   shows results using additional random seeds (3 seeds total), along with standard deviations. Across the replicates, additional seeds yielded similar results suggesting one seed was sufficient to capture RD performance, as was observed for the PR and DE experiments. For simplicity, the results in Figure  , and the accompanying discussions, focused only on the outcomes from the first random seed.

  For the cognate rank outcomes (Figure  , solid lines), in which all 165 FARMA proteins are ranked based on their docking scores with the same given ligand, the “average” position within the rank for the exact crystallographic protein-ligand cognate pair shows that DOCK6 (17.1) and Vina (22.0) perform similarly followed by AD4 (31.1). Here, ranks closer to 1 indicate enhanced selection ability which corresponds to smaller areas under the curve. For context, random <Cognate Rank> outcomes would be (1+165)/2 = 83.0. As expected, when the metric changes to average family rank outcomes (Figure  , dashed lines), for which any structure within the same protein family would be considered to be a “correct” protein target, there is improvement relative to the average cognate ranks however the effect was relatively minor. For DOCK6 (avg family rank = 15.4, avg cognate rank = 17.1, difference = −1.7) and Vina (avg family rank = 20.3, avg cognate rank = 22.0, difference = −1.7) the improvements were coincidentally the same at −1.7, however the improvement was somewhat larger for AD4 (avg family rank = 27.1, avg cognate rank = 31.1, difference = −4.0). The somewhat larger improvements seen for AD4 may be related to AD4 also yielding the overall highest average CD “matrix success rate” (Table  , DOCK6 = 43.7%, Vina = 40.6%, AD4 = 46.4%) suggesting the program may be more tolerant of conformational changes in binding sites. For the average class rank outcomes (Figure  , dotted lines), which are based on the rank order of the first protein needing only to be in the correct “protein class”, the results improve dramatically relative to average cognate or average family ranks which is to be expected as there are more available pairings that could be favorably ranked. In this case, the results are similar across all three programs (DOCK6 = 7.0, Vina = 6.9, AD4 = 8.0).

  In terms of scoring the correct cognate protein-drug pair best (Figure  , cognate first rank, abbreviated CFR), from among all other 164 potential decoy pairings (165–1 = 164), the results follow DOCK6 = 28.5% > Vina = 20.0% > AD4 = 10.9% (percentages closer to 100% are desirable). Figure   also reports the “pose accurate-cognate first rank” results (termed PA-CFR) which is the subset of CFR systems in which the predicted ligand poses match the X-ray poses to within 2 angstroms. Interestingly, the PA-CFR rates (Figure  , DOCK = 91.5%, Vina = 90.9%, AD4 = 77.8%) are markedly higher than PR accuracy on the entire FARMA2025 set (Figure  b, DOCK = 78.9%, Vina = 76.0%, AD4 = 67.3%) by 12.6%, 14.9%, and 10.5% for DOCK, Vina, and AD4, respectively. Given that the CFR subset is enriched for accurate poses, this suggests that to rank protein targets accurately, generating the correct ligand pose in the target structure is crucial but not sufficient. Overall, the RD experiments in this section show that all three docking programs are moderately adept at identifying the correct protein structure for a particular approved drug.


## Conclusions
  In conclusion, the primary contributions of this study are the development of two new docking test sets to help validate molecular modeling software and the use of the test sets to benchmark three widely used docking programs: DOCK6, Autodock Vina (Vina), and Autodock 4 (AD4). Building upon our previously published SB2010 (N = 780) and SB2012 (N = 1043) databases, this work introduces SB2025 (N = 1244) and FARMA2025 (N = 165), of which the latter is a subset of protein-drug complexes organized such that the percentage of given proteins is closely aligned with 19 major protein classes relative to the number of approved compounds for that class. FARMA2025 provides a more complete representation of approved drug targets compared to either of our two previous test sets, or the well-known Astex2007 test set (Table  , Figure  ), and we believe will be a useful tool for the community to evaluate software for drug discovery and design. The study employed four specific benchmark metrics: pose reproduction (PR), cross docking (CD), database enrichment (DE), and reverse docking (RD). We also examined multiple variables which could affect docking outcome including use of different RMSD calculations (Figure  ), ligand search space and exhaustiveness parameters (Table  ), and ligand partial atomic charges (Figure  ). While not examined here, it is worthwhile noting that test sets such as SB2025 and FARMA2025 are valuable when evaluating de novo design and molecular evolution algorithms. −

  The PR studies (benchmark #1), which evaluate how well a program can recapitulate known crystallographic binding geometries, suggest that DOCK6 performed best using a larger search space (i.e., grid-based bounding box) and AD4 performed best using a smaller search space. Vina performed similarly in both sized spaces, provided that the exhaustiveness of the search was high enough (Table  ). DOCK6 had nearly the same PR success rate with Gasteiger charges (72.9%) vs AM1-BCC charges (74.6%) while AD4 showed a slight preference for Gasteiger (Figure  ). For DOCK6 in particular, this was an important finding because the recently released DOCK6 de novo design method (DOCK_DN), ,−   and genetic algorithm (DOCK_GA), which can be used to create new ligands from scratch and/or modify existing compounds make use of on-the-fly Gasteiger charge assignment. We also explored how the three programs performed using different test sets and across different protein classes. For the larger SB2025 set (Figure  a, N = 1244), small drug-focused FARMA2025 set (Figure  b, N = 165) and cross docking subset (Figure  d, N = 227, matrix diagonal only), DOCK6 showed slightly higher PR success than Vina, followed by AD4. Interestingly, all three programs yielded near identical performance on the smaller Astex2007 set (Figure  c, N = 82 systems). And, across 9 protein classes (Figure  ), all three programs showed roughly similar outcomes in terms of PR success rates “trends” ranked from high (nuclear receptors) to low (PDase).

  As a general rule, CD outcomes (benchmark #2), in which different 3D complexes from the same protein family are aligned and all ligands docked to all proteins, were more mixed with different programs yielding increased or decreased successes and/or failures (Figure  , Table  ) depending on the system(s) being studied. Some noteworthy differences, especially for the bigger protein families, include larger number of sampling failures for AD4 with ERa, larger number of sampling failures for DOCK with HMG-CoA, decreased success rates for Vina with EGFR, increased success rates for AD4 with CAII, and a particularly large number of scoring failures for Vina with HSP90. The observations underscore the potential benefits of assessing multiple docking methods or protocols on systems prior to embarking on resource intensive projects. For HSP90 in particular, the outcomes prompted us to re-run the simulations with crystallographic waters (Figure  ) which dramatically improved the Vina CD matrix success results from 19.7 to 90.3%. Another noteworthy result is that although the AD4 program yielded the overall lowest pose accuracy across cognate pairings (Table   Success, Diagonal Averages, DOCK6 = 61.6%, Vina = 59.8%, AD4 = 54.9%) it also yielded the overall highest CD pose accuracy across cognate plus off-diagonal pairings (Table   Success, Matrix Averages, DOCK6 = 43.7%, Vina = 43.2%, AD4 = 46.4%), suggesting the program is less impacted by minor variations in side chain packing.

  For the DE outcomes (benchmark #3), in which active and decoy ligands are docked to a target to assess if actives score better, we also observed that the performance of any given program depends on the system and the specific enrichment metric being considered (Figure  , Table  ). Focusing on the early enrichment results, on average, all three programs yielded AUC@1%FPR results (DOCK6 = 9.1, Vina = 4.0, AD4 = 5.3) which were substantially greater than random (random = 0.5). It should be emphasized that strong DE (Table  ) does not always correlate with good PR accuracy (Table  ) and vice versa. For example, for the CAII family, DOCK6 yielded good DE enrichment (AUC@1%FPR = 5.2) but low cross docking PR accuracy (matrix success = 27.4%) while AD4 showed the opposite trend (AUC@1%FPR = 0, matrix success = 59.3%). This underscores further the importance of assessing multiple metrics when benchmarking. Interestingly, with only one exception (system ERa), the DE result showed that the different programs select for different active ligands in the top 1% of the database with only minimal overlap (Figure  ).

  To evaluate RD (benchmark #4), we used the new FARMA2025 test set to gauge how well the programs could identify the correct protein “partner” for a given drug (Figure  ), based on the rank compared to decoy structures (values closer to 1 preferred), using three classification levels ranging from most to least difficult (cognate rank > family rank > class rank). For the challenging cognate-based level, average rank accuracy ranged from 17.1 (DOCK6) to 22.0 (Vina) to 31.1 (AD4). As expected, the more inclusive family-based level showed improved average ranks of 15.4 (DOCK6) to 20.3 (Vina) to 27.1 (AD4). For the most promiscuous class-based ranks, all three programs performed similarly (6.9–8.0). Depending on the program, the accuracy with which the exact cognate protein-drug pair was ranked first (Figure  , CFR metric) ranged from 28.5% (DOCK6) to 20.0% (Vina) to AD4 (10.9%). Notably, PR success rate for the subset of CFR systems (Figure  , PA-CFR metric) was quite high across all programs (77.8–91.5%) suggesting that to accurately rank the correct protein target best, it is necessary (although not sufficient) to generate a correct ligand pose.

  Planned future work includes the continued expansion of our validation sets to incorporate other approved drugs as they become available, as well as less well-studied proteins and ligands such as those which may be present in newer PDB depositions. And, while some parameter analysis was presented in the current study, it would also be worthwhile to perform a more systematic optimization of parameter space that would impact docking outcomes such as grid search size and grid spacing. Another direction could explore the contributions of scoring by rescoring poses generated by one program with the scoring functions of another. For example, DOCK6 poses could be rescored using Vina or AD4 scoring functions, and vice versa, to help distinguish the influence of a given function from that of the sampling algorithm, for different ranking tasks. The observations that DOCK6 failures for PR are dominated by scoring, while CD matrix failures are dominated by sampling, suggest evaluating new scoring schemes in conjunction with receptor flexibility would be particularly important goals for future DOCK development. For Vina, as both PR and CD appear to be dominated by failures in scoring, evaluating new scoring functions might instead be more worthwhile to explore. Conversely, as the dominant failures for AD4 appears to involve sampling, improvements in sampling algorithms could be prioritized.

  From a big picture and real-world application perspective, we believe it is important to employ docking parameters and protocols recommended by the developers. For example, the PR success rates reported here for DOCK6 and Vina, are in closer agreement with prior DOCK6 studies from our group, and the original Vina publication, compared to other studies outlined in Table  , for which non-standard grid parameters, at least for DOCK6, likely contributed to poorer outcomes. Our results also suggest that more sampling, at least with regards to the exhaustiveness parameters varied here, does not guarantee meaningful improvements to PR success, and that other parameters or algorithms should be explored. Another observation is that replicate calculations, using the current setups and protocols, leads to reasonably reproducible outcomes involving averages or aggregate values across all three programs, suggesting that one random seed would likely be sufficient with regards to performing prospective virtual screens. Finally, if possible, we would strongly recommend that new users perform as least some benchmarking on known systems to verify that their specific setup and protocols for new systems are sound. Importantly, the resources available as an outcome of this study will help facilitate such studies. The new SB2025 and FARMA2025 test sets, along with resources for performing benchmark calculations and analysis, are freely available for download on our laboratory’s Wiki and GitHub pages (see Methods for links). Bug reports, and suggestions for improving future versions of the benchmarking test sets are encouraged by contacting the DOCK fans mailing list at dock_fans@googlegroups.com.
