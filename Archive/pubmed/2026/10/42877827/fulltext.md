# Engineering catalytic promiscuity in serum albumin: synergistic roles of microenvironment, structural dynamics, and nano conjugation


## Abstract
  This study investigates the catalytic hydrolysis of p-nitrophenyl acetate (PNPA) in the presence of bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a biocatalyst, with an emphasis on the role of protein conformation, substrate concentration, and environmental factors. Further experiments with varying BSA and PNPA concentrations demonstrated Michaelis–Menten-type kinetics and a positive correlation between BSA concentration and the rate of hydrolysis. This effect was attributed to increased availability of catalytically active histidine residues and potential conformational changes that expose active sites. Comparisons with α-lactalbumin and free histidine under similar conditions indicated the superior catalytic efficiency of BSA, due to both crowding effects and its structural microenvironment. The effects of pH, ionic strength, and substrate alkyl chain length were also examined, revealing optimal catalytic activity at pH 7.4 and a significant rate enhancement for substrates with extended hydrophobic regions, likely due to stronger BSA–substrate interactions. Additionally, BSA structural alterations through denaturation, PEGylation, and gold nanoparticle (Au-NPs) conjugation were evaluated, showing varied catalytic performance due to differences in rigidity and nature of microenvironment etc. The molecular docking studies indicated the involvement of not only histidine but also serine and arginine residues in the catalytic activity.


## Introduction
  Developing biocompatible catalysts that operate under mild conditions for ester hydrolysis is critically important due to their broad applicability in sustainable chemical processes, pharmaceutical synthesis, and environmental remediation. Traditional ester hydrolysis often relies on harsh acidic or basic conditions and elevated temperatures, which can lead to undesired side reactions, energy inefficiency, and environmental hazards.1 Biocompatible catalysts, such as proteins or biomimetic systems, offer a green alternative by enabling reactions to proceed efficiently at physiological pH and ambient temperatures. This approach minimizes energy consumption and chemical waste, aligning with principles of green chemistry.2 Furthermore, such catalysts are inherently non-toxic and compatible with biological systems, making them ideal for applications in drug development, where controlled hydrolysis of prodrugs is necessary, and in biomedical devices requiring gentle conditions. Their use in environmental contexts, such as the breakdown of pollutants or waste, also ensures minimal ecological disruption.3 Thus, these catalysts represent a crucial step toward more sustainable and versatile chemical technologies.

  In this context, a protein, such as bovine serum albumin (BSA) can be considered as a good alternative due to its unique combination of biocompatibility, specificity, and eco-friendliness. Its structured binding pockets and functional residues enable selective interactions with ester substrates, supporting targeted hydrolysis under mild physiological conditions, without the need for extreme pH or high temperatures required by synthetic catalysts. BSA is cost-effective, widely available, and has a high thermal and chemical stability that maintains catalytic activity across diverse conditions.4 Its antifouling properties prevent non-specific adsorption, preserving catalytic function, while its adaptability for immobilization on surfaces or matrices allows for controlled release and reusability.5 BSA is also versatile, as it can be chemically modified, engineered, or integrated with nanomaterials to create hybrid systems with enhanced catalytic or multifunctional properties, while its biodegradable origin minimizes environmental impact, and its low immunogenicity makes it safer for use in human or animal exposure.6,7 These qualities make BSA an effective, sustainable, and adaptable choice for catalytic applications in both biological and industrial settings.

  Herein we have utilized BSA as biocatalyst for ester hydrolysis at physiological pH in buffered medium (Fig. 1). The effects of microenvironment, as defined by pH and ionic strength of the medium, on the catalytic activity of BSA have been investigated in detail. Though BSA showed better catalytic activity over HSA protein, hydrolysis of phosphate ester appeared to be difficult even with BSA under the ambient condition. Interestingly, ester derivative with long alkyl chain showed faster hydrolysis compared to corresponding methyl ester. Further, we synthesized various BSA composites by reacting it with a polymer (PEG-400), and incorporating gold nanoparticles (Au-NPs). The catalytic activities of these systems were evaluated and subsequently compared with the native BSA.


## Results and discussion

### Kinetics study of hydrolysis of PNPA using BSA as a bio-catalyst
  To examine the catalytic activity of Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) on hydrolysis of p-nitrophenyl acetate (PNPA), the time-course experiment was conducted in a buffered medium at pH 7.4 (Fig. 2a). The UV-visible spectrum of native PNPA showed a major absorption band at 270 nm region with a molar extinction coefficient value (ε) of 1.54 × 104 M−1 cm−1, this was probably due to the π–π* transition.8 The addition of BSA (0.5 mg mL−1) led to an increase in absorbance at the 405 nm band (ε = 15 500 M−1 cm−1) with a concomitant hypochromic shift at the 280 nm region. The saturation in the optical signal was observed after ∼30 min. The formation of this new absorption band at the 405 nm region indicated the formation of p-nitrophenolate ion in the reaction medium.9 The kinetics study indicated a pseudo-first order reaction with a rate constant value 0.028 min−1. The rate constant was calculated using the equation:1where kobs is the pseudo-first-order rate constant, t is the time at which absorbance was measured, A0 and At are the absorbance at zero and t min, and A∞ is the final absorbance when the reaction was completed. The rate constant (kobs) value was determined from the slope of a linear plot of log[(A∞ − A0)/(A∞ − At)] vs. t, at a constant initial substrate concentration.

  To comment on the hydrolytic efficiency of BSA, we also used a phosphoester derivative (diethyl 4-nitrophenyl phosphate, DENP) as substrate. Under similar reaction conditions, BSA showed negligible catalytic activity on the hydrolysis of DENP (Fig. 2b). This is due to the higher stability of the P–O bond compared to the C–O bond due to the larger size and higher electronegativity difference between phosphorus and oxygen.10,11 To systematically study the catalytic efficacy of the BSA on ester hydrolysis, we have followed the time course experiments at different substrate concentrations (PNPA) against a fixed amount of catalyst (BSA) and vice versa. Also, the effect of microenvironment, such as pH and temperature of the surroundings, on the hydrolysis of PNPA has been investigated, etc.


### Effect of substrate and catalyst concentrations on ester hydrolysis
  At first, we have monitored the hydrolysis of PNPA in the presence of BSA across a range of concentrations. A notable increase in the rate constant from 0.020 to 0.072 min−1 was observed with rising BSA concentrations from 0.25 to 1.25 mg mL−1 (Fig. 2d). This enhancement in reaction rate may be attributed to the availability of the catalytically active binding site of BSA, which can facilitate catalytic interactions with the substrate.12,13 Additionally, at higher concentrations, BSA might induce “crowding” effects, which may contribute to the observed kinetic enhancement by increasing the effective local concentration of PNPA near the protein surface and promoting a greater probability of productive collisions. Following the initial observation of BSA-enhanced hydrolysis rates, we extended our study to examine how variations in substrate concentrations affect the reaction kinetics.14

  Furthermore, we investigated the kinetics of the hydrolysis reaction at various substrate concentrations ranging from 5 µM to 25 µM, keeping the BSA concentration fixed at 0.5 mg mL−1. This study demonstrates that the hydrolysis of PNPA in the presence of BSA follows typical Michaelis–Menten kinetics. The absorbance vs. time plot showed a concentration-dependent increase in the initial rate (Fig. 2c). At low substrate concentrations, the reaction rate increases linearly with PNPA concentration, as most of the catalytic or binding sites on BSA remain unoccupied. As the substrate concentration increases, the chances that all of the available binding or catalytic sites on BSA are fully occupied. This can lead to maximal catalytic efficiency until the BSA molecules become saturated with PNPA. Thus, to better understand the kinetics, a Lineweaver–Burk plot is constructed by plotting the reciprocal of the initial reaction rate (1/V) against the reciprocal of the substrate concentration (1/[S]). The linearized form of the Michaelis–Menten equation is given by:2where V and Vmax are the initial hydrolysis rate and the maximum hydrolysis rate, Km is the Michaelis constant, and [S] is the substrate concentration. Here, the substrate concentration ranged from 5 µM to 100 µM. Thus, the dependence of 1/V and 1/[S] is shown in Fig. S1; it can be seen that 1/V is linear to 1/[S]. From the linear fit, the kinetic parameters were obtained as Km = 162.5 µM and Vmax = 0.436 min−1. The linear relationship observed between 1/V and 1/[S] confirms that the catalytic reaction follows Michaelis–Menten-type kinetics.


### Structure–activity relationship in protein-catalysed PNPA hydrolysis
  To gain deeper mechanistic insight into protein-mediated catalysis, we extended our study to compare the hydrolytic activity of different proteins. We observed a significant decrease in the rate of PNPA hydrolysis when another protein, α-lactalbumin, was involved as the catalyst under similar conditions (rate constant = 0.007 min−1) (Fig. 3a). Both α-lactalbumin and BSA have isoelectric points in the range of ∼4.5–4.7, which essentially indicate almost identical charge distribution at pH 7.4 medium. However, unlike BSA, lactalbumin possess only 4 histidine residues.15,16 Since histidine can be considered as the primary interaction site for the catalytic hydrolysis of PNPA, we would expect a decrease in the rate of the reaction on going from BSA to lactalbumin protein. Although histidine is the main catalytic site, the distinct microenvironment provided by the native protein, along with the crowding effect and improved solubility, could promote the rate of ester hydrolysis.17 To evaluate this hypothesis, we examined the hydrolysis of PNPA in the presence free l-histidine, l-serine, and l-arginine individually at concentrations corresponding to their approximate abundance in BSA. l-Histidine showed a pseudo-first-order rate constant of 0.0029 min−1, whereas l-serine and l-arginine exhibited lower rate constants of 0.00121 min−1 and 0.00132 min−1, respectively. In comparison, native BSA displayed a substantially higher rate constant of 0.028 min−1, indicating that the individual amino acid residues alone exhibit only limited catalytic activity. These results demonstrate that although histidine plays a more prominent catalytic role than serine or arginine, the significantly enhanced hydrolytic activity of BSA arises from the cooperative contribution of multiple amino acid residues within the structured protein microenvironment, which facilitates efficient substrate binding and catalysis (Fig. S9).

  To further investigate the contribution of histidine residues to the catalytic activity of BSA, a selective chemical modification experiment was performed using diethyl pyrocarbonate (DEPC), a histidine-targeting reagent. As shown in Fig. S11, DEPC treatment substantially reduced the catalytic activity of BSA (rate constant = 0.010 min−1) toward PNPA hydrolysis compared with untreated BSA (rate constant = 0.028 min−1). The progressive loss of catalytic activity upon DEPC-mediated modification supports the involvement of histidine residues in the ester hydrolysis process. This observation is consistent with the enhanced catalytic activity observed for free histidine, which suggest favorable interactions between PNPA and histidine-containing regions of BSA. Although DEPC modification may also perturb the local protein microenvironment, the observed decrease in catalytic activity provides additional evidence supporting the contribution of histidine residues to BSA-mediated ester hydrolysis.

  When ester hydrolysis was monitored in the presence of HSA as a catalyst under identical reaction conditions, we observed a slight decrease in the reaction rate (0.015 min−1). This might be due to structural and functional differences between the two proteins. BSA has more histidine residues (16 compared to 10 in HSA), which are critical for catalytic activity in ester hydrolysis.18,19 Additionally, the tertiary structure of BSA and hydrophobic binding pockets are better optimized for substrate binding and transition state stabilization, enhancing its catalytic efficiency.20,21


### Effect of microenvironment on ester hydrolysis
  To elucidate the role of protein structural organization in governing pseudo-enzymatic activity, the catalytic behaviour of thermally denatured BSA was systematically investigated. It is well established that increasing temperature induces a sequence of reversible or irreversible conformational transitions in serum albumins, ultimately leading to partial unfolding and structural destabilization.22 For instance, Far-UV circular dichroism analysis revealed a significant reduction in the characteristic α-helical signature of BSA upon heat treatment, indicating partial unfolding and disruption of its native secondary structure (Fig. 3b).23,24 Complementary spectroscopic evidence further supported structural perturbation of the protein. The denatured BSA exhibited an absorption maxima peak at around 275 nm, slightly blue shifted (∼5 nm) as compared to native BSA, consistent with altered microenvironmental polarity surrounding aromatic residues. Additionally, the denatured BSA showed substantially low emission intensity compared to the native protein, which corroborated with previous literature reports (Fig. S2).25 Consistent with this structural perturbation, catalytic studies showed a pronounced initial increase in the absorbance at 0 min time in PNPA hydrolysis in the presence of denatured BSA, attributed to the exposure of previously buried nucleophilic and catalytic histidine residues, which enhances substrate accessibility and promotes rapid ester cleavage (Fig. S3). However, after the initial rapid increase in absorbance, the rate of the reaction was found to be sluggish (k = 0.013 min−1) (Fig. 3c). This indicated that denatured protein likely undergoes aggregation and loses the organized microenvironment necessary for efficient catalysis, resulting in diminished hydrolytic efficiency. These findings highlight the critical role of native protein architecture in sustaining pseudo-enzymatic activity.26

  Since the microenvironment of the catalytic site could influence the kinetics of the hydrolysis reaction, we have investigated the role of pH and ionic strength on the extent of ester hydrolysis by BSA protein. When the hydrolysis reaction was monitored under similar conditions in the presence of 40 mM NaCl, a 2.3-fold decrease in the overall reaction rate was observed (Fig. 3d). This might be due to the salting-out effect of NaCl, which substantially decreases the solubility of BSA in the aqueous medium.27 Hence, the effectiveness of the BSA catalyst towards the PNPA was compromised, resulting in a decrease in the reaction rate. Additionally, the observed kinetic suppression can be attributed to ionic strength-mediated electrostatic screening, which diminishes favourable substrate–protein interactions and reduces the probability of productive catalytic encounters. Furthermore, salt-induced perturbation of the hydration shell and local microenvironment around catalytic residues may influence transition-state stabilization and substrate accessibility.28

  The pH of the solution plays a crucial role in the efficiency of imidazole as a catalyst, since imidazole exists in equilibrium between its protonated and deprotonated forms depending on the pH. As expected, the PNPA hydrolysis catalyzed by BSA exhibited a pronounced pH dependence (Fig. 4a and b). As shown in Fig. S12, the rate constant increased progressively with increasing pH, with a marked increase observed between pH 7 and 8, followed by a near-plateau in the alkaline pH range. This behavior suggests that the catalytic activity is strongly influenced by the ionization state of one or more functional groups within the catalytic microenvironment. The imidazole group of histidine, whose pKa can be modulated by the local protein environment, may contribute to this pH-dependent catalytic behavior by participating in proton transfer and general-base catalysis during ester hydrolysis. At lower pH, protonation of the imidazole group may limit its ability to function as a general base, whereas increasing the pH favors the deprotonated form, which is more suitable for proton-transfer-mediated catalysis. The near-plateau observed at higher pH values may arise from the combined effects of the ionization states of other amino acid residues, changes in the protein microenvironment, and the contribution of hydroxide ions to ester hydrolysis. Thus, the observed pH dependence supports the involvement of an ionizable catalytic group, with histidine being a plausible contributor to the general-base catalytic process.29


### Effect of alkyl chain length on ester hydrolysis
  Further, we compared the extent of ester hydrolysis for two different substrates with varying lengths of alkyl substituents (R = CH3, PNPA vs. C11H23, PNPD). The rate of hydrolysis was found to be faster for PNPD (k = 0.038 min−1) compound than that observed with PNPA under similar condition (at 25 °C, pH 7.4) (Fig. 4c). The faster hydrolysis of PNPD is likely due to several factors related to substrate binding and protein interaction. Long-chain ester compounds possess extended hydrophobic regions, which can interact favourably with the hydrophobic pockets in BSA, allowing for stronger binding affinity and more stable enzyme–substrate complex formation.30 This anchoring effect would bring the substrate closer to catalytic groups, such as imidazole of histidine residue, facilitating faster hydrolysis. Also, the reduced mobility of long-chain substrates within the binding site of BSA could make such probe molecules available for repeated catalytic action, resulting in faster reaction rates compared to short-chain esters, such as PNPA.


### Catalytic activity of BSA-polymer composite
  Further, we have compared the efficiency of polyethylene glycol (PEG-400)-BSA composite towards the hydrolysis of PNPA. PEG, known for its hydrophobic interactions with protein surfaces, can induce conformational changes in BSA, thereby altering its structural stability as well as functional properties.31,32 The UV-visible spectra of PEG-BSA composite showed very high molar absorptivity compared to native BSA (Fig. S4). Probably due to enhanced light scattering from the macromolecular crowding induced by PEG, alongside minor changes in the aromatic microenvironment that amplify chromophore oscillator strength. Concomitantly, a substantial decrease in fluorescence emission intensity was observed, which may be attributed to static quenching arising from PEG–protein interactions and reduced conformational flexibility of the protein matrix.33 The far-UV CD spectra of PEG-BSA closely resemble that of native BSA, indicating preservation of the secondary structural integrity upon polymer complexation (Fig. S5). These combined spectroscopic observations suggest that PEG-400 induces conformational rearrangement and changes in the local protein microenvironment without extensive disruption of the overall secondary structure. In agreement with these spectroscopic observations, catalytic studies revealed a decrease in the rate of PNPA hydrolysis in the presence of the PEG-BSA composite (k = 0.015 min−1) compared to native BSA (0.028 min−1) under identical reaction conditions (Fig. 4d). This reduction in catalytic activity may arise from altered substrate accessibility and restricted conformational dynamics of the protein upon PEG interaction, which could hinder the optimal positioning of PNPA near the catalytic residues. Thus, the observed decrease in catalytic activity is likely associated with changes in the protein microenvironment and functional dynamics induced by PEG-400 rather than extensive disruption of the protein's secondary structure.34,35 On the other hand, PEG alone showed negligible catalytic activity toward PNPA hydrolysis (k = 0.002 min−1), confirming that the observed reaction is primarily protein-mediated (Fig. S6).


### Catalytic activity of BSA-Au NPs composite
  The nanoparticle–protein composite was synthesized following the literature-reported procedure.36 The UV-visible spectrum of the composite showed tailing in the region of 300–380 nm. The tailing in the 300–380 nm region for BSA-AuNPs arose from the interaction of BSA with the nanoparticle surface, causing plasmon damping and overlapping absorption features.37,38 The BSA-functionalized nanoparticles (BSA-NPs) exhibited a notable reduced in absorbance intensity compared to the native BSA (Fig. 5a). This was accompanied by a moderate red shift in the absorption peak and a broadening of the absorption band, which are indicative of BSA adsorption onto the surface of the gold nanoparticles (AuNPs). Fluorescence (FL) spectroscopy further confirmed the interaction between BSA and AuNPs through changes in the intrinsic fluorescence of BSA, suggesting alterations in the microenvironment of aromatic amino acid residues (Fig. S7a). Far-UV circular dichroism measurements further revealed a decrease in negative ellipticity, indicating partial perturbation of the native secondary structure of BSA upon binding to AuNPs (Fig. S7b). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis showed that the BSA-AuNP composite possessed an average hydrodynamic diameter of 129 ± 20 nm, indicating the formation of stable protein-coated nanoparticles (Fig. S7c). The inset FE-SEM micrograph showed a homogeneous distribution of the nanoparticles with minimal aggregation, further confirming the successful synthesis of the BSA-AuNP composite (Fig. S7d). When the BSA-AuNP composite was utilized as a catalyst for ester hydrolysis, the reaction proceeded at a significantly faster rate (k = 0.041 min−1) than that catalyzed by native BSA (k = 0.028 min−1) (Fig. 5b). In contrast, bare AuNPs and citrate-stabilized AuNPs exhibited negligible catalytic activity, with pseudo-first-order rate constants of 0.001 min−1 and 0.002 min−1, respectively (Fig. S8). These control experiments demonstrate that the enhanced catalytic activity does not originate from the gold nanoparticles themselves but rather from the synergistic interaction between BSA and the AuNP surface. Immobilization of BSA on the nanoparticles likely increases the accessibility of catalytically active amino acid residues (e.g., histidine and lysine) while providing a favorable local microenvironment for substrate binding and catalysis.39 Furthermore, interactions between BSA and the AuNP surface may stabilize the protein conformation, thereby preserving its catalytic functionality and contributing to the improved reaction kinetics. Overall, the BSA-AuNP hybrid system combines enhanced catalytic efficiency with improved structural stability, making it a promising protein–nanoparticle biocatalyst.40–44


### Docking studies of BSA protein with ester derivatives
  Docking studies were conducted between PNPA and PNPD with the BSA protein (PDB ID-4f5s). The binding affinities of the PNPA-BSA complex were calculated as −6.4, −5.9, −5.6, and −5.3 kcal mol−1 at sites A, B, C, and D, respectively, highlighting site A as exhibiting the highest binding affinity for PNPA. Similarly, for PNPD, four possible conformations of the PNPD-BSA complex were identified, with binding affinities of −7.3 (site A), −7.1 (site B), −6.4 (site C), and −6.2 kcal mol−1 (site D). Subsequent analysis of PNPD focused on site A due to its maximum binding affinity. A comparison of affinity scores revealed that PNPD binds more strongly to the BSA protein than PNPA (Fig. S15).

  The PNPA molecule interacted with the BSA protein through a variety of non-covalent interactions with neighbouring amino acid residues. For example, HIS 145 and SER 192 participated in hydrogen bonding with the acyl ester group of PNPA, while ARG 435 and LYS 431 formed salt bridges with the nitro group. Additionally, ARG 458 engaged in a cation–π interaction with the phenyl unit (Fig. 6a). Similarly, PNPD demonstrated interactions with amino acid residues such as HIS 145 and ARG 185, which formed hydrogen bonds with the acyl ester group. This finding indicates that the hydrolysis of ester derivatives, such as PNPA and PNPD, primarily occurs with histidine residues (imidazole units) acting as the active catalytic site (for BSA). ASP 111 and LEU 112 were involved in hydrogen bonding with the nitro group. Like PNPA, ARG 144 formed a cation–π interaction with the phenyl unit in PNPD (Fig. 6b).45 However, unlike PNPA, the long-chain unit of PNPD engaged in alkyl–alkyl, π-alkyl, and van der Waals interactions with several amino acid residues, which may account for the higher binding affinity of PNPD.46–50


## Conclusion
  In this study, we have systematically investigated the catalytic potential of BSA in the hydrolysis of a simple ester, PNPA, highlighting the influence of substrate, catalyst concentration, and microenvironment on reaction kinetics. The results demonstrated that BSA, due to its tertiary structure and accessible histidine residues, significantly accelerates PNPA hydrolysis under optimized conditions, following Michaelis–Menten kinetics. Enhanced reaction rates with increasing BSA concentrations suggest a role for histidine-rich regions in facilitating catalysis, which we corroborated by comparing hydrolysis rates with another protein, α-lactalbumin, and isolated histidine, both showing significantly lower activity. Furthermore, we observed that pH and ionic strength modulate the catalytic efficiency, with pH 7.4 being ideal due to balanced protonation states of histidine residues. The impact of substrate alkyl chain length revealed that ester derivative with longer alkyl residue experienced faster hydrolysis, likely due to enhanced binding with the hydrophobic pockets of BSA. Modifying BSA through polymer conjugation (PEG) resulted in diminished catalytic activity, indicating that structural flexibility is crucial for optimal catalytic function. On the contrary, functionalization with NPs improved the catalytic activity presumably by exposing larger number of catalytic sites (histidine, lysine etc) and creating suitable microenvironment. Finally, from molecular docking studies we could identify that along with histidine, other amino acid residues, such as serine and arginine were also responsible for catalysis of ester derivative. Not only that, we witnessed sufficiently high binding affinity for ester derivative with a long alkyl chain, PNPD compared to corresponding methyl ester. This corroborated well with experimental observation.
