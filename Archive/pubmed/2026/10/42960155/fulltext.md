# Aptamers without SELEX: De novo design from protein structure in hours


## Abstract
  Systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX)-based aptamer discovery can be constrained by target availability, selection biases, and optimization requirements. We introduce Xelari, a modular pipeline for de novo DNA and RNA aptamer design from protein structures without target-specific SELEX data or retraining. Xelari combines algorithmic modules with surrogate models trained on physical or structural references. InNA reproduced nucleotide-amino-acid interaction energies with RMSE of 0.19 kcal/mol across 483,735 reference pairs. AliNA achieved a mean F1 of 0.757 on 339 PDB-derived RNA evaluation sequences. Edgar correlated with experimental folding energies but did not reproduce their absolute scale, supporting its use as a ranking heuristic. Under the reported calibration, ValentiNA showed MAE of 0.931 kcal/mol and Spearman ρ of 0.795 across 32 retrospective records associated with 27 structural templates; this analysis does not establish family-independent validation. Prospectively, three DNA aptamers designed against Treponema pallidum protein structures showed cell-associated binding in a treponeme assay (apparent KD, 0.91–2.57 μM). A fourth DNA aptamer served as a capture reagent in a serum-based hybrid aptamer-antibody assay for Tau441. These applications support feasibility without establishing intended-target engagement in the cellular assay or a general success rate, positioning structure-based design as a complement to experimental selection.


## Introduction
  Systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX), introduced in 1990,1 remains the principal experimental approach for aptamer discovery. Aptamers have several practical properties that distinguish them from protein binders, including chemical synthesis, access to backbone and sugar modifications, relatively low intrinsic immunogenicity, and compatibility with targets that can be difficult to address using conventional antibody-generation methods.2,3,4 Their clinical translation, however, depends on substantially more than the discovery of a high-affinity sequence. To date, the FDA has approved two aptamer drugs5: pegaptanib (2004, against VEGF) and avacincaptad pegol (2023, against C5).6,7 The limited clinical adoption of aptamers therefore cannot be attributed to SELEX alone. Nevertheless, SELEX imposes constraints at the discovery and optimization stages that motivate complementary approaches to aptamer generation.8

  Many conventional protein-target SELEX protocols require preparative quantities of purified target in its native conformation. For a significant proportion of biologically relevant proteins, this requirement remains technically difficult: integral membrane proteins, metastable complexes, proteins with post-translational modifications and disordered regions are difficult to express recombinantly or lose their functional conformation during solubilization and purification9; such targets remain correspondingly underrepresented among well-characterized proteins.10 The screening itself starts with a library of ∼1015 random sequences and covers less than 10−9 the total space of possible sequences (440 ≈ 1024 for a 40-nucleotide randomized region).11 Between selection rounds, amplification can introduce sequence- and structure-dependent biases that alter pool composition.8,12 Selection is also performed under defined conditions of buffer composition, ionic strength, temperature, and target presentation. Candidates enriched under these conditions can show different binding properties when transferred to other experimental or physiological environments.8,12 For practical use, the aptamer must carry chemical modifications that ensure nuclease stability, assay compatibility, and acceptable pharmacokinetic properties (2′-fluoro, 2′-O-methyl, phosphorothioate, terminal protection against exonucleases, conjugation with PEG).2,13,14 When modifications are introduced after selection, they can alter the conformational dynamics and binding interface, which can lead to a loss of affinity or specificity, after which a separate cycle of re-optimization is required.2,14

  Given these methodological constraints, computational approaches to aptamer design are now perceived by the community as a path to a rational search independent of SELEX constraints, following the impact of deep-learning structure prediction and de novo design methods in protein engineering.15,16

  Existing in silico tools for aptamer design fall broadly into two strategies. Sequence-only methods operate on the primary sequences of the aptamer and target and are trained on one-dimensional data from the literature or, more often, on HT-SELEX pools. They have been implemented as discriminative predictors of aptamer-protein binding,17,18,19 language-model-based approaches, and generative models.20,21,22,23,24,25 Sequence-only approaches infer aptamer properties from primary sequences without explicitly representing the three-dimensional geometry of the aptamer-protein interface. This can limit physical interpretability and may reduce robustness under distribution shift, particularly for target classes, structural motifs, or nucleotide chemistries that are underrepresented or absent from the training data. RNA secondary-structure prediction itself remains challenging for pseudoknotted RNAs.26 More generally, representations based on a single predicted conformation do not explicitly capture ensembles of energetically accessible aptamer states or their dependence on solution conditions. These limitations motivate complementary structure-based approaches that explicitly represent the three-dimensional target interface and the physicochemical determinants of molecular recognition.

  Structure-based and physics-based methods construct predictions from the 3D geometry of the complex. In terms of implementation—classical molecular dynamics and empirical docking—these methods do not require parameterization on target-specific aptamer-protein datasets, as they rely on general-purpose physical models of molecular interactions. These methods provide physically interpretable descriptions of interaction contributions (electrostatic, van der Waals, hydrogen bonding, and desolvation) and allow the effects of chemical modifications to be modeled prior to synthesis. The applicability of this strategy to de novo design requires the simultaneous solution of a set of interrelated subproblems: prepare the target structure, localize the binding site, construct the binding conformation of the aptamer, predict its secondary and tertiary structure, evaluate the free energies of folding and binding, and account for chemical modifications. For several of these subtasks, a reproducible physical or computational reference physical solution exists; however, the computational cost of direct reference calculations is incompatible with screening 104–106 candidates. Structure-prediction architectures capable of modeling protein-nucleic-acid complexes (RoseTTAFoldNA27 and AlphaFold328) provide another route, but experimentally resolved aptamer-protein complexes remain limited in number and substantially redundant at the target and aptamer-family levels.29 This limits the size and diversity of aptamer-specific structural training sets and, particularly, independent assessment of generalization to new targets.

  Combined with the limitations of the sequence-only approach outlined earlier, these constraints leave an important methodological gap in de novo aptamer design. Neither approach alone fully addresses the challenge of efficiently discovering aptamers against previously uncharacterized targets.

  We address that gap by decomposing the de novo aptamer design problem into sequential subproblems. For those subtasks for which a reproducible physical reference method can be applied at scale, this makes it possible to generate training data without target-specific SELEX experiments and to train specialized surrogate models that approximate the corresponding reference calculations at substantially lower computational cost.30 The strategy of machine-learned surrogates has been developing in computational chemistry for nearly two decades and has led to a number of established solutions in quantum chemistry and molecular property prediction.31,32,33,34,35,36 Here, we apply the same principle across multiple coupled stages of rational aptamer design.

  Here, we present the Xelari platform—a pipeline for the rational de novo design of aptamers against protein targets with available three-dimensional structures. Time-consuming physical calculations are replaced, where applicable, by surrogate models trained on physical reference data, while other stages of the pipeline are implemented as algorithmic modules. This decomposition is intended to reduce dependence on target-specific aptamer data while retaining explicit structural and energetic information throughout the design process. Active per-target computation requires approximately 2–3 h on a single GPU; the observed wall-clock time on shared GPU infrastructure remains below 24 h and includes scheduling latency, while scaffold-library construction is performed offline. We benchmark individual modules retrospectively against PDB-derived and other reference datasets and evaluate prospective application in two distinct experimental settings. Three de novo DNA aptamers designed against Treponema pallidum proteins were evaluated in a reported whole-cell treponeme-binding assay.37 In a second application, an Xelari-designed DNA aptamer against human Tau441 functioned as the capture reagent in a serum-based hybrid aptamer-antibody assay.38 Because the Tau441 study did not provide an aptamer-only 1:1 dissociation constant or independent mapping of the aptamer-binding epitope, we treat it as evidence of functional deployment in an orthogonal assay context rather than as quantitative affinity validation. Together, these prospective studies show that Xelari-generated candidates can retain experimental function after synthesis in more than one biological and assay setting, but they do not establish a general target-level success rate or superiority over experimental selection. The workflow is therefore intended to provide candidates for direct experimental testing when target-specific selection is difficult. The reported runtime describes computational candidate generation and does not include synthesis or experimental confirmation.


## Results

### Xelari architecture
  Xelari uses the three-dimensional structure of a protein target to generate an affinity-ranked list of aptamer sequences and corresponding target-bound complex models. The input is an experimentally determined or in silico-predicted protein structure in mmCIF or PDB format, together with user-defined inputs including aptamer length, optional target-site or surface-region specification, target and nucleotide modifications, desired affinity, ionic composition, temperature, and specificity. If no binding region is specified, the binding site is selected automatically. For each candidate, Xelari reports the nucleotide sequence, predicted secondary structure in dot-bracket notation, specified modifications, and a relaxed three-dimensional model of the aptamer-protein complex.

  The pipeline consists of nine sequential stages (Figure 1), implemented by eight modules. The computational part consists of five machine-learning models (INGA, InNA, AliNA, Edgar, and ValentiNA) and three algorithmic modules (Ehrlich, DiNA, and MoNA) that do not require training on data. The trained models are applied to new protein targets without additional fitting or fine-tuning. Together, the modules perform target preparation and relaxation, surface-site selection, construction of short target-contacting nucleotide fragments, assembly of full-length aptamer candidates, assessment of secondary structure and folding stability, complex relaxation, and final affinity ranking. Once these user-defined inputs are specified, candidate generation, filtering, ranking, and final candidate selection proceed automatically without manual intervention. All model weights and algorithmic parameters used during prediction are fixed before application to a new target and are not adjusted on a per-target basis.Figure 1Architecture of the Xelari de novo aptamer design pipeline

  Three learned modules serve as surrogates for computationally expensive energetic calculations. InNA is a model trained on r2SCAN-3c density-functional theory (DFT) calculations that approximates nucleotide-amino-acid residue interaction energies and is used during DiNA seed-fragment construction. Edgar model estimates nucleic acid folding free energy for thermodynamic filtering and is trained on umbrella-sampling MD calculations. ValentiNA model estimates aptamer-protein binding free energy and performs the final ranking of candidates. The remaining trained models address structural tasks: AliNA predicts nucleic-acid secondary structure, whereas INGA is used for conformational relaxation. The performance of these components against their respective structural, computational, or experimental reference data is evaluated separately later.

  On a single NVIDIA H200 GPU, the per-target computation requires approximately 2–3 h. This includes completion and relaxation of the input protein structure, binding-site selection, seed construction, scaffold assembly and filtering, complex relaxation, and affinity ranking. The scaffold library is generated offline and reused across targets and is therefore not included in the per-target runtime. On the shared GPU infrastructure, scheduling and resource-allocation delays increased the observed wall-clock time, which remained below 24 h per target.


### Target preparation
  The first stage of the pipeline converts the input protein structure into a complete, atomistic model for subsequent surface analysis and seed-fragment assembly. Missing atoms, incomplete side chains, and short backbone gaps are reconstructed using PDBFixer (OpenMM package),39 while larger missing regions are reconstructed with Boltz-2 using the input structure as a template.40 Standard crystallographic additives are removed, while ligands and cofactors are transferred to the reconstructed model (see materials and methods). Because subsequent site selection and aptamer construction depend directly on the local target geometry, the reliability of these stages is limited by the structural quality of the input model, particularly within the candidate binding region.

  The reconstructed structure undergoes structural relaxation with INGA (see materials and methods)—a Conditional Flow Matching model based on an adapted Equiformer v3 architecture and trained on molecular dynamics trajectories.41,42 INGA is designed to predict a conformation of the most populated conformational cluster sampled in the corresponding MD trajectory (architecture details in Section S1). Conventional geometry optimization methods such as GFN-FF converge to local potential energy minima and cannot overcome large energy barriers. Molecular dynamics can traverse multiple stable basins but tends to stagnate within each basin for many steps before escaping. INGA is designed to complement these approaches and can be applied alongside them for accelerated stable-state search.

  INGA was evaluated on the Chignolin benchmark.43 The most populated cluster in the reference MD trajectory was designated as the target state. Inputs were sampled separately from the target cluster, non-target clusters, and sparsely populated regions of the conformational landscape (see materials and methods). The resulting conformational distributions and transitions between clusters are shown in Figure 2. Because INGA is stochastic, five predictions were generated for each input, and the output with the fewest bond-length violations was retained for analysis (see materials and methods and Table S1).Figure 2INGA results on ChignolinChignolin conformations from molecular dynamics trajectories projected onto the first two time-lagged independent components (TICA 0 and TICA 1).   For inputs from alternative populated clusters, raw INGA moved 53.1% of structures into the target cluster, compared with 0.0% after GFN-FF relaxation of the input alone. After GFN-FF refinement of the INGA outputs, 55.7% were in the target cluster. For sparsely sampled starting conformations, target-cluster recovery was 25.6% with GFN-FF alone, 59.2% after INGA, and 56.8% after subsequent GFN-FF refinement. In contrast, for inputs already within the target cluster, retention after the complete INGA-GFN-FF step was 50.4%, compared with 96.4% after GFN-FF alone (Table S1). Thus, INGA promoted transitions toward the dominant MD-sampled basin primarily for structures originating outside that basin, while retention of structures that already occupied that basin was reduced.

  Raw INGA outputs contained substantial energetic and geometric artifacts: mean potential energy increased by 170–211 eV relative to the input across the three validation sets, the mean number of unphysical bond lengths per structure was 2.51–14.36, and up to 28% of outputs contained more than five unphysical bond lengths. GFN-FF refinement of the INGA outputs reduced the residual energy increase to 1.8–5.3 eV but did not resolve the bond-length artifacts introduced by INGA: the mean number of unphysical bond lengths (3.28–16.96) and the fail rate (up to 30%) were slightly higher after refinement than in the raw outputs (Table S1). Following GFN-FF refinement, structures undergo a 1-ns GROMACS relaxation, after which a representative conformation from the most populated trajectory cluster is selected for downstream surface analysis. This relaxation is performed using the temperature and ionic conditions specified by the user, allowing these parameters to affect the molecular environment and the relaxed structure passed to downstream stages.


### Analysis of surface site specificity
  The reconstructed and relaxed structure is passed to the Ehrlich, an algorithmic module that ranks surface regions by how distinguishable they are from the surfaces of other proteins. Ehrlich is used as a specificity-oriented pre-filter: it restricts subsequent aptamer construction to locally distinctive regions of the target.

  The protein surface is divided into overlapping local patches, each represented by descriptors of surface geometry and local amino acid composition. For a target patch p, the geometric and compositional components of its match to the reference surface library are denoted G(p) and C(p), respectively, and combined as:(Equation 1)F(p)=αgG(p)+αcC(p)where both components are mapped to a common normalized scale and combined with equal weights αg = αc (materials and methods and supplemental information). Lower values of F(p) correspond to locally more distinctive target regions in the operational Ehrlich score. Candidate patches are retained when F(p) ≤ τ, with τ = 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, and 0.2 corresponding to the predefined Low, Medium, High, and Very High specificity settings, respectively.

  We first examined whether the Ehrlich surface representation distinguishes local surface patterns associated with the same antigen from those assigned to unrelated antigens. The benchmark comprised 14 non-homologous antigens. Within each antigen, local surface patches were grouped into 4–5 representative surface clusters, each represented by its medoid patch, yielding 68 medoid patches in total (Table S2; materials and methods). Pairwise comparison of these 68 representatives yielded 2,278 unordered pairs: 132 within-antigen comparisons and 2,146 between-antigen comparisons (Figure 3A).Figure 3Heatmap of predicted similarity between different proteins(A) 68 × 68 symmetric matrix of Ehrlich pairwise similarities computed for 14 non-homologous antigens (4–5 representative surface-patch medoids per antigen obtained by PFH-space clustering). On-diagonal blocks correspond to intra-protein pairs (n = 132; mean = 0.88 ± 0.13, median = 0.93); off-diagonal entries correspond to inter-protein pairs (n = 2,146; mean = 0.16 ± 0.10, median = 0.14).   The two groups were strongly separated. Within-antigen similarities had a mean of 0.88 ± 0.13 and a median of 0.93, whereas between-antigen similarities had a mean of 0.16 ± 0.10 and a median of 0.14. Within this benchmark, Ehrlich assigned substantially higher similarity scores to surface clusters from the same antigen than to clusters from unrelated antigens, demonstrating strong discrimination between the two groups.

  The degree of within-antigen similarity varied substantially across the panel. Several targets showed highly homogeneous surface-cluster representations, with mean within-antigen similarities ≥0.94 and minimum values ≥0.85. By contrast, PDB: 3A67, 4F9P, and 5U8Q contained markedly more divergent local surface clusters, with mean within-antigen similarities of 0.72, 0.72, and 0.79 and minimum values of 0.47, 0.25, and 0.49, respectively. This variation indicates that the local surface landscape is not equally homogeneous across targets and supports retaining multiple representative patches in Ehrlich rather than reducing each antigen to a single averaged surface descriptor.

  The separation between within- and between-antigen comparisons was not absolute. Several unrelated antigen pairs contained individual surface-cluster medoids with comparatively high similarity. The highest cross-antigen similarities were observed for 2UZI-3ETB, 2UZI-2XTJ, and 2UZI-3UYP (PDB: 2UZI, 3ETB, 2XTJ, 3UYP) with maximum pairwise similarities of 0.68, 0.58, and 0.61, respectively. These high-scoring cross-antigen comparisons indicate local surface similarity according to the Ehrlich descriptor, but do not by themselves establish epitope mimicry or experimental cross-reactivity.

  We next tested whether the same representation recovered interface similarity in an external dataset. Ehrlich was applied to the 729 protein-interface pairs derived from the PatchBag interface dataset,44 spanning 191 PDB structures and 65 Pfam families (Figure 3B). Interface pairs associated with the same Pfam-family groups showed systematically higher Ehrlich similarity than pairs drawn from unrelated families, reproducing the principal ordering reported for the reference dataset. Importantly, Pfam annotations and pairwise sequence identity are not used to calculate the Ehrlich score, which is based on local surface geometry and amino acid composition.

  Together, the internal and external benchmarks support the use of Ehrlich as a pre-screen for local surface similarity before aptamer construction.


### Localization of the binding site and assembly of seed fragments on the target surface
  DiNA module constructs short oligonucleotide seed fragments directly on candidate target-surface regions identified by Ehrlich. Rather than docking a complete oligonucleotide, DiNA first ranks candidate surface regions and then generates 4–6-nt seed fragments along the selected protein surface. Once a candidate backbone has been constructed, nucleotide identities are selected using InNA pairwise interaction-energy estimates for amino acid residues within 15 Å of each seed position. InNA reproduces r2SCAN-3c reference interaction energies with an RMSE of approximately 0.19 kcal mol−1 per nucleotide-amino-acid pair (discussed later). Site-ranking weights and seed-construction hyperparameters are provided in Tables S3 and S4, respectively.

  We evaluated whether DiNA can localize an experimentally observed aptamer-binding site from the protein structure without an anchor constraint or information about the bound aptamer. The benchmark contains 38 protein-aptamer co-crystal structures representing 19 distinct protein targets, of which 12 are represented by RNA aptamers and 7 by DNA aptamers. DiNA was run with the same default settings for each target, and the top-ranked surface region returned by the site-ranking procedure was used for seed construction. Localization accuracy was quantified by DCC, the Euclidean distance between the centroid of the predicted seed population and the centroid of the experimentally observed protein-aptamer contact zone (materials and methods), following the center-distance convention used in pocket-localization benchmarks.45

  11 of the 19 protein targets are represented by a single reference complex and therefore require no aggregation across alternative crystallographic binding sites. DiNA localized the predicted seed region within 5 Å of the experimental-site centroid for 10 of these 11 targets, with a median DCC of 3.14 Å and a range of 1.92–5.38 Å (7 RNA-associated and 4 DNA-associated targets). This subset provides the most direct localization assessment because each target is associated with a single reference site in the benchmark.

  The remaining eight targets are represented by 2–12 co-crystal structures. These structures cannot in all cases be treated as replicate observations of a single site because different aptamer sequences can occupy spatially distinct regions of the same protein. This effect is particularly pronounced for thrombin, which is represented by 12 reference complexes: one structure gives a DCC of approximately 2.5 Å, whereas the other 11 give values of 23.0–34.8 Å. A single default DiNA run returns one top-ranked site and therefore cannot simultaneously reproduce every alternative site occupied by different aptamers. Across the 27 structures belonging to these eight multi-reference targets, 8 structures fall within 5 Å; when each target is instead scored against its closest-matching experimentally observed site, 7 of 8 targets fall within 5 Å.

  Results are therefore reported under two complementary aggregation schemes. At the target level, a protein is considered localized if the predicted region lies within 5 Å of at least one experimentally observed aptamer-binding-site centroid available for that target. Under this any-known-site criterion, 17 of 19 targets (89%) were localized within 5 Å, with a median DCC of 3.14 Å and an interquartile range of 2.70–4.50 Å (Figure 4D). Median DCC values were 3.17 Å for the 12 RNA-associated targets and 3.14 Å for the 7 DNA-associated targets. When all 38 co-crystal structures are instead scored independently, thereby asking whether DiNA recovers the particular site occupied in each individual complex, 18 of 38 structures (47%) fall within 5 Å, and the median DCC is 5.25 Å.

  At the target level, the two predictions above the 5 Å threshold were PDB: 3V7E at 5.38 Å and PDB: 6DB8 at 10.99 Å. All 19 targets were within 15 Å of at least one experimentally observed site centroid.


### The InNA pairwise interaction scoring function reproduces the r2SCAN-3c reference energies with sub-kcal/mol accuracy
  InNA provides a computational surrogate for the fixed-geometry interaction energy of a nucleotide-amino-acid residue pair and is used by DiNA to score local nucleotide placements at the protein surface. For each pair, InNA predicts the interaction energy as the sum of atom-wise contributions (materials and methods). Reference energies were obtained using the composite DFT method r2SCAN-3c in the ORCA 5 program.46,47 The individual molecular fragments were pre-optimized with GFN2-xTB,48 whereas their relative pair geometries were systematically sampled without subsequent pair relaxation.

  The complete InNA reference dataset contains 13.09 million nucleotide-amino-acid pair geometries. Quantitative analysis in Figure 5 was performed on a curated evaluation panel of 483,735 geometries comprising seven amino-acid-nucleotide combinations, with 69,105 geometries per combination. Across this panel, InNA reproduces the reference values of r2SCAN-3c with R2 = 0.9972, RMSE ≈ 0.19 kcal/mol per pair (Figure 5A). The energies of nucleotide-amino acid residue pair interactions in the test set range from −19.0 to +56.0 kcal/mol, with a median of −1.5 kcal/mol (Figure 5B). The typical binding contact depth (median among attractive pairs, n = 373,189) is −2.1 kcal/mol, while strong binding contacts (5th percentile) reach −6.35 kcal/mol (Figure 5C).Figure 5InNA-predicted versus r2SCAN-3c reference interaction energies(A) Model-predicted versus r2SCAN-3c reference interaction energies for the curated evaluation panel of 483,735 nucleotide-amino-acid pair geometries.   Prediction accuracy was preserved within the favorable-energy range most relevant to local contact scoring. RMSE was 0.194 kcal/mol for pair geometries with Eref < 0 and 0.193 kcal/mol for the most favorable 5% of the complete evaluation panel (Eref ≤ −5.91 kcal/mol). Thus, InNA maintained comparable absolute error across the complete panel and the favorable-energy subsets.

  To assess transfer across amino acid identity, lysine and glutamic acid were excluded entirely from the training and validation sets and evaluated only after model fitting (Figure 5D). Across the combined LYS and GLU subset (n = 138,210), InNA achieved R2 = 0.9975 and RMSE = 0.193 kcal/mol, comparable with the five amino acid identities represented during training (n = 345,525; R2 = 0.9971, RMSE = 0.194 kcal/mol). Individually, LYS-DC and GLU-DG yielded R2 = 0.9968 and 0.9978, respectively, with RMSE values of 0.193 kcal/mol for both subsets (Table S5). These results show no detectable loss of prediction accuracy for these two held-out amino acid identities under the same geometry-generation protocol.

  The approximately 0.19 kcal/mol error is small relative to the several-kcal/mol interaction-energy scale of favorable pair geometries in the evaluation panel, supporting the use of InNA as a local r2SCAN-3c surrogate during DiNA seed construction. InNA is not used as a direct estimator of whole-complex binding free energy; final affinity estimation is performed downstream by ValentiNA.


### Predicting the secondary structure of candidate sequences
  Full-length aptamer assembly requires identification of nucleotide positions that participate in intrachain base pairing and those that remain single-stranded and available for target contacts. Xelari uses AliNA, a previously published U-Net/Transformer secondary-structure predictor,49 to provide this topological representation during scaffold construction and post-assembly topology control. The published network architecture was preserved in a lighter configuration; all model weights were retrained de novo using an updated two-stage training scheme (materials and methods).

  After two-stage retraining, AliNA reached a mean F1 of 0.758 on 306 PDB-derived RNA sequences in the higher-similarity stratum and 0.749 on 33 sequences in the lower-similarity stratum (Table S6). The 339-sequence PDB benchmark was excluded from AliNA model-weight training and was disjoint from the training corpus at the assigned Rfam-family level. Performance was similar across the two sequence-similarity strata, although the lower-similarity subset was small (n = 33). We examined pseudoknots separately, since most secondary-structure predictors do not model them: only the higher-similarity RNA subset contained enough pseudoknotted structures for stable statistics (n = 86), where AliNA reached F1 = 0.645, while the lower-similarity RNA subset and the ssDNA set contained only 14 and 4 such structures, respectively, and were not scored. On the PDB-derived single-stranded DNA set, the mean F1 was 0.810 and the median 0.971 over the 58 structures for which AliNA returned a prediction (Table S7).

  We next asked whether AliNA-predicted secondary structures were compatible with the downstream three-dimensional reconstruction step used by Xelari. For 13 non-G-quadruplex RNA aptamers with experimentally determined PDB structures, AliNA predictions agreed with base pairs extracted from the experimental coordinates with a median F1 score of 0.767 (mean, 0.778). Supplying the AliNA prediction to trRosettaRNA2 changed the median RMSD from 2.04 to 2.00 Å and the median TM-score from 0.324 to 0.330; the mean ΔTM-score was +0.018, and the median was +0.00950; with positive changes in 11 out of 13 structures and ΔTM-score >0.05 in 2 out of 13.

  Two conclusions follow, and they are narrower than the aggregate numbers suggest. First, AliNA-derived topology is compatible with the reconstruction step: supplying it changed reconstruction quality only marginally, which is what the architecture predicts, since trRosettaRNA2 consumes external secondary structure as an auxiliary input feature rather than as a rigid geometric restraint. Second, this is a compatibility test and not an independent benchmark of trRosettaRNA2, because some of these PDB structures may be represented in its PDB-derived training data; the small ΔTM-score therefore cannot be read as a generalization result for either model. What the panel does establish is the property the pipeline actually requires of this module: AliNA-predicted base pairs agree with those extracted from the corresponding experimental coordinates closely enough to serve as topological input to reconstruction.

  Because Xelari uses an RNA-based three-dimensional reconstruction stack, DNA candidates are reconstructed initially in RNA representation and subsequently converted to DNA before molecular-dynamics refinement. RNA and DNA are not assumed to be structurally interchangeable in this workflow: the RNA representation is used only as an intermediate coordinate representation during three-dimensional reconstruction, whereas secondary structure is predicted directly from the ssDNA sequence, and downstream refinement is performed using DNA-specific molecular-dynamics parameters. RNA-specific tertiary interactions that depend on the ribose 2′-hydroxyl are therefore not assumed to be preserved by the coordinate-conversion step.

  We evaluated the structural consequences of this procedure at both local and whole-structure levels. In the post-relaxation scaffold population, evaluated before application of the final B-like-geometry acceptance filter, 86.3% of converted DNA chains were classified as B-like, 13.7% as mixed, and none as A-like, whereas 99.5% of RNA chains were classified as A-like. Median sugar-pseudorotation phase shifted from p = 24.1° in RNA to 149.3° in DNA, and the North/C3′-endo sugar-pucker fraction decreased from 92.9% to 19.5%. Median backbone torsion δ shifted from 82.0° to 139.3°; the corresponding median glycosidic torsion χ values were −156.1° and −113.6°, respectively (Table S8). These changes indicate that molecular-dynamics refinement drives most converted DNA scaffolds away from the RNA-like local sugar-backbone geometry inherited at the initial coordinate-conversion step.

  We evaluated the DNA branch of the reconstruction workflow on 58 experimentally resolved ssDNA structures from the PDB (7–53 nt). After RNA-based reconstruction, coordinate conversion to DNA and molecular-dynamics refinement, the refined models matched the experimental structures with a median RMSD of 2.52 Å (mean, 3.24 Å) and a median lDDT of 0.724, with RMSD below 3 Å in 35 of 58 cases (Table S7). Both metrics are computed over heavy atoms that could be placed in one-to-one correspondence between model and reference, so they characterize the paired core of the fold and are not whole-molecule errors: unpaired and non-corresponding regions do not enter them and neither metric addresses protein-binding energetics after conversion.


### Folding free energy estimation
  Direct calculation of nucleic-acid folding free energies by enhanced-sampling methods such as umbrella sampling (US) or adaptive biasing force is incompatible with the throughput required during candidate screening,51,52 where approximately 103 assembled structures may need to be evaluated. Xelari therefore uses Edgar as a surrogate for the corresponding simulation-derived folding free-energy calculation. Edgar predicts folding free energy from nucleotide sequence and secondary structure without explicit three-dimensional simulation at inference.

  Edgar was trained on folding-energy labels derived from SMD/US calculations. On a held-out computational test set of 5,290 structures, it reproduced the simulation-derived reference values with R2 = 0.958 and RMSE = 0.91 kcal/mol, at approximately 1.7 ms per 100-nt sequence on a consumer-grade GPU. Because training and internal test labels were generated by the same simulation protocol, this benchmark measures how closely Edgar approximates its computational reference and nothing more. It is also not homology-controlled for DNA: sequence augmentation of the DNA subset preceded random partitioning, so augmented variants of one parent structure can occur in more than one subset (materials and methods). The question that matters for the pipeline is therefore how the model behaves against experiment.

  We evaluated transfer to an external literature compilation of experimental folding energies. Of 57 records, 56 had both experimental and predicted values (36 RNA and 20 DNA); Mathews39 lacked a prediction and was excluded. Without experimental recalibration, Pearson r was 0.846 and Spearman ρ was 0.829. However, MAE was 6.69 kcal/mol, RMSE was 8.49 kcal/mol and predictive R2 was −3.976. The squared Pearson correlation was 0.716 and is distinct from predictive R2. Regression of predicted on experimental energy gave a slope of 1.793 and an intercept of −3.489 kcal/mol (Figure 6). ### Generation and structural validation of the scaffold library
  MoNA assembles full-length aptamer candidates by grafting DiNA-derived target-contacting seed fragments onto backbones drawn from a precomputed scaffold library. Scaffold generation is performed offline and independently of the target and is therefore excluded from the per-target runtime. Candidate sequences are filtered at the sequence and secondary-structure levels, stratified by length, and selected to increase diversity in base-pair topology. For duplicate predicted topologies, the sequence with the more favorable Edgar-predicted folding free energy is retained. Selected scaffolds are reconstructed in three dimensions with trRosettaRNA2 using the AliNA-predicted secondary structure as an auxiliary structural input and reconstruction quality is controlled by agreement between the intended and coordinate-derived base-pair patterns (materials and methods).

  Reconstructed scaffolds are subsequently placed in protein-associated geometries by docking against a receptor set and subjected to explicit-solvent molecular-dynamics relaxation before final structural filtering and deduplication. This conditioning step is not used to infer affinity for the receptor proteins; rather, it exposes scaffold conformations to a protein-surface environment before they enter the reusable library. The current library contains approximately 12,000 RNA and 11,000 DNA single-chain scaffolds spanning 15–45 nt (Figure S1).

  The DNA branch requires an additional reconstruction step because trRosettaRNA2 generates RNA coordinates. DNA scaffolds are therefore reconstructed initially in RNA representation and then converted to DNA with naskit, which changes nucleotide chemistry while initially retaining the RNA-derived sugar geometry. We asked whether converted structures remained trapped in an RNA-like backbone state after molecular-dynamics relaxation. In the post-relaxation DNA population evaluated before the final B-like-geometry acceptance filter, 86.3% of chains were classified as B-like, 13.7% as mixed and none as A-like; by comparison, 99.5% of relaxed RNA chains were classified as A-like and 0.5% as mixed. Median pseudorotation phase P and backbone torsion δ were 149.3° and 139.3°, respectively, for DNA, compared with 24.1° and 82.0° for RNA; the north/C3′-endo sugar-pucker fraction was 19.5% for DNA and 92.9% for RNA (Table S8).

  These distributions show that the converted scaffolds do leave the RNA-like sugar-backbone geometry inherited at the coordinate-conversion step: after refinement, 86.3% of DNA chains classify as B-form and none as A-form, against 99.5% A-form for the RNA models, with the median pseudorotation phase angle moving from 24.1° to 149.3°, the median δ torsion from 82.0° to 139.3°, and the north-pucker fraction from 92.9% to 19.5%. The analysis is aggregate and local: it establishes that relaxation reaches DNA-like backbone geometry, not that the tertiary fold or the protein-binding energetics survive conversion. It is complementary to the structural benchmark above, which reports a median paired-heavy-atom RMSD of 2.52 Å with values below 3 Å for 35 of 58 structures.

  The current scaffold library does not enumerate G-quadruplex folds, and MoNA does not generate such topologies during graft assembly. G-quadruplex aptamers, a structurally distinct and therapeutically explored class,53,54,55 are therefore outside the present generative scope of Xelari.


### Assembly of full-length aptamers from the scaffold library
  MoNA incorporates DiNA-derived seed fragments onto geometrically compatible scaffold positions of precomputed scaffolds. Candidate alignments are generated from base-independent sugar-phosphate triads, filtered for steric overlap and generalized Gauss linking number and then grafted into the selected scaffold. Grafting is performed so that the local contact geometry used during DiNA scoring is retained as closely as possible; the rest of the scaffold provides the surrounding fold context (materials and methods; Table S9).

  Because grafting changes the seed-region sequence, the parent base-pairing pattern can be disrupted. MoNA re-predicts each assembled sequence with AliNA. If agreement with the parent scaffold is F1 < 0.8, compensatory mutagenesis searches paired positions outside the seed region for substitutions that improve topology recovery while minimizing changes in GC content. The search stops at F1 = 0.8 or after 1,000 iterations. Candidates that reach the iteration limit retain their final F1 and remain eligible for downstream ranking; F1 = 0.8 is a recovery target, not a hard acceptance filter.

  We evaluated 500 MoNA-assembled RNA sequences of 20–25 nt immediately after seed grafting and before compensatory mutagenesis (Table S10). At this stage, median agreement between the AliNA-predicted assembled topology and the parent scaffold topology was F1 = 0.75 (mean, 0.67). Of the 500 assemblies, 208 (41.6%) already met the predefined topology-recovery target of F1 ≥ 0.8, whereas 292 (58.4%) fell below this threshold; 42 assemblies (8.4%) recovered the parent topology exactly (F1 = 1.0). Compensatory mutagenesis was subsequently applied to the 292 subthreshold assemblies. All 292 reached F1 ≥ 0.8 within the allowed 1,000 iterations, resulting in 500 of 500 assemblies (100%) meeting the operational topology-recovery target after mutagenesis. Thus, seed grafting disrupted the parent secondary-structure topology below the predefined threshold in 58.4% of assemblies, while the compensatory-mutagenesis procedure restored all subthreshold cases to F1 ≥ 0.8 in this benchmark.

  Using the pre-mutagenesis F1 values, we compared the Edgar-predicted folding energy of each assembled topology with the minimum-free-energy (MFE) reference for the same sequence. Overall, 326 of 500 candidates (65.2%) were within 1 kcal/mol of that reference. Candidates with pre-mutagenesis F1 ≥ 0.8 were more often within this interval than candidates with pre-mutagenesis F1 < 0.8 (73% versus 60%) and had smaller absolute energy offsets (median, 0.15 versus 0.70 kcal/mol; Mann-Whitney p = 1.0 × 10−4). Pre-mutagenesis F1 was weakly inversely associated with the signed energy offset (Spearman ρ = −0.21, p = 1.3 × 10−6). Because the MFE reference and Edgar score come from different energy models, we treat this comparison as a descriptive cross-model consistency check, not as a thermodynamic bound or a test of structural identity. Fifty-seven candidates (11.4%) scored below the MFE reference.

  Topology recovery and energy offset were correlated, so they should not be treated as independent evidence for fold validity. The benchmark starts from already assembled candidates and therefore does not report the fraction of all attempted seed-scaffold poses that survive alignment, steric filtering, and GLN filtering. The topology and energy statistics reported above refer to the pre-mutagenesis assembled candidates. Compensatory mutagenesis was then applied to the 292 candidates with F1 < 0.8, all of which reached the predefined F1 ≥ 0.8 topology-recovery target. The benchmark contains RNA assemblies only.

  After assembly, candidates are ranked by the summed InNA interaction energies of the grafted residues with the target, and the highest-ranked complexes are passed to INGA relaxation and ValentiNA affinity prediction.

INGA is first used to relax the bound geometry toward a locally stable conformation. The resulting complex is then subjected to explicit-solvent GROMACS relaxation using the temperature and ionic conditions specified by the user before ValentiNA scoring. We do not interpret this relaxation step as evidence that the global free-energy minimum has been reached.

### Introduction of chemical modifications
  For supported chemistries, modifications are included in the molecular model before complex relaxation and ValentiNA scoring. The model therefore evaluates the modified complex directly. Only modifications with an explicit molecular representation were considered.

  The panel contains 16 chemically modified and 16 canonical records. Spearman ρ was 0.735 for modified and 0.565 for canonical records; MAE was 1.180 and 0.683 kcal/mol, respectively (Table S15). The classes differ in target composition, affinity range, and experimental conditions. The higher error in the modified group cannot therefore be attributed to chemistry alone, and the panel does not establish chemistry-independent absolute calibration.

  We further examined three comparison sets representing different types of chemical modification (Table S12). In the thrombin-binding TBA series, native TBA, and three C5-substituted variants share the same 15-nt G-quadruplex scaffold and exosite-I binding site. Experimental affinities followed the order W4+W12 > TBA-T4K > TBA-T4W > native TBA, and ValentiNA reproduced the same ordering. The TBA-T4W/TBA-T4K pair provides the most direct comparison because the modification occurs at the same nucleotide position. This analysis concerns scoring of existing G4 complexes; Xelari does not currently generate G-quadruplex aptamers.

  In the Apt8 series, Apt8, Apt8-1, and Apt8-2 share the same 23-nt base sequence and IgG1 Fc target but differ in their 2′-F/2′-deoxy pattern. Their measured KD values were 75.0, 25.5, and 110.6 nM, respectively, and ValentiNA reproduced the experimental order, Apt8-1 > Apt8 > Apt8-2. Thus, the model captured both an increase and a decrease in affinity produced by changes in sugar chemistry. The predicted difference between Apt8 and Apt8-1 was smaller than the experimental difference, so this comparison supports directional ranking rather than quantitative ΔΔG prediction.

  The AF113 pair (PDB: 3DD2, 5DO4) probes a backbone modification within one anti-thrombin RNA-aptamer lineage. We used the paired BLI measurements reported at 303 K, which place the native AF113-1 aptamer at KD = 1.871 nM and the phosphorodithioate variant AF113-18 at 1.8 pM, corresponding to ΔΔG = −4.18 kcal/mol. ValentiNA ranked the variant correctly, with calibrated values of −12.77 and −14.34 kcal/mol, but recovered only about a third of the effect (ΔΔG = −1.57 kcal/mol). Two features restrict the comparison with direction rather than magnitude. The deposited 5DO4 construct differs from the native scaffold by more than the phosphorodithioate linkage, also carrying 2′-methylselenyl-uridine at two positions and a different 2′-F substitution pattern, so the measured difference is not attributable to a single chemical change. And the structural model used for the native scaffold is the Toggle-25t deposit 3DD2, whose own filter-binding affinity (0.54 nM, Table S12) differs from the BLI value used for the energetic comparison; structure and affinity therefore come from related but not identical constructs and assays.

  Across the three comparison sets, ValentiNA reproduced the experimental direction or ordering for C5, sugar, and backbone modifications, while the magnitudes of the predicted effects were not quantitatively reproduced and could be either attenuated or exaggerated. Together with the chemistry-stratified benchmark, these results support qualitative modification-aware affinity ranking for the evaluated chemistries, but not quantitative prediction of the effect of an individual substitution or selection of optimal modification sites.


### Prospective experimental validation
  Module-level benchmarks do not establish whether the complete workflow produces experimentally functional sequences. We therefore evaluated Xelari prospectively in two settings that differ in target biology and experimental readout: binding to intact treponeme cells and capture of human Tau441 in a serum-based sandwich assay.


### Prospective live-cell binding by Xelari-designed aptamers
  Xelari was applied prospectively to three T. pallidum lipoproteins: Tpp17 (PDB: 4U3Q), Tpp47 (PDB: 1O75), and Tp0751 (PDB: 5JK2). T. pallidum is technically demanding to maintain in continuous host-cell-free culture, which makes selection directly against intact cells challenging.57 No target-specific aptamer prototypes or SELEX data were used, and no target-specific selection rounds were performed: candidate generation and ranking started from the deposited target structures, with automatic selection of the final candidate. The resulting DNA aptamers were synthesized with a 5′ Alexa Fluor 647 label and evaluated in the whole-cell treponeme assay reported by Bruno et al.37 That study reported under 24 h wall-clock time per target on its shared computational infrastructure; this figure refers to the module versions and hardware of that campaign and is not a runtime benchmark of the present pipeline version.

  Whole-cell fluorescence titrations in that assay yielded apparent KD values of 1.09, 2.57, and 0.91 μM for the candidates designed against Tpp17, Tpp47, and Tp0751, respectively.37 Three features bound what these numbers can support. The measurement is made on intact cells and therefore reports avidity and target accessibility in a cellular context rather than a 1:1 purified-complex constant. Binding was not confirmed against the purified intended targets, so engagement of the specific lipoprotein used for design is not established.

  The live-cell experiments demonstrate cell-associated binding of the designed sequences, but they do not by themselves assign the measured signal exclusively to the intended protein target or provide purified-protein 1:1 dissociation constants. Whole-cell apparent affinity can additionally reflect target accessibility and membrane presentation, local target density and rebinding, nonspecific surface interactions, and possible ligand depletion. Target copy number and surface density were not measured, so the magnitude or direction of any shift relative to a purified-protein KD cannot be determined from these experiments. The three cellular measurements were therefore analyzed separately from the purified-complex ValentiNA benchmark.

  The ValentiNA predictions reported in the original T. pallidum study preceded the global experimental post-calibration used in the present work. ### Prospective application to Tau441 detection in serum
  In a second prospective study, Xelari was applied to human Tau441 in a distinct biological and analytical setting.38 Because Tau441 is intrinsically disordered and lacks a single experimentally resolved full-length conformation, aptamer design used a full-length structural model integrating sequence and post-translational-modification information, 118 short-range XL-MS cross-links, and structural information from available Tau-antibody complexes. The model was generated with Boltz-2 and subjected to 10-ns molecular-dynamics relaxation before use as the Xelari input.

  The design request specified a region around Ala390 that was spatially separated in the structural model from the Thr217 region recognized by the companion antibody. The resulting computational complex placed the predicted aptamer-binding region around residues 380–400, but the binding epitope was not mapped independently by experiment. The DNA aptamer was synthesized with a 5′ primary amine and used as the capture reagent in a hybrid aptamer-antibody fluorescent lateral-flow assay.

  The cited study reports concentration-dependent signal for unphosphorylated Tau441 and for pT217-Tau441 spiked into pooled human serum across three independent serial dilutions, with pT217-Tau441 visible at 1 pg/mL in all three trials and visual detection of the unphosphorylated form varying between trials; a separate panel showed no visible signal for GDF-15, Aβ42, HE4, or human serum albumin.38 We do not derive a limit of detection from these titration images and because both recognition reagents contribute to the sandwich signal, neither the sensitivity nor the specificity of the assay can be attributed to the aptamer. This application therefore evidences functional deployment of an Xelari-designed sequence in an orthogonal, serum-based format rather than an aptamer-level affinity, or selectivity measurement.

  The Tau441 study supports functional use of the Xelari-designed aptamer as a capture reagent in this assay architecture, but it does not establish the precise aptamer-binding epitope or provide a direct aptamer-only 1:1 dissociation constant. The accepted study reports a calculated KD of 0.92 nM from the design workflow; in the absence of a direct aptamer-only affinity measurement, that numerical prediction is not treated here as experimentally validated, and the Tau441 data are not included in the quantitative ValentiNA affinity benchmark.

  Taken together, the two prospective studies comprise four Xelari-designed DNA aptamers evaluated in two distinct experimental settings: direct binding to intact treponeme cells and use of a Tau441 aptamer as a capture reagent in a serum-based sandwich assay. The evidence supports the narrower conclusion that Xelari-generated DNA sequences can retain experimental function after synthesis in more than one biological and assay context. It does not establish a general target-level or per-candidate success rate. Neither study reports the complete denominator of candidates ranked, synthesized, and experimentally tested; prospective RNA designs have not yet been evaluated and neither study constitutes fully independent third-party validation. Broader prospective testing on unrelated purified targets, with predefined candidate denominators and independent experimental replication, will be required to establish generalization and success rates.


## Discussion
  Aptamer discovery has traditionally begun with selection. Here, prospective evaluation began with structure. Three Xelari-designed DNA aptamers bound intact treponeme cells, while a fourth DNA aptamer subsequently functioned as a capture reagent for human Tau441 in a serum-based sandwich assay. Several intermediate operations were benchmarked separately against computational or experimental references. The central test is therefore not whether any one model reproduces its reference labels, but whether a sequence of structure-based decisions can yield experimentally testable candidates. The two prospective studies provide an initial demonstration that it can, across two different biological and assay settings. They have not yet established how often the approach will succeed across the target space.

  The prospective whole-cell data also set the scale of the present result. The three aptamers bound intact cells with apparent KD values of 0.91–2.57 μM. After application of the global ValentiNA calibration used in this study, the corresponding predictions differed from the measured apparent affinities by 1.06- to 1.18-fold. Measurements on live bacteria incorporate target accessibility and membrane presentation, local target density and rebinding and nonspecific surface interactions, none of which a purified 1:1 complex model represents; the correspondence in these three cases is therefore not a general error estimate for cellular binding. Just as importantly, micromolar affinity remains well below what successful SELEX campaigns reach after enrichment and optimization.4,8,12 These are first-generation candidates, not affinity-matured aptamers. The Tau441 study contributes a different form of prospective evidence: functional use of an Xelari-designed aptamer in a serum-based assay, without a direct aptamer-only affinity measurement.

  That distinction changes how Xelari should be positioned. Its current contribution is not to outperform SELEX on affinity, but to move target-specific experimentation later in the discovery process. Instead of beginning with an enrichment campaign, one can begin with a structural model, generate a ranked set of candidates and test those candidates directly. The shift may be useful when purified target production is difficult,9 when the relevant conformation is unstable outside its native context,58 or when a rapidly testable starting candidate is more valuable than immediate affinity maturation. Cell-SELEX and related approaches remain important alternatives when native presentation is essential.9 Structure-based design therefore complements experimental selection rather than replacing it.

  The modular architecture is useful for a second reason: it makes several potential failure modes separately inspectable. An unsuccessful design can arise from the target model, site selection, seed geometry, loss of the intended fold during scaffold assembly, complex relaxation, or final ranking. These causes are not independent, and errors can propagate or compensate across stages, so strong retrospective performance of individual modules cannot be read as end-to-end accuracy. The benefit is diagnostic rather than arithmetic: a failed candidate can be examined against specific hypotheses about where the design broke down, rather than treated only as a negative sequence-level outcome. The MoNA benchmark is instructive in this respect, because incomplete topology recovery after grafting identifies a concrete source of attrition before affinity scoring is even reached.

  Surrogate predictions inherit the limitations of their computational reference labels. Affine calibration can map ValentiNA scores onto an experimental scale but cannot restore physical contributions absent from the reference calculations. In the retained retrospective panel, modified records have larger absolute errors than canonical records (MAE 1.180 versus 0.683 kcal/mol), but differences in target composition and assay conditions prevent attribution of that difference to chemistry alone. The reported calibration and the template-aggregated analysis do not establish performance on aptamer or target families absent from calibration. Ranking association and absolute prediction error must therefore be assessed separately. Candidate ranking can remain useful where the absolute energy scale is not.

  Chemical modification provides a direct test of the value and the limits of explicit molecular representation. Across three comparison sets spanning C5 base substitutions, 2′-F/2′-deoxy sugar chemistry and a phosphorodithioate backbone modification, ValentiNA reproduced the experimental direction or ordering of the affinity change in every case, but not its magnitude and the deviations are not one-sided: predicted differences exceeded the measured ones in the modified thrombin-aptamer series and fell short of them in the 2′-F/2′-deoxy and phosphorodithioate comparisons. Together with the chemistry-stratified panel, this supports modification-aware ranking for the chemistries examined here and excludes quantitative ΔΔG prediction or chemistry-independent absolute calibration. The choice of modification sites was not evaluated and remains an experimental design problem.

  G-quadruplexes mark a boundary of the current workflow rather than an open performance question. The model can score deposited G4 complexes, but every G4 record in our panel is a thrombin aptamer, so topology is confounded with target identity and the stratified statistics cannot be read as a topology effect. The decisive limitation is generative: the current scaffold library and MoNA assembly do not produce G-quadruplex folds. Post-hoc scoring of G4 complexes therefore does not extend the de novo design claim to that class.

  Structure-first design also makes the target model part of the experimental hypothesis. Missing loops, alternative conformations, oligomeric state, membrane orientation, glycosylation, and other post-translational modifications can change which surfaces are physically accessible. Xelari can accept experimental or predicted structures, but the present work does not show that those inputs are interchangeable in practice. Automation therefore reduces repeated target-specific fitting and manual sequence engineering; it does not remove the need to judge whether the supplied structure represents the biologically relevant state. For difficult targets, that judgment may become the dominant source of uncertainty.

  Several extensions follow directly from the present failure modes. Broader scaffold coverage and improved preservation of topology after grafting should address structural attrition, whereas more rigorous conformational sampling and final-state free-energy calculations could improve discrimination among closely ranked candidates. The current generative scope also remains restricted to single-chain DNA and RNA aptamers and excludes double-stranded architectures and G-quadruplexes.

  The next decisive test is a prospective series broad enough to measure success rate as well as affinity: unrelated protein folds, different surface chemistries, soluble and membrane-associated targets, DNA and RNA designs and both purified-protein and cellular assays. Failures need to be counted alongside successes, because a design platform is defined not only by the affinity of its best examples but by the probability that a new target yields a usable binder. The present prospective evidence now spans four DNA designs and two experimental settings, but neither study reports the complete denominator of candidates ranked, synthesized, and tested, and neither study constitutes fully independent third-party validation. Prospective RNA designs have also not yet been evaluated. A larger predefined series with complete outcome reporting and independent experimental replication would therefore be required to establish general success rates and to determine whether iterative structure-based optimization can mature first-generation candidates toward high-affinity aptamers without returning to large-scale target-specific selection.

  Target-specific selection was not required to obtain three DNA aptamers that bound intact treponeme cells, and a fourth Xelari-designed DNA aptamer functioned as a Tau441 capture reagent in serum. These studies establish prospective experimental activity in two settings, but do not define the overall success rate of the approach or the extent to which first-generation designs can be affinity-matured. Those questions will require broader prospective testing across unrelated targets and systematic optimization of initial candidates. Structure-based design should therefore be viewed as a complement to experimental selection rather than a replacement for it.


## Materials and methods

### Data sources, training, and test datasets
  The computational part of the Xelari platform consists of five trainable models (INGA, InNA, AliNA, Edgar, and ValentiNA) and three algorithmic modules (Ehrlich, DiNA, and MoNA), which do not involve training on data. Training, validation, and test datasets were constructed individually for each platform model; their composition, sizes, and data acquisition protocols are provided in the corresponding subsections of this section (INGA, InNA, AliNA, Edgar, and ValentiNA). An external experimental set of aptamer-protein affinities for post-calibration of ValentiNA and the consolidated benchmarks was compiled from the open-access databases and an internal collection; the list of sources and numerical characteristics is provided in the supplemental information.

  Dataset partitioning is module-specific and is described with each model below. For InNA, the test set was defined by amino acid residue type, with lysine and glutamic acid held out entirely. For AliNA, training and validation data were partitioned using a family-aware procedure, and the 339-sequence PDB evaluation benchmark was excluded from model-weight training and from Rfam families represented in the training corpus; normalized Levenshtein distance was used to stratify sequence similarity within the evaluation set. For ValentiNA, homology partitioning was applied simultaneously to the protein and the aptamer, so that the coarse-grained training set is homologous only to the all-atom PMF training set and does not overlap the validation and test sets; experimental data enter only the test and calibration subsets.59 For Edgar, partitioning was random over structures and because sequence augmentation of the DNA subset preceded partitioning, augmented variants of one parent structure can appear in more than one subset, so that split is not homology-controlled for DNA. These schemes are deliberately matched to each task, but they do not amount to a single homology-independent test of the platform as a whole and we do not present them as one. For experimental affinity evaluation, independence should additionally be assessed at the aptamer-family and target-family level rather than by PDB identifier alone; the retrospective panel used here is separated by structure only and does not meet that stricter criterion.


### Preparation of the target protein structure

#### Automated structure completion with PDBFixer
  Missing atoms and small gaps (up to 8 residues) were addressed using PDBFixer (OpenMM project; http://github.com/openmm/pdbfixer, PDBFixer 1.9+ with mmCIF output support), which scanned backbone chains for sequence discontinuities, filled small gaps using standard amino acid templates with Dunbrack rotamer library side-chain geometries, inserted missing hydrogens, and repaired incomplete side chains. Processed structures were saved in mmCIF format to preserve full atom and residue metadata. For larger structural gaps exceeding 8 residues, including missing loops, secondary structure elements, or entire domains, Boltz-2 was employed (available at https://github.com/jwohlwend/boltz). Input preparation included MSA generation via ColabFold/MMseqs2, with template-based predictions run using 4 recycling steps, 200 diffusion steps, and 5 independent seeds; the highest-confidence output (selected by mean pLDDT, with >70 considered high confidence) was used to replace missing regions. Ligands, cofactors, and ions from the initial model were transferred to the reconstructed structure, excluding atomic overlaps; otherwise, they were subjected to a minimal shift to correct the overlap; standard crystallographic additives (polyethylene glycol, sulfate, glycerol, acetate, ethylene glycol, etc.) were removed based on a predefined list of Chemical Component IDs. The reconstructed structures were saved in mmCIF format.


#### Conformational relaxation using the INGA neural network model
  INGA is designed to sample conformations from the most populated MD-sampled cluster accessible from a given input structure. The model was trained on molecular dynamics trajectories generated for this study from three sources: antigen-structure simulations used for surface-analysis benchmarking (see materials and methods), relaxation trajectories of protein-DNA complexes from the ValentiNA training subset (see materials and methods), and relaxation trajectories of DNA structures generated during Edgar training (see supplemental information).

  To define the target state, we cluster the trajectory for each system separately and designate the most populated cluster as the most long-lived state. Prior to clustering, we apply reference alignment and dimensionality reduction. Reference alignment renders conformations invariant to rigid-body rotation and translation; the reference frame is the global medoid—the frame with minimum mean Cα-RMSD to all other frames. For each MD frame, only Cα coordinates are extracted (or P, C5′, and C3′ for nucleic acids) and projected with TICA, which captures the slowest conformational degrees of freedom.60 We run HDBSCAN clustering over the first 5 TICA components on the full set of MD frames. It groups high-density regions into clusters and treats sparse frames as noise.61 The most populated cluster is designated as the target (most populated MD-sampled) state.

  Each frame in the dataset serves as an input. For each input frame, the 20 nearest target-state training frames in TICA space are identified, and one is selected at random as the paired target frame. Training is performed for 250 epochs with 50 Flow Matching steps, σsource = 0.1, and Adam optimizer.

  To assess its viability, we run INGA on the Chignolin peptide model system on trajectories from the benchmark.43 The target state selection was performed in the same way as for training systems. The dataset was split into three validation sets.1)Frames from the target (most populated MD-sampled) cluster—tests model behavior when the input is already near the target state.2)Frames from all non-target clusters—tests the ability to transition from other long-lived attractors to the target.3)25% of HDBSCAN noise frames—high-energy, short-lived transition states that probe generalization to sparsely sampled conformational regions.

  From each validation set, 250 random frames were sampled for the test. During evaluation, the model was run five times on the same input. The output with the fewest bond-length violations is selected and further refined with GFN-FF.

  Following INGA and GFN-FF refinement, the prepared target undergoes an explicit-solvent GROMACS relaxation before surface analysis. The temperature and ionic composition specified by the user are applied at this stage as molecular-dynamics simulation conditions. The resulting relaxed conformation is used as the structural input for the downstream surface-analysis and aptamer-design stages.


#### Target surface analysis and binding site selection with Ehrlich
  Ehrlich is an algorithmic module for identifying surface regions of a target protein that have low similarity to surfaces represented in a reference protein database; no data training is performed. The full specification of parameters and formulas is provided in the supplemental information “Construction of the molecular surface” section.

  The solvent-excluded surface (SES) is constructed using an implementation equivalent to MSMS with a solvent probe radius of 1.4 Å and a triangulation density of approximately 3 vertices Å−2.62 Each surface vertex is associated with its three-dimensional coordinates, local surface normal, and nearest heavy protein atom. The resulting triangulated surface is segmented into overlapping local patches centered on surface-exposed anchor atoms. The default geodesic patch radius is 8 Å. Patch area and vertex count are retained during preprocessing and are used to reject degenerate surface regions.

  Each patch is described independently by geometric and compositional features. Surface geometry is encoded using a Point Feature Histogram (PFH)63 constructed from relative positions and orientations of pairs of surface vertices. Three angular features are discretized into five bins each, giving a 125-dimensional PFH descriptor. An aggregated 180-dimensional Viewpoint Feature Histogram (VFH) is additionally stored for efficient indexing operations.

  The compositional representation is a 20-dimensional vector over the canonical amino acid types. Residues contribute to the corresponding component according to an exponential distance kernel centered on the patch anchor, with λ = 1.0 throughout this study. The resulting vector is normalized before comparison.

  Geometric divergence between patches is calculated from their PFH representations using a symmetrized χ2 comparison. Compositional divergence is evaluated either by cosine comparison or by an optimal-transport formulation in which the cost matrix is derived from BLOSUM62 substitution scores. Geometric and compositional components are normalized to comparable numerical ranges and combined with equal weights (α = β = 1). The resulting pairwise measure is transformed to the bounded similarity score Tij∈[0, 1] used for surface comparison. Complete definitions are given in the supplemental information, Equations S8–S16.

  For selection of target-specific regions, each target patch is compared with a precomputed reference surface library. Reference structures are obtained from AlphaFold DB v4,15,64 with an organism-specific subset used where applicable. The geometric and compositional match of a target patch to the reference library are combined into the operational score F(p) defined in Equation 1. Candidate patches are retained when F(p) ≤ τ. The predefined thresholds are τ = 0.8 for Low, 0.6 for Medium, 0.4 for High, 0.2 for Very High specificity. If the user specifies an anchor point, priority is given to patches containing it; if no patch satisfies the requested threshold, τ is relaxed in increments of 0.1 until a candidate region is retained.

  For the internal surface-discrimination benchmark, 14 non-homologous antigens were selected from experimentally determined antigen-antibody structures in the PDB (Table S2). Antigen chains were extracted, and missing structural regions were reconstructed where necessary using the target-preparation procedure described earlier. Each antigen was subjected to a 300 ns molecular-dynamics simulation in GROMACS 2024.1 using Amber ff19SB for proteins,65 along with the OPC3 water model and the corresponding Li-Merz ion parameters.66,67 Trajectories were clustered using the Gromos procedure with a 0.25 nm cutoff to obtain representative protein conformations for subsequent surface analysis.

  For the matrix analysis, local surface patches generated by Ehrlich were grouped within each antigen in PFH descriptor space into 4–5 representative surface clusters. Each cluster was represented by its medoid patch, giving 68 medoid patches across the 14 antigens. Ehrlich similarity was calculated for every unordered pair of medoid patches, producing the symmetric 68 × 68 matrix shown in Figure 3A. Excluding self-comparisons, the matrix contained 132 within-antigen and 2,146 between-antigen pairwise comparisons. These two groups were used for the descriptive analysis reported in the results.

  For external validation, Ehrlich was applied to the 729 protein-interface pairs derived from the interface dataset of Budowski-Tal et al.,44 comprising 191 PDB structures and 65 Pfam families. The analysis compared Ehrlich similarities for interfaces associated with the same Pfam-family groups with those for interfaces from different families. Pfam labels were used exclusively to define the reference classes for evaluation and were not supplied to the Ehrlich scoring algorithm.

  All surface construction, descriptor calculation, reference-database comparison, and threshold filtering steps are executed automatically once the target structure, specificity level, and optional anchor region have been supplied.


#### Construction of seed fragments on the target surface with DiNA
  DiNA is an algorithmic module that constructs short oligonucleotide seed fragments on a selected region of the target-protein surface. The module takes as input the protein structure after reconstruction and relaxation and a set of eligible surface patches identified by the Ehrlich. The output is a population of 4-6-nt seed structures with three-dimensional coordinates and nucleotide-protein interaction-energy estimates obtained with InNA. The DiNA pipeline comprises five stages.


#### Surface discretization
  The protein surface is represented as a set of van der Waals spheres carrying quasi-uniform surface point clouds with outward radial normals. Points occluded by neighboring atomic spheres are removed and the surviving surface points form a graph used for subsequent site and trajectory searches. The full construction and sampling parameters are given in the supplemental information.


#### Site ranking
  Candidate regions of the surface graph are ranked by the summed fixed weight of the amino acid residues represented in the region:(Equation 2)R(Ω)=∑a∈A(Ω)wawhere Ω is a candidate set of surface-graph vertices, A(Ω) is the set of amino acids contributing at least one vertex to that region, and wa is the fixed weight assigned to residue type a. The weights are chemistry-motivated heuristic parameters and are not learned from data. Aromatic residues were up-weighted to favor surface environments compatible with nucleobase stacking, whereas the contribution of charged residues was reduced to prevent electrostatic attraction from dominating site ranking; residue types not assigned specific chemistry-based adjustments retained the baseline weight. The resulting weight vector was specified manually rather than optimized against the DCC localization benchmark and is held fixed across all targets, with no target-specific adjustment during prediction (Table S3).


#### Trajectory search
  Within a selected surface region, candidate paths for seed construction are generated by a particle-based heuristic search. Particle motion combines terms that discourage repeated sampling of previously visited locations, favor trajectories toward high-ranking unvisited residues, and suppress immediate backtracking, while constraining motion to the local surface tangent plane. Under the default configuration, 100 particles are generated from each of four emitters over ten epochs, yielding 4,000 candidate trajectories per selected site before geometric filtering (Table S4).


#### Seed-chain assembly
  Retained trajectories are converted into nucleotide backbones using a library of implemented natural or supported modified sugar-phosphate building blocks. Backbone geometries are refined by conformational sampling within geometrical ranges derived from crystallographic nucleotide structures, while sterically incompatible conformations within the strand or between the strand and protein are rejected. Spatial clash queries are accelerated using a voxel index.


#### Position-dependent nucleotide selection
  Once a valid seed backbone has been obtained, nucleotide identity is selected position by position using InNA interaction-energy scores calculated against amino acid residues within 15 Å. Sterically incompatible configurations are removed before InNA evaluation, and permitted base orientations are sampled to identify the lowest-scoring configuration among the evaluated states.


#### Validation using the DCC metric
  The ability of the DiNA module to localize the binding site on the target protein was evaluated using the DCC criterion established for ligand-binding-site prediction benchmarks.45 In its original form, DCC is measured from the predicted pocket center to the nearest ligand atom; here, DCC (distance from center to center) is the Euclidean distance between the centroid of the DiNA-predicted seed population and the centroid of an operationally defined protein-aptamer contact zone in the reference co-crystal.

  For each reference complex, the experimental contact zone Aexp comprises solvent-accessible protein N/O atoms lying within 3.5 Å of at least one aptamer N/O atom. Solvent accessibility is evaluated by spherical discretization with a probe radius Rprobe = 1.4 Å, Npts = 64 test points per atom, and tabulated van der Waals radii; a protein atom is assigned to the surface set if at least one test point remains unoccluded (supplemental information, Equations S27–S32). The 3.5 Å criterion follows the short-range polar-contact convention used in standard hydrogen-bond analysis and in interface-contact analysis.68,69 The reference centroid cexp is the unweighted mean of the coordinates in Aexp, and the predicted centroid cpred is the unweighted mean over the aggregate heavy-atom cloud of the DiNA seed population assigned to the predicted region. DCC is then(Equation 3)DCC=‖cexp−cpred‖2

  The evaluation set comprises 38 protein-aptamer co-crystal structures across 19 distinct protein targets (12 RNA-associated and 7 DNA-associated targets) with experimentally observed binding sites. During prediction, aptamer coordinates and the reference contact site were withheld; only the prepared protein structure was supplied to DiNA, with no anchor position specified. The deposited aptamer coordinates were used only after prediction to construct the reference contact zone for evaluation.

  Eleven targets are represented by a single co-crystal structure. Eight targets have multiple reference complexes, some corresponding to different aptamer-binding regions on the same protein. Results are therefore reported both per structure, with all 38 complexes evaluated independently and per target. For the target-level analysis, the minimum DCC over the available reference complexes of a target is reported; this measures whether the DiNA prediction coincides with at least one experimentally observed aptamer-binding region for that protein. The identity of the structures grouped under each target and the per-structure DCC values are provided with the deposited benchmark data.

  Because DiNA receives only protein coordinates during this evaluation, the topology of the reference aptamer is not an input to site localization. Consequently, a reference complex containing a G-quadruplex aptamer can be used to evaluate localization of the corresponding protein surface site without implying that the current Xelari scaffold-generation procedure can generate G-quadruplex aptamers.


### InNA pairwise interaction-energy model

#### Model and reference-energy generation
  InNA is a neural-network model for predicting the interaction energy between a single nucleotide and a single amino acid residue in a fixed three-dimensional geometry. Each atom is represented by its atomic number, partial charge, and molecular identity (nucleotide or amino acid fragment), while pairwise interatomic distances provide the geometric input to distance-modulated transformer layers. The model predicts an atomic energy contribution from each final atom representation and sums these contributions to obtain the pair interaction energy.

  After the interaction blocks, an atomic energy contribution εa is predicted from the final embedding of each atom using a multilayer perceptron. The total pair interaction energy is obtained by summing the atomic contributions:(Equation 4)ΔEpair=∑aεa

  This atom-wise decomposition follows the extensive-energy formulation commonly used in neural-network potentials.33,70,71,72 Detailed architectural specifications are provided in the supplemental information.

  Reference interaction energies are defined as:(Equation 5)ΔEint=Etot(AA,NT)−Etot(AA)−Etot(NT)where AA denotes an amino acid residue and NT denotes a nucleotide. Energies were calculated using r2SCAN-3c in ORCA 5.0.1 under vacuum conditions.46,47 Fragment termini were capped to approximate continuation of the peptide and nucleic-acid backbones. The constituent fragments were individually optimized with GFN2-xTB before pair generation48; relative orientations and separations were then sampled systematically, and the assembled pair geometries were evaluated without further pair optimization. The complete capping and geometry-generation protocol is provided in the supplemental information.


#### Training and evaluation
  The complete dataset contained 13.09 million nucleotide-amino-acid pair geometries and was divided into training (9.163 million), validation (1.309 million), and test (2.618 million) sets. To evaluate transfer across amino acid identity, lysine and glutamic acid, together with the corresponding modified forms represented in the dataset, were excluded from the training and validation partitions and reserved for testing. Model optimization used mean-squared error between InNA predictions and r2SCAN-3c reference energies. The model was trained in a single stage using mean-squared error between predicted and r2SCAN-3c reference interaction energies as the objective function and AdamW for optimization. Detailed training hyperparameters are provided in the supplemental information.

  Evaluation in Figure 5 used a curated panel of 483,735 geometries spanning seven amino-acid-nucleotide combinations (69,105 geometries per combination). LYS and GLU formed the amino-acid-identity hold-out subsets, whereas the other five amino acid identities were represented during training. Prediction accuracy was quantified by R2, RMSE, and MAE relative to r2SCAN-3c reference energies. RMSE was also calculated for negative-energy pair geometries (Eref < 0) and for the most favorable 5% of the complete evaluation panel. No post hoc calibration or re-fitting was applied to the predictions used for these analyses; full evaluation details are provided in the supplemental information.


#### Nucleic acid secondary structure prediction with AliNA
  AliNA uses the U-Net/Transformer secondary-structure prediction architecture described previously in a lighter configuration.49 In brief, local nucleotide-context features are mapped to a two-dimensional pairwise representation and processed by a convolutional encoder-decoder with a Transformer bottleneck. The model returns a base-pair probability matrix, which is converted to a single secondary structure by probability filtering followed by iterative selection of non-conflicting base pairs. The implementation accepts sequences up to 256 nt. At inference, AliNA does not require nearest-neighbor thermodynamic parameters as explicit inputs. Full architectural details are provided by Nasaev et al.49 and summarized in the supplemental information.


#### Training and evaluation
  For the present study, all model weights were retrained de novo using a two-stage training procedure. Pre-training was performed on 30,856 RNA sequences with experimentally derived nucleotide-reactivity profiles from curated NGS and Ribonanza datasets.73 The resulting model was subsequently fine-tuned on a higher-confidence set of 3,988 RNA sequences supported by additional co-evolutionary evidence. Structural labels for fine-tuning were generated using an annotation workflow that integrates experimental nucleotide-reactivity profiles with predictions from multiple RNA secondary-structure methods and co-evolutionary information. A detailed description of the pre-training and fine-tuning datasets construction and annotation procedure is provided in the supplementary methods. To reduce leakage from related RNAs sharing similar sequences and folds, the pre-training and fine-tuning datasets were partitioned using the family-aware procedure described below rather than at random. The 339 PDB sequences with experimentally determined structures were reserved for evaluation and excluded from AliNA model-weight training. For sequence-similarity analysis, the evaluation set was divided into a higher-similarity stratum (306 sequences) and a lower-similarity stratum (33 sequences) relative to the training corpus using normalized Levenshtein distance.

  To control for family-level overlap, the updated AliNA training corpus was partitioned using the same family-aware procedure as in the original AliNA study.49 RNA-family assignments were obtained with Infernal against Rfam using the curated family gathering thresholds. Sequences assigned to an Rfam family represented in the PDB evaluation set were excluded from the corresponding training and validation partitions. Thus, the PDB benchmark used for evaluation was disjoint from the training data at the assigned Rfam-family level. Exact sequence matches were additionally removed, and sequence-distance sensitivity was assessed using normalized Levenshtein distance.


#### Downstream structural validation
  Two distinct panels were used to evaluate AliNA, and they should not be conflated. Secondary-structure accuracy was evaluated on 339 experimentally resolved PDB RNA structures, split by sequence homology to the training data into 306 structures with close homologs and 33 with only distant homology; reference base pairs were extracted directly from the experimental coordinates using naskit, and performance was evaluated separately for the two strata. Compatibility with downstream three-dimensional reconstruction was evaluated on a separate and smaller panel of 13 non-G-quadruplex RNA aptamer structures. For each of these, trRosettaRNA2 was run with the AliNA-predicted secondary structure supplied as an auxiliary structural input, and the resulting model was compared with the corresponding experimental structure by RMSD and TM-score computed with US-align in RNA mode; the effect of the AliNA-derived input was quantified against an otherwise identical reconstruction performed without it. Because the evaluated aptamers are synthetic sequences rather than members of natural RNA families, informative evolutionary homologs were not expected: trRosettaRNA2 was run with its default sequence-search procedure, and no externally generated multiple sequence alignment was supplied by the Xelari workflow.

  The DNA branch was evaluated on 58 experimentally resolved ssDNA structures from the PDB. Each sequence was processed by AliNA for secondary-structure prediction and reconstructed with trRosettaRNA2 in RNA representation; the resulting models were converted to DNA with naskit and refined by molecular dynamics with DNA parameters to relax the RNA-derived geometry, including sugar geometry and helical parameters. Secondary-structure accuracy was scored by F1 against reference base pairs extracted from the experimental DNA coordinates. Refined models were compared with the experimental structures by RMSD and lDDT computed over heavy atoms placed in one-to-one correspondence between model and reference after superposition, so that unpaired and non-corresponding atoms do not enter either metric; the reported values are consequently not whole-molecule errors. The reconstruction stage and the RNA-to-DNA conversion stage were recorded in separate output files; Table S7 reports the post-refinement values, and the per-stage records are provided in the deposited data.


#### Edgar folding-free-energy assessment
  Edgar is a graph neural network used in Xelari as a surrogate for the SMD/umbrella-sampling calculations used to generate its folding-energy reference labels. The model takes a nucleotide sequence and its secondary structure in dot-bracket notation as input and returns a predicted folding-energy (kcal/mol) with a prediction-uncertainty estimate; three-dimensional coordinates are not required at inference. The implementation is available at https://github.com/XELARI-CORP/Edgar.

  Each nucleic acid is represented as a graph in which nucleotides are nodes and covalent backbone connections and secondary-structure base pairs form distinct edge classes. Backbone-local information is processed separately from base-pair connectivity, after which a Transformer encoder integrates long-range sequence context. The final readout returns the folding-energy and heteroscedastic-uncertainty estimates. Training uses a Gaussian negative-log-likelihood objective based on the predicted mean and variance. Full architectural specifications and optimization parameters are provided in the supplemental information.


#### Training data and evaluation
  The Edgar reference dataset comprised 26,447 single-stranded RNA and DNA structures. RNA secondary structures were collected from public structure databases after duplicate removal and filtering of non-standard bases; the DNA subset was derived from experimentally determined PDB structures and expanded by sequence augmentation. Three-dimensional structures were generated with trRosettaRNA2,50 with DNA models converted from the initial RNA representation using naskit (https://github.com/XELARI-CORP/naskit) before molecular simulation. Dataset composition and augmentation procedures are detailed in the supplemental information.

  Reference folding-energy labels were generated by steered molecular dynamics followed by umbrella sampling, using end-to-end distance as the reaction coordinate. Potential-of-mean-force profiles were reconstructed by WHAM, and the PMF-derived unfolding free energy was sign-inverted to obtain the folding-energy target used for supervised training. The reference calculations used a single fixed thermodynamic condition; temperature and ionic composition are not independent Edgar inference inputs in the current implementation. Complete simulation and convergence parameters are provided in the supplemental information.

  The reference dataset was divided into training, validation and held-out test subsets in a 70:10:20 ratio. Internal performance was assessed against the simulation-derived reference labels using R2 and RMSE. Transfer to experimental measurements was evaluated separately on 56 RNA and DNA sequences of up to 75 nt with literature folding free energies without recalibration74,75,76,77,78,79; agreement was quantified using Pearson and Spearman correlations and linear regression.


#### Scaffold library construction
  The module constructs a structurally diverse library of short nucleic acid scaffolds (15–45 nt in length) for subsequent assembly into full-length aptamers. The module is algorithmic; no data training is performed. Library construction comprises six stages: generation of pseudorandom sequences, secondary-structure prediction and preliminary filtering, selection of a structurally diverse subset, three-dimensional reconstruction, protein-surface docking, followed by molecular-dynamics relaxation, and final filtering. Detailed parameters for all stages are provided in the supplemental information.

  Pseudorandom nucleotide sequences are generated at approximately 60% GC content. For each sequence, the AliNA predicts the secondary structure in dot-bracket notation. If multiple sequences produce the same secondary structure, the variant with the more favorable Edgar-predicted folding energy is retained. Candidate structures are filtered for repetitive sequence motifs, supported pseudoknot depth, terminal elements, internal unpaired-region length, and secondary-structure connectivity (supplemental information).

  Structures are grouped by length and selected within each length group using greedy maximally distant addition. Implementation details are provided in the supplemental information.

  Each selected scaffold is reconstructed with trRosettaRNA2 using the AliNA-predicted secondary structure as an auxiliary structural input. Reconstruction quality is evaluated by recovering base pairs from the resulting coordinates with naskit and comparing them with the target secondary structure; models are retained at F-score ≥0.8. For DNA scaffolds, the RNA coordinates generated by trRosettaRNA2 are converted to DNA with naskit by replacing ribose with deoxyribose and uracil with thymine while initially preserving the reconstructed sugar geometry. RNA-to-DNA conversion is restricted to this three-dimensional reconstruction step: secondary-structure prediction is performed directly on ssDNA. Edgar operates on sequence and secondary-structure inputs, whereas geometry-dependent downstream evaluation is performed on DNA coordinates after conversion and relaxation.

  Each scaffold is docked to protein receptors from the Methanocaldococcus jannaschii proteome using MEGADOCK4.80 Docking is used only to provide protein-associated starting geometries for molecular-dynamics relaxation; the receptor proteins are not treated as binding targets. The resulting complexes are relaxed in GROMACS using the nucleic-acid- and protein-specific force fields described in the supplemental information. Representative conformations are obtained by trajectory clustering.

  Following relaxation, the sequence- and topology-level criteria are reapplied, and redundant structures with identical nucleotide sequences are removed according to heavy-atom RMSD. DNA scaffolds are additionally classified according to A-/B-like sugar-backbone geometry, and only structures meeting the predefined B-form criterion are retained in the final DNA library.

  To assess the conformational consequence of RNA-to-DNA conversion, RNA and DNA scaffold structures are analyzed after molecular-dynamics relaxation and before application of the final DNA B-form acceptance filter. Sugar pseudorotation phase P, backbone torsion δ, glycosidic torsion χ, sugar-pucker state, and BI/BII backbone state are calculated with DSSR. Each nucleotide contributes an A- or B-form vote according to the scaffold classifier, and a chain is assigned to a conformational class when more than 70% of its nucleotide assignments support that state; chains not reaching the threshold for either class are designated mixed. Conformational classes and descriptor distributions are evaluated separately for RNA and DNA scaffolds. The classifier implementation and final-filter criteria are specified in the supplemental information.


#### Assembly of the full-length aptamers from the scaffold library
  The MoNA module transfers seed fragments selected by DiNA into full-length scaffolds from the topology library, generating candidate aptamer sequences. The module is algorithmic and requires no model training.

  The input consists of a seed structure positioned on the protein target and a library of PDB scaffolds. Each nucleotide is represented by three averaged reference points on the sugar-phosphate backbone: the junctions with the preceding and following residues and the central anchor on the C4–C5′ bond. This base-independent triad representation is used to identify scaffold positions geometrically compatible with replacement by seed residues and can also be applied to supported modified nucleotides (supplemental information).

  For each scaffold-seed triad pair, the optimal rigid transformation is calculated using the Kabsch method. Candidate correspondences are selected when the transformed triad centroids lie within rmatch = 3.0 Å and are refined by mutual nearest neighbors (MNN). When three or more correspondences are available, the transformation is recalculated using the expanded point set to improve alignment stability (supplemental information).

  Each candidate pose is then subjected sequentially to steric and topological filtering. The steric filter evaluates overlap between heavy scaffold and protein atoms using scaled van der Waals radii (α = 0.8) and an allowed clash fraction of τ = 0.10. Poses surviving this step are evaluated for mechanical entanglement of the scaffold backbone with protein loops using the generalized Gauss linking number (γGLN = 0.8). Additional filtering parameters are given in Table S9.

  For each retained pose, scaffold residues at the matched positions are replaced by the corresponding seed residues. The graft retains the seed residue geometry, including the glycosidic torsion χ and backbone torsion δ, to preserve the local DiNA-derived contact geometry as closely as possible, whereas the remainder of the scaffold provides the surrounding fold context. The final pose is selected by maximizing the number of matched neighbors while minimizing the RMSD between corresponding triads.

  Because grafting alters the sequence within the seed region, the assembled sequence is re-evaluated with AliNA, and its predicted secondary structure is compared with the parent scaffold by base-pair F1. If F1 is below 0.8, compensatory mutagenesis iteratively substitutes complementary positions outside the seed region to improve topology recovery while minimizing changes in GC composition. The search terminates when F1 reaches 0.8 or after 1,000 iterations.

  The resulting candidates are ranked by the summed InNA interaction-energy scores of the grafted residues and passed to INGA for complex relaxation and ValentiNA for affinity prediction. Detailed mathematical definitions of the alignment and filtering procedures are provided in the supplemental information.

  The archived MoNA topology-recovery benchmark comprises 500 assembled RNA candidates of 20–25 nt. The primary topology analysis uses sequence and secondary-structure records obtained immediately after seed grafting and before compensatory mutagenesis. For each candidate, the AliNA-predicted secondary structure was compared with the parent scaffold topology. Candidates with F1 < 0.8 were subsequently subjected to the standard compensatory-mutagenesis procedure, which continued until F1 ≥ 0.8 or the maximum of 1,000 iterations was reached. The fraction meeting the F1 ≥ 0.8 criterion was recorded before and after compensatory mutagenesis. Base-pair precision (P) is defined as the fraction of predicted base pairs present in the parent topology, recall (R) as the fraction of parent-topology base pairs recovered in the prediction, and F1 as their harmonic mean:(Equation 6)F1=2PR(P+R)

  F1 ≥ 0.8 corresponds to the operational topology-recovery target used during assembly, whereas F1 = 1.0 denotes exact recovery of the parent base-pair set. Only post-assembly candidates are represented in this benchmark; scaffold-seed proposals that fail alignment or geometric filtering are not part of the analysis. The benchmark contains RNA assemblies only.

  For the same candidates, folding-energy consistency is assessed by comparing the Edgar-predicted energy of the assembled topology with the sequence MFE reference associated with the same benchmark record. The signed energy offset is defined as the Edgar energy minus the MFE reference and the corresponding absolute offset is used for threshold-based comparisons. The fraction of candidates with an absolute offset of no more than 1 kcal/mol is calculated. Because the two quantities originate from different energy models, this analysis is treated as a cross-model consistency comparison rather than as an independent estimate of physical folding free energy.

  Associations between sequence length and pre-mutagenesis F1 and between pre-mutagenesis F1 and the signed energy offset were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation. Absolute energy offsets for candidates with pre-mutagenesis F1 ≥ 0.8 and pre-mutagenesis F1 < 0.8 were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Compensatory mutagenesis was subsequently applied to the 292 candidates with pre-mutagenesis F1 < 0.8, all of which reached the predefined F1 ≥ 0.8 topology-recovery target.


#### Prediction of the complex binding free energy with ValentiNA
  ValentiNA returns a scalar score for an aptamer-protein complex; after affine post-calibration, this score is mapped to the experimental binding-free-energy scale. Architectural details are given in the supplemental information. For retrospective association analyses, the ValentiNA affinity score is defined as S = -ΔGpred, so larger scores denote stronger predicted binding.

  Training used two homology-partitioned stages: 10,278 coarse-grained PMF-derived labels followed by 1,690 all-atom PMF-derived labels. Simulation protocols, force fields, reaction coordinates and convergence criteria are provided in the supplemental information.

  The reported predictions were mapped onto an experimental energy scale using the 17 literature calibration records in Table S11. The reported unweighted least-squares fit gives α = 0.0234 ± 0.0047 (kcal/mol)/(kJ/mol) and β = −9.43 ± 0.30 kcal/mol, with linear-fit R2 = 0.63 and residual RMSE = 0.77 kcal/mol. The panel revision in this manuscript does not refit these coefficients or regenerate model predictions: it reconciles the displayed calibrated outputs with the retained evaluation records. Separation by PDB identifier does not establish independence, since related aptamer-target systems can occur in both calibration and evaluation.


#### Validation protocol
  The quantitative retrospective analysis uses 32 records. The three whole-cell apparent affinities are excluded from all purified-assay statistics. Record-level results describe the retained observations, while a separate sensitivity analysis takes the median of the displayed reference energies and the median of the displayed predictions within each structural-template group. The latter produces 27 groups and does not establish statistical independence.

  Reference energies in Table S12 are calculated as ΔG° = RT ln(KD/C°), with KD in mol/L, C° = 1 mol/L and R = 0.001987 kcal mol−1 K−1, at the temperature listed for each record. Summary statistics use the displayed two-decimal reference and predicted energies, making the calculations reproducible directly from the table. Pearson r and Spearman ρ measure association; linear-fit equals squared Pearson r for an ordinary least-squares fit with an intercept. MAE, RMSE, and mean signed deviation are computed from prediction minus reference. For comparison, predictive R2 relative to the identity line is 0.561 at record level and 0.573 after template aggregation; these values are distinct from the linear-fit R2 values in Table S13. No inferential significance or independent-system sample size is claimed.

  Chemistry strata follow the modification labels of the retained Table S12 records. The G4 group includes both assay records for 4DII, records for PDB: 1HUT, 4DIH, 6GN7, 7NTU, 6EO7, 6EO6, and the W4+W12 model derived from 6EO6. For template aggregation, the repeated records sharing 4DII and those sharing PDB: 4NI7 were each collapsed into a single template group; W4+W12 is grouped with 6EO6, and Apt8-1 and Apt8-2 are grouped with PDB: 3AGV. All other retained records form their own template groups. Grouping modified derivatives by template is a structural-input sensitivity analysis, not an assertion that their molecular identities or affinities are identical.


#### Chemical-modification workflow and retrospective evaluation
  For chemistries represented by the structural workflow, modifications are introduced before final complex relaxation and affinity scoring. Sugar substitutions such as 2′-F, 2′-O-Me, and mixed ribose/deoxyribose positions, together with supported base modifications, are represented explicitly when the corresponding molecular building blocks are available. Modified residues undergo the same geometric and steric-compatibility checks as canonical residues before downstream relaxation. Terminal labels and other design options handled outside the modeled complex are not included in the quantitative affinity claims reported here.

  Chemistry-dependent performance was evaluated within the same 32-record purified-complex benchmark used for the principal experimental analysis. Records were classified according to the chemistry explicitly represented in the corresponding complex or modification model, yielding 16 chemically modified records and 16 canonical DNA/RNA records. No target-specific retraining, chemistry-specific fine-tuning or separate modification-specific affine calibration was performed.

  Modification-specific analyses were separated by the type of chemical change. The thrombin TBA series comprised a dedicated native-TBA BLI reference together with TBA-T4W, TBA-T4K, and W4+W12, all based on the same 15-nt G-quadruplex scaffold and exosite-I target. The 2.8-nM native-TBA value belongs to this dedicated BLI series and is distinct from the 4DII benchmark MST measurement; the ValentiNA value used for the native series reference is the score of the 4DII structural scaffold. Native TBA is also the molecular system represented by calibration entry PDB: 1HAO. Consequently, the TBA series is used to assess directional and rank-order modification effects, not as an independent estimate of calibration magnitude. TBA-T4W and TBA-T4K provide the most direct pairwise comparison within this series, and the W4+W12 complex was modeled from 6EO6.

  The 3AGV matched series was restricted to Apt8, Apt8-1, and Apt8-2, which share the same 23-nt base sequence and human IgG1 Fc target and differ in their 2′-F/2′-deoxy substitution pattern. Apt8-1 and Apt8-2 were modeled from the 3AGV structural template. The separate 2.8-nM 3AGV validation record corresponds to a different aptamer sequence and was not included in this within-series comparison.

  The 3DD2/5DO4 pair was treated as a lineage-level backbone-modification comparison rather than an isolated single-substitution test. AF113-18 is a phosphorodithioate variant of the native AF113-1/Toggle-25t anti-thrombin scaffold; the deposited 5DO4 construct additionally carries 2′-methylselenyl-uridine at two positions and a different 2′-F substitution pattern, so it is not an isolated PS2 substitution. For the energetic comparison, we used the paired BLI measurements reported in the 2016 study at matched temperature, in preference to the filter-binding value listed for the Toggle-25t deposit in Table S12, because the paired values share an assay and a temperature. The structural input for the native scaffold is nevertheless the 3DD2 deposit, so structure and affinity derive from related but not identical constructs.
