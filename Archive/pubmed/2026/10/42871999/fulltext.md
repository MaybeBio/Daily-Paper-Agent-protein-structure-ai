# Lab to codes: multimodel advanced AI-driven pipelines to drive antimicrobial peptide design for the post-antibiotic era


## Abstract
  The rapid emergence of global antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has outpaced the development of new antimicrobial agents, necessitating transformative approaches such as AI-driven in-silico drug discovery. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are promising alternatives due to their broad-spectrum bactericidal activity and limited susceptibility to resistance. However, the transition from bench to bedside remains constrained by certain challenges, such as instability, potency, and cytotoxicity. Here, well-integrated, multi-model AI-driven pipeline strategies for the mining and discovery of novel AMPs are designed to address these concerns through simultaneous multi-purpose optimization. The proposed architectural frameworks combine discriminative AMP classifiers, quantitative potency, and cytotoxicity screening filters to prioritize AMPs with good efficacy and safety profiles. To ensure novelty, the pipeline integrates multi-layer sequential and genomic screening by adopting alignment and profile-based approaches. Structural refinements are achieved through advanced molecular docking (MD) and protein folding approaches, providing mechanistic insights regarding peptide–target interactions. In parallel, enzymatic susceptibility and stability prediction models are incorporated to optimize AMP pharmacokinetic potentials. Notably, the multi-objective pipeline operates within iterative optimization loops; discriminative and generative AI models and sequential redesign strategies refine candidates based on multi-purpose closed feedback loops across stability, novelty, efficacy, and toxicity outcomes. These systematic and integrated approaches overcome key bottlenecks associated with traditional linear drug discovery, potentially reducing late-stage attrition and accelerating the transformation from in-silico predictions to wet-lab validation. Collectively, this review provides reproducible and generalizable blueprints for next-generation antimicrobial agents, demonstrating the computational potentials of AI-driven, multi-model architectural frameworks to tackle the global AMR crisis and favour precision design of AMPs with better therapeutic indices.


## Introduction
  In the 21st century, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has evolved as one of the most serious global health concerns, undermining decades of progress in modern medicine (Selvaraj et al., 2025; Jesudason, 2026). Recent global AMR trends have indicated that millions of deaths are directly attributable to bacterial AMR annually and continue to rise across both low-and high-income countries (Selvaraj et al., 2025; Jesudason, 2026). The World Health Organization (WHO) highlights that these trends are increasing exponentially across critical priority pathogens, thereby compromising the effectiveness of last-resort antimicrobial therapies and jeopardizing clinical practices such as chemotherapy and regular surgical and transplantation procedures (Selvaraj et al., 2025; Jesudason, 2026). Without transformative interventional approaches, AMR is going to impose serious health and economic burdens, potentially exceeding morbidity, and mortality outcomes within the coming decades (Sadeeq et al., 2025).

  Despite these escalating threats, traditional antibiotic development has remained critically fragile (Palmo et al., 2026). At present, few antimicrobial candidates are in clinical development, and few of these use novel mechanisms of action (Palmo et al., 2026) (Figure 1 and 2). Most current antibiotic discovery paradigms are dependent on incremental structural modifications, vigorous empirical screening, and testing. These often fail to explore the vast chemical and biological spaces that may be open for new development, which are essential to identify next-generation candidates due to high costs (Panjla et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025) (Figure 2). Consequently, there is an urgent need to transition from traditional discovery. This is where integrative and data-oriented approaches can help to accelerate drug innovation strategies (Panjla et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025).

  Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a transformative paradigm for antibiotic discovery, enabling the systematic interrogation of available large-scale chemical and biological datasets (Musin and Asyanova, 2025). AI-driven approaches, such as machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), generative modelling, and large language models (LLMs), have shown the capabilities to mine and identify novel candidates, predict their physicochemical properties, and optimize molecular designs with better efficacy and safety profiles (Zervou et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Mittal et al., 2025). Notably, studies have leveraged LLM architectural frameworks to construct end-to-end pipelines capable of generating new antibiotics from vast sequential spaces, achieving higher potency and limited cytotoxicity during wet-lab validation (Chen et al., 2025; Saleem et al., 2025). These advancements have underscored the paradigm shifts from linear and hypothesis-driven drug discovery toward iterative and AI-oriented designs and build–test cycles (Chen et al., 2025; Saleem et al., 2025).

  Amongst various emerging modalities, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) represent one of the most promising candidates, having rapid bactericidal mechanisms and broad-spectrum activity with limited propensity for developing resistance relative to conventional antimicrobial agents (Oliveira Júnior et al., 2025; Torres et al., 2025; Veldsman et al., 2025). However, their clinical translation remains limited by challenges such as suboptimal pharmacokinetics, proteolytic instability, and cytotoxicity. Addressing these limitations requires holistic frameworks, integrating multi-dimensional predictive and generative modelling alongside structural and functional optimization (Perišić et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Szymczak et al., 2025).

  The current review proposes multi-model AI-driven strategies for AMP mining and optimization, designed to bridge the gap between in-silico prediction and translational applicability (Figure 3). This multifaceted framework integrates advanced discriminative AMP classifiers for activity prediction and generative models for de novo AMP design and generation (Noor et al., 2025; Saleem et al., 2025; Sun, 2025; Szymczak et al., 2025) (Figure 3); in-silico potency filters to predict antimicrobial efficacy against proposed pathogens (Noor et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025) (Figure 3); haemolysis and cytotoxicity predicted modules to ensure host safety (Noor et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025) (Figure 3); novelty models to enhance chemical and structural diversity, both at the genomic and protein sequence levels (National Center for Biotechnology I., 2026) (Figure 3); structural alignments and folding predictions to limit three-dimensional conformations (Figure 3); proteolytic-resistant mappings to optimize peptide stability and in-vivo persistence (Figure 3).

  The incorporation of different AI-driven architectural frameworks within a unified pipeline strategy optimizes multi-purpose outcomes across novelty, stability, efficacy, and safety, addressing key bottlenecks which historically limit AMP’s clinical translation (Zhao et al., 2025; Palmo et al., 2026) (Figure 3). Importantly, the integration of structural, functional, and proteolytic constraints at initial stages demonstrates critical advancements over pre-existing pipelines, which prioritize activity-based predictions in isolation only (Musin and Asyanova, 2025; Wang et al., 2025d).

  This review highlights recent progress in AI-enabled AMP discovery and contextualizes the adoption of multi-model strategies within the broader landscape for developing next-generation novel therapeutics. Such integrative frameworks are critical for navigating the complexities associated with AMP designs and for accelerating the clinical translation of computational discoveries to combat drug-resistant pathogens (Veldsman et al., 2025; Palmo et al., 2026). This review proposes the adaptation of multi-model advanced AI-driven approaches with context for novel AMP discovery, tailored for computational biologists, microbiologists, and clinicians, showing interdisciplinary translational aspects. By doing so, this highlights that the future goals must be directed towards precision-driven antimicrobial development, which will be capable of addressing the global AMR crisis.


### Instability and non-selectivity as pre-design constraints, not post-screening failures
  Traditional antibiotic discovery paradigms, which progress linearly from drug design and synthesis to in-vitro and in-vivo validation, may have historically undermined antimicrobial development (Blaskovich and Cooper, 2025) (Figure 4). Whilst these conventional frameworks have enabled the discovery of several first-generation antibiotics, they are increasingly unable to address the many new challenges (Blaskovich and Cooper, 2025; Farha et al., 2025). One of the key limitations is their inherently low throughputs, where the majority of compounds fail at later validation stages due to poor efficacy and drug-related cytotoxic events (Blaskovich and Cooper, 2025; Farha et al., 2025). Such iterative approaches usually require extensive financial resources and experimental efforts alongside substantial time, often extending across a decade from bench to bedside (Farha et al., 2025) (Figure 4). Consequently, these pipelines are economically unsustainable, and the probability of success remains low; therefore, investments for antibiotics are comparatively limited both from the public and private sectors (Farha et al., 2025; Manan et al., 2025) (Figure 4).

  Recently, the WHO warned of the paucity of antibiotics currently in clinical development, and the low numbers of these representing truly novel therapeutic agents (Daruka et al., 2025). Mostly, candidates are derived from pre-existing scaffolds, thereby offering accretive improvements over transformative outcomes (Daruka et al., 2025; Gargate et al., 2025). This stagnation is compounded by technical bottlenecks; the difficulty of identifying compounds that penetrate bacterial membranes, evade efflux pumps, and retain bactericidal activities against drug-resistant pathogens (Daruka et al., 2025; Saleem et al., 2026). Traditional pipelines have failed to tackle the rapid evolutionary resistance approaches, creating widening gaps between therapeutic availability and clinical needs (Daruka et al., 2025; Gargate et al., 2025).

  The sequential nature of the conventional workflows has introduced a critical structural inefficiency: decision-making is suspended until later-stage wet-lab validation. Instability, non-selectivity, and cytotoxicity issues are usually detected during drug synthesis and biological testing, leading to candidate elimination due to cost attrition (Gargate et al., 2025). These “fail-late” paradigms are particularly problematic for peptide-based therapeutics, as protease instability and host cytotoxic events are context-dependent and challenging to streamline through pragmatic iterations (Oliveira Júnior et al., 2025). Altogether, these hindrances underpin fundamental bottlenecks associated with antibiotic development, necessitating a shift toward predicted and multi-purpose design frameworks which are capable of addressing stability, efficacy, and safety simultaneously at the initial stages of drug discovery (Manan et al., 2025).


### AI, as the first stage of stability, efficacy, and safety validation

#### Discriminative AMP classifiers to predict AMP mining and discovery
  Discriminative ML models are considered the computational backbone for early AMP discovery, enabling them to mine and classify candidates based on their likelihood of demonstrating adequate bactericidal activities (Meng and Proteins, 2025; Musin and Asyanova, 2025) (Figure 5). Classical ML methods, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forests (RF), k-Nearest Neighbours (kNN), and gradient-boosting frameworks, leverage engineered characteristics based on parameters, i.e., amino acid composition, physicochemical properties, and evolutionary descriptors, to differentiate AMPs from non-functional candidates (Meng and Proteins, 2025; Musin and Asyanova, 2025) (Table 1). These approaches are compatible with high-dimensional biological datasets for capturing non-linear relationships between sequential features and antibacterial properties (Waghu and Idicula-Thomas, 2020; Meng and Proteins, 2025).

  Apart from binary AMP classification, discriminative models have provided support for high-throughput screening of diverse datasets, i.e., metagenomics, proteomics, and other commercially available datasets (Meng and Proteins, 2025; Musin and Asyanova, 2025). Architectural Frameworks, i.e., RF -based AmPEP and gradient-boosting architectures (e.g., XGBoost, LightGBM), have prioritized novel AMPs, subsequently experimentally validated against clinically relevant pathogens (Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus) (Bhadra et al., 2018; Mera-Banguero et al., 2024). Importantly, ensemble frameworks have integrated multiple AMP classifiers to limit individual model bias and have improved generalization by achieving predictive accuracies around 95% (Mera-Banguero et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2024). Altogether, these strategies illustrate the critical role of discriminative models for narrowing vast sequential spaces into experimentally tractable candidates for clinical translation (Mera-Banguero et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2024).

  Recent studies have highlighted the scope of discriminative modelling by integrating DL architectures, i.e., convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and long short-term memory (LSTM) frameworks (Zhao et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2025). These models have captured local motifs and longer-range dependencies by analysing sequences as analogous to linguistic inputs, with improved representations for sequence–function relationships (Zhao et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2025). Deep classifiers, which are trained on larger AMP databases, have shown better performance in comparison to conventional feature-oriented models, having accuracies exceeding 90% and stronger correlations with in-vitro validated antimicrobial activities (Ramazi et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023). Hybrid architectural frameworks combining physicochemical features alongside sequential embeddings have upgraded predicted robustness, especially supported by transfer learning outputs from large protein corpora (Wang, 2024; Wang et al., 2025c).

  Transformer models represent greater evolutionary changes for discriminative AMP predictions, leveraging attention mechanistic approaches to capture global sequential contexts and complex residue–residue interactions (Rives et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025c). Fine-tuned protein-based LLMs, BERT-derived architectures, have given better outcomes for both AMP classification and regression tasks by predicting MICs (minimum inhibitory concentrations) against resistant pathogens (Stokes et al., 2020; Rives et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2022). Collectively, these models have reduced dependency on handcrafted features by learning directly from raw sequential datasets, enabling screening of datasets containing millions of candidates (Stokes et al., 2020; Rives et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2022). However, their performances remain contingent on the availability of larger, diverse training datasets with careful validations to mitigate bias and overfitting (Stokes et al., 2020; Rives et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2022).

  Despite having higher predictive accuracy, discriminative AMP classifiers have key limitations for translation (Stokes et al., 2020). Most models are trained on overlapping benchmark datasets, limiting their generalizability (Stokes et al., 2020). Furthermore, classification accuracy is not sufficient to capture clinical endpoints, such as stability, potency, and toxicity (Noor et al., 2025; Szymczak et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Consequently, discriminative models are considered essential for AMP discovery and rapid filtering to prioritize candidates, but they must be incorporated alongside multi-purpose, AI-driven pipelines for downstream predictions of pharmacological stability, efficacy, and safety profiles (Noor et al., 2025; Szymczak et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). This multifaceted integration can mark necessary progressions from isolated predictive models toward holistic and AI-based frameworks for AMP design and prediction (Noor et al., 2025; Szymczak et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Despite reported higher classification metrics, direct comparison amongst discriminative models remained constrained by differences in dataset provenance, validation strategies and negative-set construction; these limitations are critically discussed in section 1.3.3.


#### Generative AI models for de novo AMP design
  Discriminative models are used to score and prioritize pre-existing peptide sequences, while generative frameworks demonstrate fundamental shifts toward de novo design and generation of entirely new AMPs by learning underlying sequential distributions (Lin et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a; Zervou et al., 2024). Deep generative architectural frameworks, such as diffusion models, generative adversarial networks (GANs), large language models (LLMs), and variational autoencoders (VAEs), have enabled exploration of previously inaccessible and vast sequential spaces (Van Oort et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2023; Zervou et al., 2024; Guan et al., 2025) (Figure 6, Table 2). These models are highly effective for addressing the traditional discovery pipelines’ limitations by incorporating multi-purpose outputs, such as stability, potency, and limited toxicity, directly within the design processes.

  Amongst these, GAN-based methods have good capabilities for the de novo generation of structurally and functionally novel and active AMPs (Dong et al., 2025b; Lai et al., 2025). Bidirectional and conditional GAN architectural frameworks have enabled iterative feedback loops between generators and discriminators, incorporating auxiliary classifiers and regressors to implement physicochemical constraints alongside antimicrobial properties (Vasconcelos et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2025b; Lai et al., 2025). These approaches have produced novel AMPs having broad-spectrum activity against multidrug-resistant pathogens exhibiting lower MICs and minimal haemolytic activity (Vasconcelos et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024; Lai et al., 2025). However, GAN models are sensitive to training instability and usually require careful fine-tuning, necessitating incorporation alongside downstream predictive models for guiding sequential optimization (Zervou et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024; Lai et al., 2025).

  Variational autoencoders (VAEs) have allowed complementary approaches by encoding sequences into continual latent spaces, enabling controlled interpolations for property-guided AMP design (Zhao et al., 2025). Conditional VAEs can incorporate physicochemical features, i.e., charge, molecular weight, and hydrophobicity parameters, into the design and generation processes, with targeted structural and functional profiles (Zhao et al., 2025). VAEs have provided interpretable and stable latent frameworks compared to GANs; their outputs usually require filtering and validation to ensure their biological relevance (Xie et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). Diffusion-based approaches have recently evolved as stronger generative tools, iteratively processing random noises into biologically relevant AMPs (Wang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024).

  These models, in combination with pretrained protein-based LLM embeddings, have demonstrated higher success rates for generating potent and diverse AMPs with better in-vivo efficacy and safety profiles (Wang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Generative performance metrics must be interpreted cautiously because computational outputs such as validity, novelty and predicted bioactivity cannot establish biological and translational generalizability, especially when reward functions and discriminative filters inherit overlapping available AMP datasets; these concerns are thoroughly discussed further in section 1.3.3.

  Collectively, generative AI frameworks have enabled the transition from passive screening to active mining and optimization of sequential spaces, accelerating the novel AMPs discovery (Lai et al., 2025; Lai et al., 2025). However, the translation impacts depend on their incorporation of discriminative inputs, structural verification, and Iterative experimentation (Lai et al., 2025; Lai et al., 2025).

  Most efficient strategies strongly rely on multi-model and hybrid pipelines, where generative AI models de novo design and generate non-natural and novel AMPs, learn distributions within peptide sequential spaces and propose previously unseen sequences, thereby shifting computational discovery towards de novo AMP designs (Lai et al., 2025). Generative models are increasingly being combined alongside hybrid frameworks, where AMPs are evaluated using structural, novelty, bioactivity, stability and toxicity predictors (Lai et al., 2025) (Table 3). Therefore, hybrid approaches can offer attractive routes towards multi-purpose AMP optimization, although they require independent validations, model calibration, followed by experimentally validated feedback (Lai et al., 2025) (Table 3).

  On the contrary, discriminative models assess whether predicted peptides possess antimicrobial properties from pre-existing resources and learn quantitative relationships between predefined biological endpoints and peptide representations, therefore suitable for classification, regression and AMP prioritization (Lai et al., 2025) (Table 3). Convergence of both generative and predictive paradigms demonstrates critical steps toward precision-driven AMPs design and generation to address the accelerating global AMR burden (Li et al., 2025; Mittal et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Zervou et al., 2025).


#### Dataset overlapping, negative-set construction and over-generalization
  One of the key limitations associated with discriminative models; independent predictors are developed and evaluated using overlapping databases (Agüero-Chapin et al., 2023). Mostly, positive AMPs are repeatedly extracted from commercially available repositories, followed by diverse combinations of sequence-length filtering, redundancy reduction and train–test partitioning (Agüero-Chapin et al., 2023). Consequently, comparisons amongst models cannot be interpreted as evaluations based on independent biological information (Agüero-Chapin et al., 2023).

  Importantly, random splitting places identical and evolutionarily related AMPs across test and training partitions, exploiting sequential similarities rather than learning determinants of bioactivity (Zhao et al., 2026). They can inflate performance metrics, explaining why models report excellent internal benchmark performances but can generalize less effectively to AMPs from previously unexplored families and ecological environments (Zhao et al., 2026). Thus, complex architectures must not be interpreted as biologically superior when sequential overlapping, benchmark provenance, and homology between evaluation and training datasets have not been controlled (Wang et al., 2025).

  Negative class has represented one of the equally important sources of bias (Yang and Xu, 2024). Experimentally validated inactive AMPs are scarce because unsuccessful AMPs have not been reported systematically (Yang and Xu, 2024). Fewer AMP classifiers have been constructed using negative datasets from computationally convenient and reliable sources (Yang and Xu, 2024). Absence of AMP annotations does not support experimental evidence of microbial inactivity. If artificially negative outputs differ from positive AMPs depending on amino-acid composition, length, charge, and cellular origin, AMP classifiers can learn these dataset-construction artefacts rather than biologically active determinants (Zhao et al., 2026). These concerns have been extended to specialized safety classifiers, where incomplete non-haemolytic sequences and heterogeneous assay conditions can influence labels, thereby apparently affecting model performance (Yang and Xu, 2024).

  Performance metrics across AMP prediction models must not be interpreted as directly comparable measures for superiority (Agüero-Chapin et al., 2023). Although several studies have reported F1-score, accuracy, AUROC, and MCC values exceeding 90%, these outcomes are derived from heterogeneous datasets, sequence-redundancy thresholds, negative-set definitions, and validation strategies (Zhao et al., 2026). Moreover, these performance metrics capture different aspects of predictive behaviour; therefore, they cannot be ranked interchangeably across studies. Accordingly, the performance metrics summarized in Table 1–9 must be interpreted as within-study evidence of model performance only. Direct superiority claims can be justified when competing models have been evaluated on the same independent datasets using redundancy control, identical preprocessing, decision thresholds and evaluation metrics (Zhao et al., 2026).

  Many discriminative models are constructed using benchmark families assembled from commercially available repositories and usually supplemented with UniProt/Swiss-Prot-derived negative sequential spaces (Zhao et al., 2026). Consequently, test and training datasets contain near-identical and evolutionarily related AMPs (Zhao et al., 2026). Moreover, highly cross-validated performance metrics partly reflect recognition of AMPs, which are represented during training rather than generalization to novel AMPs (Renaud and Mansbach, 2023). Independent test datasets can provide stronger and novel outputs only when their sequences are not included in model development and are separate from training data; labelling them as “independent datasets” cannot support biological independence (Renaud and Mansbach, 2023). Predicted models must be supported by cross-validation, and benchmark families must be interpreted cautiously and evaluated on prospective metagenomics, rigorously held-out external datasets and experimentally validated candidates (Chen et al., 2025).

  Future benchmarking must prioritize wet-lab validated both negative and positive AMPs, explicitly mention assay conditions and database provenance, limit cross-partition sequential redundancy using cluster-oriented splitting, and assess models on temporally separated and external datasets (Chen et al., 2025). Performance metrics must be reported against family-held-out and experimental tests, alongside traditional random splits; this would provide stronger measures of whether AI-predicted models can generalize to the novel AMP sequential spaces that are intended to discover (Chen et al., 2025).


#### In-silico safety screening for therapeutic-index modelling
  The key barriers to the clinical translation of AMPs are host-cell toxicity, reflecting their membrane-disruptive properties (Rathore et al., 2025). Consequently, cytotoxicity-predicting models have emerged as equal objectives alongside better efficacy profiles within modern computational pipeline strategies (Rathore et al., 2025). Haemolysis and cytotoxicity are major factors in later-stage AMP therapeutic failures, highlighting the need to incorporate safety-predicted models at the initial stages of AMP design rather than as downstream filters (Rathore et al., 2025). This transition has accelerated the development of AI-driven cytotoxicity models by mapping peptide sequence spaces and structural features that link them to toxicity endpoints, enabling rapid in-silico triaging of novel AMPs before synthesis and wet-lab validation.


##### In-silico haemolytic filters
  Classical ML methods used physicochemical descriptors; however, recent approaches have increasingly leveraged DL and protein-based LLM models for capturing higher-order sequential structural–cytotoxicity relationships (Abdelbaky et al., 2024) (Figure 7; Table 4). For instance, CNNs and RNNs demonstrated improved performance for predicting haemolytic activity directly by learning local motifs and from sequential representations associated with membrane disruption and toxicity properties (Abdelbaky et al., 2024). The latest architectural frameworks, i.e. tAMPer, integrated sequential embeddings from protein-LLMs (ESM-2) alongside structural features, improving peptide toxicity predictions by incorporating 3D contexts alongside primary sequential information (Ebrahimikondori et al., 2024). These advancements reflect broader transitions toward representation learning and reduce reliance on handcrafted features, improving generalisation across diverse datasets.

  Haemolytic predictions have evolved from binary outcomes toward quantitative modelling. ConsAMPHemo and the latest regression-based models predict haemolytic concentrations (HC50), the peptide concentrations causing 50% lysis of red blood cells, and higher HC50 demonstrates greater blood-cell compatibility due to lower haemolytic toxicity, providing both mechanistic insights and predictive capabilities (Xie et al., 2025). Therefore, integrated pipelines have combined efficacy and safety profiling alongside binary predicted outcomes, highlighting the importance of multi-purpose learning for optimizing therapeutic indices rather than single outputs (Bhatnagar et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2025). However, certain challenges remain within dataset bias, a lack of negative data demonstrating those AMPs having haemolytic effects compared with successful, safer ones, cross-species generalization, and assay variability, underscoring the need for wet-lab validated datasets and standardized benchmarks (Bhatnagar et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2025). Discriminative AMP classifiers reporting performance of haemolysis-predicted models must be interpreted in the context of overlapping datasets and heterogeneous experimental outputs (Bhatnagar et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2025).

  AMP haemolytic models have progressed from descriptor-driven binary classifiers, i.e. HemoPred with neural-network and ensemble systems (HemoNet, HLPpred-Fuse and HAPPENN) (Win et al., 2017; Hasan et al., 2020; Timmons and Hewage, 2020), to advanced graph and transfer-based learning, and multi-purpose structural-aware predictors (AMPDeep, tAMPer and ToxGIN) (Salem et al., 2022; Ebrahimikondori et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024). These latest models not only identify haemolytic AMPs, but also estimate quantitative toxicity outputs (haemolytic concentrations), more relevant for prioritizing AMPs based on therapeutic index (the ratio of antibacterial activity to toxicity) than mere binary labels (Win et al., 2017; Hasan et al., 2020; Timmons and Hewage, 2020). These evolutionary approaches strongly support the incorporation of cytotoxicity-predicted models as front-end design constraints within AMP mining and discovery pipelines. However, reported performance metrics can be inflated by datasets overlapping with limited external validation.


##### In-silico cytotoxicity filters for AMP therapeutics
  Traditional wet-lab approaches for toxicity assessment are labour-intensive, costly and may rely on in-vivo models, reducing their scalability during early-phase discovery. Consequently, there have been sustained shifts over the past years towards computational-based toxicity predictions, leveraging (Gupta et al., 2013). Recently, DL has enabled rapid in-silico screening of novel AMPs (Gupta et al., 2013). Early efforts are primarily focused on similarity-driven approaches, but are limited by poor generalization (Sharma et al., 2022) (Figure 8; Table 5). ToxinPred2 has illustrated that there is a transition toward hybrid frameworks incorporating motif discovery, sequence similarity (BLAST), and ML scoring, achieving better predictive outcomes (AUC = 0.99) across various protein datasets (Sharma et al., 2022).

  Toxicity-predicting models have evolved from feature-engineered classical ML (SVM using motif features and amino acid composition) to representation-oriented learning frameworks (Jain and Kihara, 2019). ToxinPred demonstrated sequence-derived features that effectively distinguished toxic from non-toxic AMPs, but was limited to constrained datasets and shorter peptides (Gupta et al., 2013). Advanced models (ToxDL and TOXIFY) integrated evolutionary information, gene ontology, and deep neural architectural frameworks to enhance generalization across diverse protein families (Villegas-Morcillo et al., 2020). Notably, DL models, i.e., ToxDL and TOXIFY, incorporated end-to-end sequential learning to reduce dependency on handcrafted features and enabled adequate capture of latent toxicity outputs across diverse biological contexts (Villegas-Morcillo et al., 2020).

  Multimodel architectural frameworks have integrated structural features, evolutionary profiles (PSSM), and protein LLM-based embeddings. ATSE demonstrated how attention mechanisms, graph-based learning, and transfer learning can enhance toxicity predictions by integrating both sequential and structural contexts (Wei et al., 2021). Notably, contemporary approaches have increasingly adopted hybrid and ensemble strategies, combining motif analysis, similarity searches, and ML predictions to reduce false positive outcomes by improving robustness (Sharma et al., 2022). Such integrative architectural frameworks have outperformed individual approaches by balancing specificity and sensitivity across diverse datasets (Sharma et al., 2022). Altogether, these are paradigm shifts toward multi-layered AI-driven predictive strategies, where toxicity must be embedded as core design constraints within AMP discovery pipelines.


#### In-silico potency screening
  One of the key limitations of early AMP models was that they performed binary classification, which was not sufficient for AMP translational prioritization. In practice, the key useful endpoints are quantitative efficacy outcomes, measured as MICs (lowest concentration of an antimicrobial agents which can inhibit visible growth of pathogens following incubation for a certain time period), and it favours triage, ranking, and optimization against specific drug-resistant pathogens (Wu et al., 2014; Chiu et al., 2026). In recent years, the computational approaches have shifted from sequence-based statistical screenings toward species-based MIC regressions, where the goals are to estimate how strongly novel AMPs inhibit bacterial activities against WHO priority pathogens (Wu et al., 2014; Chiu et al., 2026) (Figure 9; Table 6).

  Traditional potency-predicted models mainly relied on handcrafted sequential descriptors (Wu et al., 2014). An amino-acid activity contribution and linked substitution-based sequential optimization and wet-lab validation have demonstrated that computationally guided AMPs could achieve better potency both in-vitro and in-vivo (Wu et al., 2014). This has been followed by explicit MIC-prediction architectural frameworks, i.e., pseudo-amino-acid compositions alongside Gaussian kernel regression models for E. coli, establishing MIC predictions as distinctive computational tasks (Chiu et al., 2026). These early approaches demonstrated that antimicrobial potency outcomes were not only predictable but also empirically validated (Chiu et al., 2026).

  Incorporation of DL regression architectural frameworks has improved predictive potency and accuracy (Dean et al., 2021). PepVAE coupled VAEs alongside activity-predicted models showed that gradient boosting performed better against E. coli; median R2 = 0.73 and root mean square error (RMSE)= 0.50. MBC-Attention approaches, combining multi-branch CNNs along with attention for E. coli MIC regression, demonstrated Pearson correlation = 0.775 and RMSE = 0.533, respectively (Dean et al., 2021). Recently, an ensemble model integrated genomic and sequence-derived features against WHO-priority pathogens (Pearson correlations of 0.756 for S. aureus, 0.781 for E. coli, and 0.802 for P. aeruginosa, respectively), marking the transition from generic activity screening towards pathogen-based quantitative potency predicted outcomes (Chung et al., 2024).

  BERT-AmPEP60, fine-tuned ProtBERT for species-based MIC predictions and reported mean squared errors (MSEs) against E. coli (0.266) and S. aureus (0.303) and Pearson correlations of 0.796 and 0.753, respectively (Chung et al., 2024). ESKAPEE-MICpred incorporated two-input transfer-learning and ensemble architectural frameworks for pathogen-specific MIC predictions (Sharma et al., 2024). A recent pipeline (AMPredictor) has integrated generative designs alongside potency scorings: bifunctional AMP frameworks linked GAN generators to graph-convolutional MIC regressors, achieved RMSE = 0.5348 and Pearson= 0.7072, and validated 24 novel AMPs in an animal infection model (Dong et al., 2025b). Collectively, these architectural frameworks show how potency predictions have become a central layer within multi-purpose AMP pipelines to jointly optimize antimicrobial activity and novelty against resistant pathogens (Chung et al., 2024).


#### In-silico novelty screening
  In silico novelty screening must be considered one of the key components of the pipeline to limit the rediscovery of known AMPs. Novelty must be evaluated at two stages: first, against well-known peptide sequential spaces and proteins to limit homology; second, against genomics and transcriptomics spaces to determine whether the sequence maps back to untranslated regions, known genes and unannotated ORFs in pre-existing assemblies (Gertz et al., 2006; Camacho et al., 2009; Buchfink et al., 2021) (Table 7). In the past few years, it has relied on profile-based or alignment retrieval systems instead of traditional AI models (Gertz et al., 2006; Camacho et al., 2009). tBLASTn served as a standard translated-search-based method for mapping protein and peptide queries to nucleotide databases against all six reading frames (Gertz et al., 2006; Camacho et al., 2009) (Figure 10). tBLASTn has upgraded the reliability for nucleotide homologous searches by providing composition-oriented statistical outcomes, making it more effective for genome-level novelty outputs rather than naive translational searches (Gertz et al., 2006; Camacho et al., 2009) (Figure 10).

  At the protein sequence level, novelty screening requires searching for candidates against commercially available large databases (Fu et al., 2012; Steinegger and Söding, 2017; Buchfink et al., 2021). MMseqs2 and DIAMOND have become crucial in recent times, as they conserve BLAST-like sensitivity and limit the time costs for large-scale datasets (Buchfink et al., 2021). DIAMOND has improved based-matched BLAST-like sensitivity and delivers approximately 80-fold speedup over BLASTP, as well as completing an nr versus UniRef50 tree-of-life scaling (Buchfink et al., 2021) (Figure 11). MMseqs2 demonstrated higher sensitivity than PSI-BLAST at far higher speeds; therefore, it can be run iteratively for larger workflows (Steinegger and Söding, 2017). To limit redundancy, CD-HIT considers practical upstream filters allowing it to quickly cluster highly similar sequences and limit inflated novelty due to near-duplicate inputs (Fu et al., 2012; Steinegger and Söding, 2017; Buchfink et al., 2021).

  Pairwise local alignments alone can miss remote homologies for peptides that are short and compositionally biased towards precursor proteins (van Kempen et al., 2024). Many higher-confidence novelty workflows add domain-based or profile-HMM screenings. HMMER tools (jackhammer and phmmer) have provided profile-based sequential similarities that are considered more sensitive than simple pairwise methods for remote relationships (Finn et al., 2011). InterProScan has helped to determine whether a candidate is within an already known protein and motif architectural landscapes (Jones et al., 2014). Foldseek has extended novelty screenings beyond primary sequences by enabling fast structural searches; useful when sequential identities are lower and fold-based similarities suggest that candidates belong to pre-existing structural families rather than representing truly novel scaffolds (van Kempen et al., 2024).

  For the AMP pipeline, multi-layer novelty screening is essential and requires removal of redundancy with CD-HITs. Peptides and proteins must be searched against well-known databases with BLASTP, MMseqs2 and DIAMOND, respectively, and promising AMPs must be backed onto genomic assemblies and transcript catalogues with tBLASTn to check mapping with known homologous regions (Fu et al., 2012; Steinegger and Söding, 2017; Buchfink et al., 2021). Later, InterProScan and HMMER can be applied for remote family assignments, and, when structural predictions are considered as an essential part of the workflows (Finn et al., 2011; Jones et al., 2014). Foldseek can be used to exclude structurally familiar scaffolds to limit rediscovery risks before downstream wet-lab validation (van Kempen et al., 2024).


#### In-silico stability screening
  Poor stability associated with rapid degradation by host and microbial proteases is one of the major translational barriers (Mathur et al., 2018). Therefore, in-silico AMP stability screening has become a valuable tool, with two key goals: firstly, to recognize protease-sensitive cleavage motifs that affect peptide half-life; secondly, to estimate circulation half-life as one of the proxies (Mathur et al., 2018) (Figure 12; Table 8). In recent times, advanced ML models incorporate evolutionary, sequential, structural, and physicochemical parameters, which are essential to identify early triage layers to flag unstable AMPs before synthesis and redesign to make protease resistance (Song et al., 2012; Mathur et al., 2018).

  PeptideCutter contains knowledge-based cleavage simulators and maps potential non-enzymatic and enzymatic cleavage sites, as well as providing rapid first-pass stability assessments by highlighting liability sites (Turalić et al., 2025). The protease-specific ML PROSPER can predict integrated feature-based cleavage sites across various proteases, and when followed by PROSPERous, can improve high-throughput predictions by expanding the coverage across 90 proteases (Song et al., 2012; Song et al., 2018). These tools are essential for AMP optimization, as shorter AMPs are sensitive to single cleavage events. Therefore, they provide advanced information regarding the peptide’s engineering, such as cyclization, residual substitutions, and D-amino-acid design strategies (Song et al., 2012; Song et al., 2018; Turalić et al., 2025).

  Procleave has incorporated both sequential and 3D structural information to improve protease-based cleavage-site prediction, reflecting that local conformation often determines whether nominal motifs are actually cleaved (Li et al., 2020b). On the contrary, panCleave possesses pan-protease RF models for computational-based proteolysis and is used to mine encrypted AMPs from extinct human proteomes, leading to wet-lab validations of antimicrobial fragments, highlighting that cleavage predictions must be used constructively for AMP discovery (Kelley et al., 2024). PlifePred, a complementary cleavage-site tool, has addressed other stability dimensions by predicting peptide circulation half-life from experimentally validated data (Mathur et al., 2018). Altogether, these approaches highlight the need for adapting a multi-model workflow in which AMPs are screened for novelty, pharmacokinetic stability, potency, and toxicity before peptide synthesis.


#### In-silico molecular docking approaches for structural optimization
  Molecular docking (MD) is considered the principal component of structure-based AMP optimization, which predicts the binding modes and target specificity at atomic resolution. Unlike sequential-based predicted models, MD models ligand–receptor interactions to rationalise how AMPs interact with microbial membranes, enzymes, and intracellular targets (Davis and Baker, 2009; Lemmon and Meiler, 2012; DeLuca et al., 2015) (Figure 13; Table 9). In recent years, MD has advanced from rigid-body, physics-oriented scoring to hybrid architectural frameworks incorporating diverse ML, structural predictions, and flexible docking, improving performance metrics (Davis and Baker, 2009; Lemmon and Meiler, 2012; DeLuca et al., 2015). Traditional MD tools are widely used due to their interpretability and robustness, particularly during the initial stages of virtual screening (Morris et al., 2009) (Figure 13, Table 9).

  AutoDock and AutoDock Vina have been foundational for peptide-protein docking. AutoDock Vina demonstrated better improvements in terms of speed and accuracy due to multi-threading and effective scoring functions, making them ideal for larger-scale virtual screening (Morris et al., 2009). Rosetta-based MD frameworks (RosettaLigand) have incorporated conformational sampling alongside physics-based energy functions to predict binding poses and optimized peptide-protein interactions, suitable for flexible ligands (Davis and Baker, 2009; Lemmon and Meiler, 2012; DeLuca et al., 2015). Notably, tools have extensively validated structure-based drug discovery and identified novel bioactive compounds. However, traditional MD approaches struggle with solvent effects, protein flexibility, and free-energy estimations (Davis and Baker, 2009; Lemmon and Meiler, 2012; DeLuca et al., 2015).

  Recently, MD approaches have been transformed by integrating AI and structure-predicted models (Jumper et al., 2021; Abramson et al., 2024). AlphaFold has drastically increased the availability of high-quality protein structures, removing key bottlenecks for docking workflows (Jumper et al., 2021). AlphaFold3 can directly model protein-peptide and protein-ligand complexes, outperforming by predicting binding conformations (Abramson et al., 2024). Diffusion-based models (DiffDock) have reframed docking as a generative issue and significantly improved hit rates by correctly predicting bindings (Corso et al., 2022). Collectively, it highlights paradigm shifts toward data-oriented structural modelling by learning representations that complement and replace physics-based scoring.

  MD approaches to identify novel bacterial targets against predicted AMPs require intensive computational resources. Latest MD frameworks support closed-loop optimization and larger-scale virtual screenings (Zhang et al., 2025; Voitsitskyi et al., 2026). Ultra-large-scale approaches screen billions of candidates against targeted structures, ensuring in-silico high-affinity binders and novel scaffold discovery before wet-lab validation (Zhang et al., 2025; Voitsitskyi et al., 2026). Hybrid systems have integrated docking with ML–based selection and scoring, e.g., LigDockTailor, to optimise performance outputs based on ligand properties (Zhang et al., 2025). Altogether, MD critically bridges sequence-oriented AMP design and structure-based optimization, enabling refinement for binding affinity and specificity within an advanced multi-model AMP pipeline.


## Current AI-based approaches for mining, discovery, and optimization of AMPs
  Evolution from classical ML to DL approaches and LLMs has revolutionized the computational toolkits for AMP mining and discovery in recent times (Fernandes et al., 2023; Perišić et al., 2025). However, increasing architectural frameworks and complexities do not guarantee better performance metrics (Fernandes et al., 2023; Perišić et al., 2025). There is no single AI framework that is optimal for AMP mining, identification, and characterization; therefore, it is dependent on the multitask models and available data resources (Fernandes et al., 2023; Perišić et al., 2025).

  Multitask-matched models will be more beneficial than automatic progression based on their complex architectural frameworks. Classical models, RF, SVM, and XGBoost are effective for imbalanced and smaller datasets for rapid binary classification, while DL models, such as transformers, CNNs, and LSTMs, have outperformed for larger and labelled sequential datasets by learning long-range residual dependencies (Moretta et al., 2020; Musin and Asyanova, 2025; Yu et al., 2025). For structural tasks, 3D-aware geometric DL approaches are required for receptor-based designing and membrane-interactive modelling (Fernandes et al., 2023). Depending on the available computational resources, decisions regarding selecting various architectural frameworks vary with modality and predictive outcomes.

  Models, such as AMPGen and EBAMP, have demonstrated that an architecture framework-based selection can be task-driven, i.e., RL-based and diffusion models were used for de novo AMP generation, while transformer-oriented MIC regressors predicted efficacy, and ensemble models favoured safety outcomes with multi-purpose screening when stability and interpretability were critical (Sharma et al., 2024; Veldsman et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). Earlier evolutionary and genetic strategies demonstrated better multi-objective outputs regarding optimization, but their performances were hampered by the noisy biological data and limited datasets (Shin et al., 2022; Agüero-Chapin et al., 2023).

  AMP workflows can span heterogeneous objectives, i.e., structure-based classification and designs, target-based specificity, MICs regression, and toxicity prediction (Wang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025d; Zhao et al., 2025). Multi-model evolution can expand the AMP discovery toolkit, depending on whether architectural choices are aligned with data quality and biological contexts, not complexity alone (Cao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025b). Therefore, a multi-model AI-driven pipeline strategy, as reviewed in the current paper, for predicting diverse architectural frameworks, aligning with the best practical implications for AMP discovery and optimization, is essential (Cao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025b) (Figure 14).

  Multi-model AI-driven pipelines can facilitate mechanistic-informed routes to accelerate AMP discovery by progressing from wet-lab explorations to computation-based triage (Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025d) (Figure 14). Therefore, mining larger metagenomic datasets using single-model screenings will be insufficient, as AMP discovery has intrinsic multi-objectives, i.e., predicted potency must be balanced against novelty, stability, bioactivity, and safety (Figure 15). Multi-model AI pipelines can address these concerns by combining foundation models to encode peptide grammar to enable targeted prioritization, discriminative predicted models for pathogen-specific potency, and safety filters to predict cytotoxicity and haemolysis (Li et al., 2024). Recent studies have explicitly demonstrated that multi-stage AI approaches have delivered validated AMPs against Gram-negative bacteria inside compressed design and test cycles, including pathogen-targeted outputs (Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025d).

  A multi-model AI-driven pipeline can mine and discover novel AMPs from diverse resources, i.e., natural, genomics, metagenomics, and de novo generative spaces, to predict efficacy and safety profile before wet-lab validations, thereby limiting the peptides that must be synthesized and assayed to achieve clinical relevance (Santos-Júnior et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). It has been supported by recent ML and DL frameworks by identifying AMPs from microbiomes, subsequently in-vitro validation, and generating de novo AMPs with higher “hit rates” coupled with discriminative and safety models (Jin et al., 2025). MIC-based models against Gram-negative strains further prioritize WHO-listed pathogens to accelerate mechanism-driven selection (Bae et al., 2025).

  Stronger computational and preclinical performances cannot guarantee downstream therapeutic successes. AMPGen provided contemporary examples of attrition at the computational-experimental interfaces (Jin et al., 2025). Although its XGBoost discriminators, diffusion-driven generators and LSTM-based target-specific scorer prioritized 40 de novo AMPs (Jin et al., 2025). 38 were synthesized and 31 of these met antimicrobial activity criteria (success rates of 81.58%) and were target-dependent: 19/20 synthesized AMPs targeting E. coli were active compared with only 8/18 targeting S. aureus (Jin et al., 2025). After multimodel computational filtering, a subset of highly prioritized AMPs had failed experiments, illustrating in-silico outputs alone cannot be considered as substitutes for biological validation (Jin et al., 2025).

  At later translational stages, pexiganan (MSI-78) has provided a historical example (Lipsky et al., 2008). Despite having strong potency and advancement to phase III trials for managing infected diabetic foot ulcers, the clinical outcomes did not translate into regulatory approvals; the studies failed to meet their primary efficacy objectives (Lipsky et al., 2008). Iseganan, broad-spectrum protegrin analogues with better antimicrobial activity, failed to reduce ulcerative oral mucositis in a larger and randomized Phase III trial recruiting 545 patients (Trotti et al., 2004). Together, these studies have illustrated a continuum of attrition starting from computational predictions to wet-lab bioactivity, final clinical outcomes, and reinforce the urgent need for incorporating multimodel AMP pipelines to demonstrate pharmacological (PK/PD) and physiological constraints before clinical progression (Trotti et al., 2004; Lipsky et al., 2008).

  Table 10 summarizes different multi-model ML approaches with reported wet-lab, in-vitro, and in-vivo validation by highlighting their predicted outcomes, essential to demonstrate translational impacts. Prioritizing multi-model AI approaches can offer real-world performance for antimicrobial outcomes, not just in-silico validity.


### Current barriers and limitations to robust AI-driven AMP discovery
  Despite rapid advances in ML, LLMs and generative AI, the predicted performance reported for AMP models must be interpreted cautiously. Major challenges arise from dataset bias and poor representation of biodiversity. Currently available AMP databases are enriched with previously characterized, cationic and relatively shorter membrane-active AMPs, whereas inactive AMP sequences have not been reported systematically (Torres et al., 2025; Veldsman et al., 2025). Mostly negative datasets are assembled computationally from different commercial resources, rather than experimentally validated AMPs (Torres et al., 2025). Such approaches can create differences and will allow models to adapt to dataset-specific characteristics rather than biologically relevant AMPs. Homologous AMPs and sequence redundancy distributed across test and training sets can cause information leakage, thereby overestimating the generalization (Wang, 2023; Wang et al., 2026). Therefore, future benchmarking requires similarity-aware train–test separations, redundancy-controlled partitions and evaluation against robust and independent sequence families and organism sources (Wang, 2023; Wang et al., 2026).

  There is limited availability and heterogeneity of experimentally validated datasets, and only a smaller number of AMPs are characterized under universal experimental conditions. Activity annotations originating from heterogeneous assays may differ in microbial strains, medium compositions and endpoint definitions (Noor et al., 2025). MIC values obtained for the same AMPs may not be directly comparable across different studies. Moreover, safety endpoints are fragmented across cell lines, erythrocyte species and exposure conditions (Chen et al., 2025; Saleem et al., 2025). These concerns are particularly consequential for quantitative stability, haemolysis, potency, cytotoxicity, and pharmacokinetic models, where inconsistent experimental metadata can cause label noise (Chen et al., 2025; Saleem et al., 2025). Therefore, databases should evolve from sequence and bioactivity repositories towards assay-aware multimodel platforms having universal experimental protocols including pathogen-based and concentration-dependent information alongside serum binding, protease stability, and clinically-related safety endpoints (Chen et al., 2025; Saleem et al., 2025).

  Model interpretability has represented an additional barrier to translational confidence. Different AI-oriented architectural frameworks can provide strong discriminative predictions with limited insights about sequence motifs and physicochemical interactions (Chen et al., 2025; Saleem et al., 2025). Post hoc approaches (e.g. attention mappings and residue-level saliency) can enhance interpretability, but do not propose biological mechanisms, particularly for generative models, where highly predicted AMP probabilities do not establish plausible mechanisms, microbial and resistance profiles (Musin and Asyanova, 2025). Therefore, explainable AI must be coupled with mechanistic assays and experimental perturbations, so computationally identified determinants can be tested independently.

  Methodological transparency and reproducibility have remained inconsistent across AMP modelling studies; differences in databases, redundancy thresholds, sequential preprocessing, hyperparameters, random seeds and data splitting can produce variations amongst comparable methods (Mittal et al., 2025). In some studies, training sequences may occur accidentally within benchmark sets, while unavailable source code and pretrained weights can limit independent reproduction (Chen et al., 2025; Saleem et al., 2025).

  Absence of universally accepted benchmarking protocols for AMP classifiers, and differences within the benchmark compositions have made direct comparison problematic. This is amplified by class imbalances, where higher AUROC may provide incomplete performance descriptions (Mittal et al., 2025). Generative models can be assessed using divergent measures, such as validity, novelty and predicted activity, while quantitative MIC models can be evaluated using heterogeneous transformations, regression metrics and pathogen subsets (Noor et al., 2025). Temporal validations where models can be trained on available data, subsequently tested on reported AMPs can provide especially rigorous measures of prospective performance (Sun, 2025).

  Computational performance cannot equate with therapeutic performance. Most AI-generated AMPs have been evaluated in-silico, and only smaller proportions have progressed through synthesis, serum stability assessments and in-vivo validations (MIC determination and cell toxicity) (Chen et al., 2025; Saleem et al., 2025). Physiological factors (ionic strength, serum and protein binding, proteolysis, aggregation, biodistribution and immune interactions) can substantially alter activities (Chen et al., 2025; Saleem et al., 2025). Multimodel pipelines must be viewed as prioritization platforms for the next phases of AI-driven AMP discovery, requiring prospective design-build and test-learn cycles, where standardized experiments will update computational models, essential for moving from retrospective predictions towards clinically-oriented AMP engineering.


### Future directives
  Convergence of advances within structural biology, sequential modelling, and high-throughput computational approaches has favoured a shift from fragmented predicted workflows toward a well-integrated and multi-purpose strategy for AMP discovery (Palmo et al., 2026). Whilst traditional linear approaches evaluate stability, activity and toxicity sequentially, the proposed methodologies adapt closed-loop, multi-model architectural frameworks, where AMPs are iteratively refined across orthogonal predictive multi-layers (Wang et al., 2025). This advanced AI-driven design has enabled simultaneous optimization of structural and proteolytic compatibility, novelty, potency, and host safety, thereby addressing the key bottlenecks historically limited to AMPs’ clinical translation (Saleem et al., 2026).

  Multi-model AI-driven exhibits a hierarchical screening strategy. Candidate sequences can be derived from metagenomics or curated libraries and are evaluated using discriminative AMP classifiers, which later pass through quantitative MIC regression frameworks to predict and prioritize AMPs against resistant pathogens (Guan et al., 2025). Parallelly, hemolytic and cytotoxicity models serve as initial safety filters to exclude AMPs predicted to have higher cytotoxicity (Xie et al., 2025). Early-stage incorporation of potency and safety filters demonstrates key departures from traditional drug discovery pipelines, where cytotoxicity is evaluated following wet-lab validation (Xie et al., 2025).

  Following efficacy and safety filtering, shortlisted AMPs must undergo novelty assessments; genomic search and profile-based homology detection must translate with rapid sequential alignment tools (DIAMOND, MMseqs2) to identify peptides that are active and distinctive from known AMPs and conserved protein domains (Steinegger and Söding, 2017; Buchfink et al., 2021). Later, they must be structurally characterized using sophisticated structure prediction models (AlphaFold2/3) and downstream molecular interactions against novel targets, providing insights into binding orientations and potential mechanistic approaches facilitating rational optimization (Jumper et al., 2021; Voitsitskyi et al., 2026).

  Integration of degradation-aware and stability tools must be used to predict proteolytic susceptibility and AMP half-life to exclude unstable candidates (Song et al., 2012; Li et al., 2020b). Peptide modifications, such as cyclization, residual substitution, and incorporation of non-natural components, can enhance stability without impairing bactericidal activity, enabling iterative closed-loop optimization cycles to improve diverse objectives simultaneously (Song et al., 2018).

  At present, the absence of standardized datasets demonstrating the serum protein binding effects on antimicrobial activity is one of the major obstacles associated with AI-driven drug discovery (Wang, 2023; Wang et al., 2026). Most AMPs possess better potency outcomes under laboratory conditions, but exhibit reduced efficacy within physiological environments due to non-specific interactions with circulating serum proteins, i.e., albumin, globulin, and lipoproteins (Wang, 2023; Wang et al., 2026).

  There is a need to develop larger-scale and wet-lab-validated serum-binding databases for systematically quantifying AMP in the presence of human serum. Therefore, integration of serum-binding data resources alongside ML, DL and generative AI frameworks would enable the identification, prediction and optimization of AMPs’ performance under biologically relevant conditions, thereby improving the clinical translation of next-generation AMP from bench to bedside (Wang, 2023; Wang et al., 2026).

  Despite rapid advancements in AI-assisted AMP discovery, clinical translation depends on establishing regulatory and manufacturing frameworks (Zhao et al., 2026). Regulatory assessments require demonstration of higher predictive accuracies; therefore, developers must provide documentation of model provenance, predefined decision thresholds and to what extent computational predictions will contribute towards AMP selection and sequence modification (Zhao et al., 2026). Additional scrutiny is required when models can introduce unusual sequence motifs and chemical modifications for which pharmacological evidence is limited. It must converge with conventional chemistry, control and manufacturing requirements (Zhao et al., 2026).

  At present, manufacturing has become challenging for highly modified, cyclized and non-canonical AMPs, where solid-phase synthesis, analytical characterization and purification have increased production cost and complicated scale-up (Chen et al., 2025; Saleem et al., 2025). Future pipelines must incorporate developability and manufacturability as one of explicit design objectives, so that AMPs characterized and optimized using computational screenings will be compatible with scalable manufacturing production and subsequent regulatory evaluation (Zhang et al., 2025).

  From retrospective validation to larger prospective experimental studies, AI-designed AMPs must be evaluated against several clinical and resistant strains using standardized in-vitro assays. Clinical translation must demonstrate across genetically and phenotypically diverse pathogens, followed by incorporation of protein-binding and protease stability, cytotoxicity, and pharmacodynamic–pharmacokinetic relationships, before advancing towards animal models (Su et al., 2025). External validations across different laboratories will be mandatory to determine model performance beyond the datasets (Zhao et al., 2026). Most credible pipelines must route towards clinical applications possessing prospective design-build and test-learn frameworks, where AI-predicted AMPs must be subjected to diverse experimental filters and the resulting biological data must be returned to the computational pipelines. Such iterative validations will enable AMP discovery by progressing from high-performing in-silico drug design towards reproducible AMPs with better therapeutic potential (Torres et al., 2025).


### Conclusion
  An integrated, multi-model, advanced AI-driven pipeline demonstrates transitions toward systematic AMP design and optimization, where different predicted modalities operate in a concerted manner rather than in isolation. Therefore, by embedding functional, structural, and pharmacological constraints within a unified and well-integrated framework, this approach favours effective navigation of peptide design spacing to reduce later-stage attrition. These approaches are well-suited for high-throughput computing, where larger-scale AMP mining, generation and evaluation can be performed in parallel to support rapid translation from in-silico discovery to wet-lab validation for clinical translation. This multi-model strategy provides generalizable blueprints for developing next-generation antimicrobial agents to combat the global AMR crisis in the era of computational biology.
