# Benchmarking AI-driven protocols for cyclic peptide conformer prediction and ranking


## Abstract
  Generative AI is being used in peptide design to optimize binders from early research stages. One notable application is with respect to cyclic peptides, wherein AI assists in modelling conformers in bound or solution states. This work benchmarks state-of-the-art protocols' ability to predict and rank conformers of cyclic peptide binders alone and in complex with their targets, using sequence or all-atom representations. The datasets consist of cyclic peptides with experimental structures available in public repositories. Various tools were included in the benchmark: HighFold, cyclic-aware AlphaFold2 as implemented in EvoBind2, CycPepDock, Boltz-1/1x, Boltz-2, and Chai-1. We found that more recent tools provide a structure prediction advantage over their AlphaFold2-based counterparts, although accurate results with both sets of tools are largely limited to protein–peptide complexes, rather than the free solution structure. In addition, using a cyclic relative positional encoding offset proved key for accurate prediction, and the natural amino acid representation was found to outperform the more general SMILES string input. We mirror prior literature findings on linear peptides by showing that even for accurate predictions, model confidence metrics poorly correlate with experimentally observed binding affinity. Lastly, we compare the performance of AI-based tools to that of physics-based simulations, showing that the latter remain the only way to account for intrinsic conformational flexibility of cyclic peptides, but that the former have a significant price/performance advantage in complex structure prediction. This study provides insights applicable to the design and optimization of cyclic peptides, contributing to advancements in peptide-based therapeutics.


## Introduction
  Emerging advancements in generative AI are revolutionizing the field of peptide design by optimizing the structural and functional properties of peptide binders from early stages.1 These computational approaches are valuable in the design and optimization of cyclic peptides, which play a pivotal role in therapeutic development.2 Cyclic peptides, due to their constrained conformational flexibility and intrinsic structural stability, often exhibit improved metabolic stability and binding affinity compared to linear counterparts, making them attractive scaffolds for drug discovery.3,4 Recently, various academic and commercial initiatives have demonstrated the impact of cyclic peptides as therapeutic agents where higher oral bioavailability can be beneficial, offering the advantages of both small molecules and larger molecules, such as biologics, to increase potency towards their targets.5,6

  Among approved cyclic peptides as drugs, we have examples such as linaclotide, used to treat gastrointestinal disorders,7 and lanreotide, an oncology drug.8 In recent years, new molecules have been proposed to enhance oral bioavailability with properties that exceed the Lipinski rules. This is the case with Luna18, a KRAS inhibitor identified from an mRNA display library, which, despite high hydrophobicity, can reach the selective target.9 Another case is MK-0616 from Merck, developed for the treatment of high cholesterol.10 In many of these cases, computer-aided drug design has contributed to narrowing down the best candidates in terms of properties and potential success, where predicting accurately the conformations of cyclic peptides and their binding modes in solution or bound states is crucial for the development of peptide-based therapeutics.11 Following the AlphaFold era, multiple state-of-the-art approaches have emerged, providing tools for studying these peptides using either sequence-based information or detailed all-atom representations.

  Having accurate 3D representations is crucial for estimating properties of cyclic peptides, including permeability, a relevant molecular feature correlated with oral bioavailability.12 The collection of PAMPA data for cyclic peptides in public databases, such as CycPeptMPDB, has motivated the development of prediction models that can classify molecules as self-permeable or those requiring permeation enhancers to cross membranes and find intracellular targets.13 3D information is also relevant from a tractability perspective, where protein target surfaces and known protein–protein interactions can help to suggest cyclic peptides as modalities to modulate proteins of interest.14 Another option is the co-folding of molecular binders that have been developed for small molecules, nucleic acids, and both linear and cyclic peptides.15 The idea is to model how binders dynamically interact with their target proteins, offering insights into binding affinity, specificity, and conformational adaptability. Currently, co-folding has the potential to uncover novel interaction geometries that docking methodologies used to explore. Despite their potential, these methods require comprehensive evaluation to ensure reproducibility and reliability in predictive performance.

  This study benchmarks state-of-the-art protocols for cyclic peptide conformer prediction and ranking using datasets consisting of cyclic peptides with experimentally verified structures available in public repositories. This work assesses the ability of a set of tools to predict conformations and rank peptide binders using sequence and all-atom inputs. Additionally, representative conformers from enhanced molecular dynamics (MD) were obtained for one of the targets to compare the prediction performance with the output models. By leveraging a systematic benchmarking infrastructure, we aim to provide insights into the optimal modelling scenarios for this modality, thereby advancing peptide-based therapeutic development.


## Methodology

### Datasets
  A set of cyclic peptides with and without experimentally determined structures available in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) was selected and curated based on studies reporting bioactivity data. The set includes three representative groups, each of which contains data for multiple cyclic peptide binders reported for a single target: interactions of cyclic peptides containing disulfide bonds with the urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA) protein,16–18 the complement component 3 (C3c) bound to compstatin analogs,19 and the HIV integrase bound to head-to-tail cyclic peptide binders.20 The peptides bound to the target protein were annotated based on the availability of Ki or IC50 values, ensuring diverse structural and functional interactions among the peptides. All the selected structures report ideal quality metrics based on parameters such as the crystal resolution (<3 Å) and average B-factors that for most of the structures are reported below 40, which is usual for moderate thermal motions in protein complexes (Table S1). However, for some sequences, no PDB structures are available. In these cases, an internal code was assigned, and the model metrics were still calculated to correlate their affinities. A summary of the codes, sequences, affinities, and publications is provided for each of the three targets in Tables 1–3.

  The input for the tools was split into two categories: using peptide sequences alone or peptide sequences plus target sequences. In some cases the SMILES format was used as input, which was generated using the PepFuNN package.21 The SMILES was used in two cases: i) with peptides containing non-canonical amino acids that cannot be included in the sequence FASTA format, and ii) to compare side-by-side the performance using the sequence or the SMILES for head-to-tail cyclic peptides in the HIV/integrase dataset. In addition, a set of cyclic peptide conformers in solution (Table S2) was included based on a curated list available online (https://github.com/ysl-lab/CP_crystal_structures, accessed in March 2025). We also added a group of representative peptides for which experimental structure files have not been made publicly available in any form that could have resulted in their inclusion in the tested tools' training sets – whether by deposition in a structure database or inclusion in a publication: nine internal, unpublished cyclic peptides, as well as three cyclic peptides previously described in literature and shown to be well-structured via molecular dynamics simulation trajectory comparison to undisclosed NMR structure ensembles – cyclo-GNSRV, cyclo-VVGGVG, and cyclo-IVGGVG.22,23


### Modelling tools
  Based on the current state of the art, five generative AI modelling tools were selected for the benchmarking, with some focused on cyclic peptide modelling, and some having full-atom representation capabilities for cases where SMILES are used as input. Here we provide a general description of each tool:

  • HighFold: designed specifically for cyclic-peptide structure prediction and incorporates constraints such as head-to-tail cyclization and disulfide-bond connectivity. By explicitly accounting for cyclic topology, it is expected to generate more physically plausible conformations than general-purpose predictors for constrained peptides. Its performance may nevertheless depend on correct specification of the covalent connectivity and can be limited for unusual peptide chemistries or highly flexible cyclic scaffolds.24

  • Cyclic-aware AlphaFold2, as implemented in EvoBind2: EvoBind2 uses AlphaFold2 (AF2) as a structure-prediction engine within an in silico directed-evolution framework for peptide-binder design, allowing the receptor interface to adapt during peptide-sequence optimization. AF2 employs an Evoformer-based neural-network architecture trained on experimentally determined structures and evolutionary sequence information. However, EvoBind2 relies on AF2 monomer weights and is sensitive to user-defined inputs and parameter settings; consequently, we used the default settings provided by EvoBind2 instead of the parameters suggested by the original AF2 package.25

  • CycPepDock: Rosetta-based cyclic-peptide docking and refinement protocol that combines conformational sampling with a knowledge-based energy function, while allowing cyclization and other covalent constraints to be specified. Its strength is the explicit treatment of peptide flexibility and cyclic connectivity, but performance depends substantially on the initial receptor structure, sampling settings, and user-defined constraints. With the default configuration CyPepDock tended to place the peptide away from the binding site. To solve this, we used the settings provided in the Colab notebook the paper linked to at the time of writing.26

  • Boltz-1, Boltz-1X, and Boltz-2: Boltz models are deep-learning approaches for biomolecular interaction prediction that use sequence-based and full-atom molecular representations to model proteins, peptides, nucleic acids, and small molecules. Their architectures use learned geometric representations and diffusion-based structure generation, trained primarily on experimentally determined biomolecular structures. Their principal advantage is the joint prediction of heterogeneous molecular complexes; however, predictions may be less reliable for highly flexible systems, uncommon modifications or covalent linkages, and interactions that depend on substantial receptor rearrangement or explicit solvent effects.27,28

  • Chai-1: multimolecular structure-prediction method that supports proteins, peptides, nucleic acids, and small molecules from amino-acid sequence and/or SMILES inputs. It combines sequence-derived and atom-level chemical information to predict all-atom complex structures using learned structural and chemical priors. This broad input compatibility is advantageous for peptide–receptor modeling, although predicted structures remain hypotheses and may be less accurate for flexible peptides, poorly represented chemotypes, uncertain protonation states, or interactions strongly influenced by solvent and conformational dynamics.29

  • Protenix-v2: an AlphaFold3-like, diffusion-based all-atom structure-prediction framework for biomolecular complexes containing proteins, peptides, nucleic acids, ligands, ions, and modified residues. It integrates sequence and evolutionary information with atom-level chemical representations to jointly predict the structures of complete molecular assemblies. Its main advantage is broad support for heterogeneous complexes, whereas limitations include uncertainty for flexible or disordered regions, rare cyclic or noncanonical peptide chemistries, and cases in which binding depends on conformational states not represented in the available structural data.30

  Note that AlphaFold3 was not included in the study, given its somewhat restrictive licensing, and more importantly, the fact that its applicability to cyclic peptides has already been well described in the literature.31 In these studies, it was shown that AlphaFold3 provides reasonable accuracy on cyclic peptide systems that may be used to guide preliminary drug design efforts, although its performance is degraded compared to its performance on proteins. In particular, it has displayed better predictive accuracy for disulfide-cyclized peptides compared to other cyclization chemistries, lower predictive accuracy for side chain atoms over backbone atoms, and the need for manual structure validation due to cyclization constraints not being explicitly encoded.15,31 In addition, the lack of a clear relationship between confidence metrics and prediction accuracy, although some correlation exists, was flagged in the literature.15,31,32 We aim to complement these observations by discussing the relevant considerations in the context of other prediction tools.

  The tools described above were implemented as applications on the internal Novo Nordisk BioLib platform, and all available input options were used. The corresponding models were generated in monomeric and multimeric regimes (i.e., peptides alone or bound to the protein target). Unless otherwise specified, all tools were run using their default parameters and recommended settings. CycPepDock and cyclic-aware AlphaFold2 were exceptions: preliminary calculations showed that their default settings frequently displaced the peptide from the binding site, resulting in whole-complex RMSD values exceeding 10 Å. The parameters and user inputs for CycPepDock and cyclic-aware AlphaFold2 are detailed in the configuration files available in the Supporting Information. For tools without multimeric functionality, an artificial linker was used between the components.

  To assess the prediction quality, different performance metrics were calculated, including peptide backbone and sidechain RMSD in both monomeric and multimeric setups. The RMSD of the target and the whole complex was also measured, together with the dihedral RMSD. Spearman and Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to compare predicted binding confidence (i.e., pLDDT, iPDE) with experimental binding affinities. Moreover, Rosetta energy scores were calculated for the predicted structures to evaluate their out-of-the-box energetic favorability.

  To determine the impact of memorization of publicly available structures that may have been included in the models' training sets, the benchmark was performed both with the peptide sequences as provided in the data sources and with all possible circular shifts of the sequences, accounting for cyclic degeneracy. For example, cyclo-GNSRV would have also been included as cyclo-NSRVG, cyclo-SRVGN, cyclo-RVGNS, and cyclo-VGNSR. To avoid circular shifting impacting the validity of structural similarity metrics, any necessary structure alignments were performed by mapping all atoms to their exact chemical counterpart, rather than by residue index. Since this data set augmentation would have resulted in results from longer peptides being more heavily weighted, as they would contribute more individual data points, the shifts were subsequently “collapsed” by averaging all numerical results over all possible equivalent sequences, so that each peptide still contributes a singular data point to the set. The results are reported on the aggregate data set, with examples of memorization nonetheless discussed, and a comparison to the single-sequence run included in SI (Fig. S8).


### Molecular dynamics comparison
  To complement AI-driven predictions, enhanced-sampling molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted to obtain representative conformers from the trajectories and to compute similar comparison metrics to those calculated for the modeling outputs, including backbone, sidechain, and complex RMSD. As cyclic peptides' constrained nature renders them prone to becoming trapped in local free-energy minima,33 we applied bias-exchange metadynamics to enhance conformational sampling.34 Cyclic peptides lend themselves particularly well to this simulation approach, as they have been previously shown to undergo conformational transitions via the coupled movements of pairs of adjacent backbone dihedral angles, providing an intuitive choice of collective variables to bias.35

  Given the computationally-intensive nature of MD simulations, we performed the comparison on a subset of the data – one of the representative groups described in section 2.1. Notably, these peptides ultimately turned out to significantly populate their bound conformation while simulated in solution, rendering the performance evaluation in the “monomer” regime meaningful. For each sequence in the HIV-integrase data set shown in Table 3, two distinct starting structures were generated, and simulation trajectories were obtained in increments of 100 ns per replica, with a single replica for each adjacent phi/psi angle pair, and five unbiased, neutral replicas. The first 50 ns of the trajectory were discarded, as the free energy landscape was “padded” with Gaussian biases deposited as part of the simulation protocol, and the simulations were terminated upon convergence. Convergence was defined using the normalized integrated product (NIP), a similarity metric related to the Dice–Sørensen coefficient,36 with the convergence threshold set at 0.9. The trajectories were analyzed using dihedral principal component analysis for dimensionality reduction,37 and density peak-based cluster analysis.38 Relying on the notion that target binding affinity of cyclic peptides correlates with their structural pre-organization into the bound conformation,39 we simulated free peptides in solution and considered both the dominant cluster conformation, and the overall percentage of frames matching the bound conformation, defined as frames with no dihedral angle deviating more than 25 degrees from the ref. 40. The simulations were performed in the NPT ensemble, using the RSFF2 force field41 implemented in the GROMACS 2024.2 package,42 using the PLUMED 2.9.2 plugin for bias exchange metadynamics.43,44 Further details of the simulation protocol, including system building instructions, the equilibration procedure, and the exact parameter values used, are available in the Supporting Information.


## Results and discussion

### Monomer regime results
  In the monomer regime, the tools exhibit a large amount of variance, with structural similarity results ranging from a backbone RMSD of ∼1.3 Å (in the case of HighFold and Boltz-2 with a cyclic constraint), to above 4 Å (for Protenix, Chai-1, and unconstrained Boltz-2), as seen in Fig. 1. This variance is highly intuitive – in the absence of an interacting partner, which could inform the orientation of the peptide during co-folding, a relative positional encoding adjustment is necessary for the model to cyclize the peptide. A confirmation of this observation can be seen in Fig. 2, which shows representative peptide orientations for several tools, showing that in the monomeric case, peptides are only readily cyclized by dedicated cyclic peptide tools. A notable exception exists in the form of Boltz-1/1x, which occasionally renders peptides which appear approximately cyclic – and thus achieves a lower average RMSD compared to its unmodified peers. Upon closer examination, this behavior coincides with the peptide being predicted to assume its known bound conformation. As this type of prediction does not translate to peptides known to be outside the training set, we believe the cyclization seen in this case to be a consequence of memorization. We provide evidence for this assertion in Fig. S6, which shows that Boltz-1 does not reliably maintain the predicted pseudo-cyclic conformation when the peptide sequence is circularly shifted to a degenerate one that is not available in a public dataset (e.g., cyclo-ALKIDNLD to cyclo-LKIDNLDA). This claim is further supported by the slight performance degradation observed in the aggregate data set, shown here, compared to the single-sequence data set, shown in SI – this behavior is not observed for other cyclization-unaware tools.

  In the SMILES regime, wherein the cyclic peptide is provided analogously to a small-molecule ligand, the tools are on an even playing field, as the advantage of a modified relative positional encoding is no longer a factor. However, in this case, all tools perform very similarly to one another, and all perform worse than the optimal amino acid-representation predictions. This implies that the tools have a relatively limited capability for handling large, constrained molecules provided as SMILES, and that they likely parse this input in comparable ways.

  This difference in performance between using sequences or SMILES as input depends on how the molecules are tokenized (i.e., token per amino acid or per heavy atom), and how the geometry and evolutionary constraints are estimated. For example, in the case of Boltz-2, when sequences are used, additional information from multiple sequence alignments, structural templates and Ramachandran distributions are used to assign correct secondary structures based on peptide bond priors. In the case of SMILES input, 3D conformers generated for non-polymer ligand entities are used as initialization for the diffusion models, which is also affected by the number of atoms in the ligand.27,28 In consequence, using the SMILES format as input can penalize the accuracy of the estimated cyclic peptide conformer despite its utility to generate complex chemical structures beyond natural amino acids.

  An important caveat to note is that for peptides whose free structure is not known or well-defined, the monomer PDB files were generated by stripping the target out of the bound structure. Subsequent MD simulations revealed that the resulting conformation does not necessarily correspond to that dominant for the free peptide in solution. A comparison to MD dominant conformations is shown in section 3.5, with an in-depth breakdown of how well-represented the predicted monomer structures are in the MD ensembles included in the SI.

  It also bears mentioning that a simple RMSD comparison only assesses the ability of a structure prediction tool to recapitulate a reference structure, rather than the entire conformational ensemble. The latter is particularly important when discussing cyclic peptides, which are rarely – especially in solution – so rigid as to exist as a single dominant conformation.45 However, at present, tools capable of predicting conformational ensembles are in their infancy, with limitations preventing their general application to cyclic peptides: e.g., tools relying on flow matching, such as AlphaFlow46 are made with proteins in mind and do not perform well on peptides; dedicated cyclic peptide tools such as StrEAMM47 on the other hand, are limited to specific types and sizes of cyclic peptide scaffolds. As a result, this manuscript will focus on evaluating static structure predictors on the task that they were designed for, while offering some insight into how well said static structures are represented in ensembles determined by simulation.

  Finally, comparing the monomer and multimer predictions directly quantifies the contribution of receptor context to the predicted peptide conformation. Since the experimentally determined reference structures correspond to peptide–protein complexes, improved agreement of multimeric predictions with these structures indicates that the target provides information needed to recover the bound peptide conformation and binding pose. Conversely, differences between monomeric and multimeric predictions should not necessarily be interpreted as an error in the monomer prediction: an isolated peptide may populate a conformational ensemble in solution that differs from its receptor-stabilized bound state. Thus, the monomer–multimer comparison is intended to determine the extent to which each method leverages protein–peptide interaction context, while recognizing that the target-free predictions represent a bound-state-independent structural hypothesis rather than a definitive solution-state conformer.


### Multimer regime results
  In the multimer regime, the impact of memorization becomes readily apparent. On the single-sequence data set (Fig. S8 in SI), all tool-parameter set combinations perform at least reasonably well, with most structure predictions within an RMSD value of ∼1.5 Å or better, with only HighFold-monomer and cyclic-aware AlphaFold2/EvoBind2 trailing when using AlphaFold2 (AF2) monomer weights. On that data set, in the presence of a protein target, the advantage of a relative positional encoding adjustment is less pronounced as well, suggesting that the model is informed by the prediction of protein–peptide interactions consistent with a cyclized peptide backbone. However, on the aggregate data set, shown in Fig. 3, the cyclization-unaware tools display a significant drop in performance, with only the cyclization-aware tools maintaining quality predictions regardless of sequence shift.

  Moreover, we note that, as seen in the monomer regime, while the SMILES representation affords greater flexibility in terms of input specification, allowing for the introduction of non-canonical amino acids, or bypassing the need for code changes to tools that do not natively support cyclic peptides, it also appears to degrade performance. For instance, whereas most cyclization-aware tools attain an average RMSD across the whole dataset of well under 1 Å while using the natural amino acid representation, with SMILES, the performance drops to >2.5 Å (Fig. 3, top right).

  The above observations carry over into the whole-complex structure predictions, with multimer-weight tools significantly outperforming their monomer-weight counterparts, and Boltz-2-cyc and HighFold-multimer taking the overall performance crown on the aggregate dataset. It is of note that Boltz and Protenix did not display substantial performance loss between the amino acid and SMILES representations when it comes to modeling the entire complex, suggesting that treating the cyclic peptide as a small-molecule ligand results in the target protein conformation more closely matching the experimental structure. This, ultimately, has little bearing on the usability of these tools in a cyclic peptide design and characterization context. Perhaps intuitively, performance appears to be somewhat degraded for peptides which are not included in the training set (Fig. S1), though access to a larger dataset would allow for stronger conclusions to be drawn on this front.

  On the topic of varying representations, another point of difference worth noting is that there is a substantial performance delta when comparing head-to-tail macrocyclic peptides and those cyclized via a disulfide bond (Fig. S9). Namely, cyclic peptide-aware tools excel in the domain of head-to-tail peptides, whereas tools without the necessary relative positional encoding tweaks exhibit superior performance on disulfide-bonded macrocycles. This is an intuitive observation, given that disulfide bonds are fairly common in general protein data sets, leading general protein structure prediction tools trained on such data sets to favor disulfide formation.

  Lastly, it is important to consider that certain tools, particularly CycPepDock and EvoBind's implementation of AF2, are less robust to parameter settings and user input than the others. Out of the box, the default settings for both tools have a tendency to displace the peptide far from the binding site, with a whole-complex RMSD well in excess of 10 Å. With significant optimization effort (see SI), we were able to bring CycPepDock to par with its AlphaFold3 (AF3)-based competition, with the strongest peptide conformation prediction, and only slightly inferior whole-complex prediction. EvoBind's AF2 implementation, relying on monomer weights, continues to trail behind the other methods, albeit less severely so.


### Overall accuracy comparison
  On the whole, Protenix and Chai-1 appear to lead the competition when it comes to predicting the structures of cyclic peptide–protein complexes, suggesting an increment – albeit slight – in performance from one generation of AI-based structure prediction models (AF2) to the next (AF3 and its contemporaries). The improved accuracy is reflected in global and local structure similarity alike (Fig. 4 and S3), as well as the contact map comparison to experimental structures, where applicable. Note, however, that while global similarity – as shown by the RMSD, is quite good, and the relative performance observations hold true regardless – all tools still display local deviations, as evidenced by a high backbone dihedral RMSD (Fig. S3).

  However, despite the high observed prediction accuracy, we advise caution when using these tools, particularly given their present limitations. Notably, the tested tools may perform better on disulfide-cyclized peptides, as disulfide bonds and related motifs are abundant in their respective training sets, than on head-to-tail cyclized ones. In addition, other, more “exotic” cyclization chemistry, such as lariat and lasso peptides, thioether cyclized peptides, or combinations thereof, are becoming increasingly more common in both academic and industrial applications, as is the use of noncanonical amino acids (NCAAs).48–51 Such compounds may only be provided as inputs to new-generation tools, and only as SMILES strings, which we have demonstrated leads to a lowered prediction accuracy.


### Correlation of confidence metrics with experimental binding affinity
  One would be justified in believing that the better of a binder a peptide is, the more likely its complex structure is to be predicted correctly. However, this does not appear to hold true for the molecules considered in this study. We have considered the overall correlation between binding affinity – where available – and similarity between predicted and experimentally determined structure, binding affinity and model confidence metrics (namely, pLDDT, pTM, and ipTM), as well as structural similarity and model confidence. An overview of the results is shown in Fig. 5.

  Notably, there is no consistent relationship that can be observed between model confidence and experimental potency or binding affinity data: the observed correlations are weak across the board, and across the tool set, can be both positive and negative. Therefore, we would caution against using model confidence metrics as a quantitative proxy for binding affinity, or selection of optimal binders. Instead, these metrics appear more useful as a qualitative measure of whether the model should be considered valid, or a binary classifier of whether or not a compound is expected to bind a particular target.

  On the other hand, the results comparing the structure prediction accuracy to either confidence metrics or experimental potency/binding affinity data are more of a mixed bag. In the former case, it appears that for most tools, the RMSD to the reference structure tends to trend downwards as model confidence increases, with Protenix, Boltz-1/1x/2, Chai-1, and HighFold-multimer all showing some correlation. A subset of these tools also exhibits a relationship between binding affinity and structure prediction accuracy, although the utility of this correlation is limited in a drug discovery setting, where a priori structural information knowledge is not readily available. It should also be noted that the assessment of this particular correlation is made difficult by the scarcity of data points, causing most pairwise comparisons to fail the statistical significance threshold by virtue of low sample size. Binding correlations with modelling metrics have been examined with other protein–peptide complexes beyond cyclic topologies in the literature, which confirm the complexity of such correlations due to the intrinsic flexibility of the binders plus the number of heavy atoms in the peptide binders.52

  And while, therefore, the nontrivial relationship among model confidence, accuracy, and bioactivity, appears to in part be a characteristic of AI structure prediction in general, it bears mentioning that in cyclic peptides, these relationships may be further muddied by the unique conformational properties of cyclic peptides. As the conformational strain of macrocycles leads to a fundamentally different conformational space being accessible to cyclic peptides compared to their linear counterparts and larger proteins,40 models trained on predominantly linear molecules may not be able to “learn” as much about the underlying mechanisms governing their structure–activity relationships. As a result, different confidence metric scaling and thresholds may need to be applied to provide the same level of useful information compared to what is accessible for other modalities.


### Comparison with physics-based methods
  Outside of the recently emerging domain of AI-driven molecular structure and property prediction, physics-based approaches are still routinely used for molecular characterization. These tools rely on explicit calculations based on fundamental physical equations (e.g., Newtonian equations of motion) and force field-defined potentials to determine atomic positions and molecular energetics. As such, they are often more computationally expensive than their AI counterparts, but present a robust, reproducible, numerically-grounded approximation of physical processes. Among these tools, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are a common approach used to determine the dynamics and conformational preferences of flexible molecules.

  Notably, when discussing the performance of AI structure prediction tools in the monomer context, we mentioned that, due to the scarcity of structural information for free peptides in solution, the monomer reference structures assumed a bound conformation for the peptide. In order to cover the cases where the bound conformation does not match that of the free peptide in solution, we determined the conformational ensemble of a subset of our benchmark set (the HIV-1 integrase-targeting peptides) using enhanced sampling molecular dynamics simulations in explicit solvent. Following the cluster analysis of these simulation trajectories, we determined the dominant conformation in solution and its population, along with the population of the bound conformation in the free ensemble. An overview of the results is shown in Table S3 and Fig. S4. Note that, while an in-depth discussion of the MD simulation trajectories is outside the scope of this benchmark, wherein MD is used primarily as a reference point for comparison, we would like to highlight that we observed trajectories consistent with prior literature observations pertaining to cyclic peptides. Specifically, the presence of relatively high-energy conformers in non-negligible populations, consistent with ring strain causing cyclic peptides to become “stuck” in local free energy minima, and structural features in the dominant conformers consistent with prior art.35 For instance, the even-length peptides tested exhibited conformational shifts highly consistent with repositioning of opposed beta-turns and rearrangement of intramolecular hydrogen bonds stabilizing them (data not shown).35,40

  The small size of the data set makes conclusions difficult to draw, particularly given that the top-populated cluster in most of the simulation trajectories on this data set did, in fact, correspond to the observed bound conformation. This population distribution may be due to the need for additional sampling in the trajectories, despite observed convergence criteria, or could be attributable to the peptides binding the target protein in a manner related to their structural pre-organization in solution. However, in the case of the one trajectory where the top-populated cluster was not similar to the bound conformation, we observe that greater similarity is inferred between the bound-conformation cluster and the monomer structure predictions (data shown for HighFold monomer, Fig. S4) than between the static predicted structure and the top cluster, in line with the expectation that ML-based structure prediction tools will yield results similar to what they have “seen”. Thus, we advise caution in the general case, in which it is not universally true that the bound conformation will be predominantly observed in solution. These tools may, however, be used synergistically with MD, as agreement between the predicted static complex structure and the top solution trajectory cluster may be a positive indicator with respect to binding.

  Lastly, we performed a simple case study on three literature peptides not deposited in the PDB with no known binding information, but known structures confirmed by NMR to match their respective MD trajectories, which contain very high (in excess of 60%) populations of the dominant cluster, i.e., well-structured cyclic peptides.22,23 The results are shown in Fig. S5. In this case, none of the tools tested were able to recapitulate the notable structural features of well-structured cyclic pentamers and hexamers, respectively; namely, a beta-turn opposing a tight kink, and two opposed beta-turns stabilized by intramolecular hydrogen bonds, flanked by beta-branched amino acid residues.22,23 Notably, however, when provided with the amino acid sequence, the Boltz-1 family tools, unlike the others, yield an approximately cyclic peptide, and in the case of VVGGVG, with the correct orientation – this observation warrants further investigation with an expanded data set of well-structured cyclic peptides.

  In addition to the MD simulation comparison, we also utilized the Rosetta software package to score the predicted complex structures from all tested tools. The average energy scores for the cyclic peptides alone, as well as for the whole complexes are shown in Fig. S7. Notably, tools capable of native cyclic peptide treatment tend to produce higher-energy predictions. This is not necessarily solely due to worse prediction quality and could also be attributed to the introduction of ring strain into the structure. A notable exception comes in the form of HighFold, which produces substantially less strained complex structures than its counterparts, albeit with the caveat that the variance in the energy scores is very large and precludes the determination of statistical significance.

  On average, the results appear to largely mirror what was seen in the prediction accuracy benchmark, with new-generation tools performing well and HighFold being the best representative of the last generation. In all cases, however, the side chain packing is suboptimal, providing further evidence that these tools, while impressive, are still prone to local deviations, as we have seen for backbone dihedral angles as well. We thus strongly recommend relaxing any structure predictions produced by these tools, rather than using the output as-is.


## Conclusions
  With cyclic peptides establishing themselves as a strong contender for targeting biological processes previously considered undruggable, and the rapid development of tools for protein and peptide structure prediction, we consider it relevant to provide some key insights into how the modelling state-of-the-art tools predict cyclic peptide structures, and which are the inherent challenges.

  Firstly, tools specifically tailored to cyclic peptides appear to outperform general-purpose tools, although the difference can largely be traced to the latter being forced to treat cyclic peptides as small-molecule-like ligands, using a representation such as SMILES, which provides flexibility, but degrades prediction quality.

  Moreover, given the scarcity of training data including cyclic peptide structures, and the few data points that do exist largely being bound to protein targets, all tools presently available are likely best applied to protein–peptide complexes, rather than attempting to predict whether a cyclic peptide is well-structured or not, or establish its solution conformational ensemble. In these areas, physics-based tools remain the gold standard, although the difference in computational expense does provide an argument in favor of ML-based tools. One must, however, remain wary of memorization, and aware that state-of-the-art models return static structures drawn from a relatively narrow distribution. Recent models based on flow matching, as well as training on MD simulations, have shown some promise with respect to both linear46,53 and cyclic47,54 peptides, and may bridge this knowledge gap in due time despite limited present capability. What's more, outside of “pure” AI-based structure prediction, the advancements in the field of machine learning have led to the enhancement of classic physics-based techniques as well. While this article employed a classical force field given literature evidence favoring the force field used with respect to macrocyclic peptide applications,41,47 by incorporating highly accurate quantum mechanical calculations into a training set for an ML model, a number of authors have constructed machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs), which have been applied to molecular dynamics studies of other modalities in recent publications, particularly in the domain of implicit solvent treatment.56–58

  Memorization in particular is a topic deserving of more attention. This benchmark aims to shed some light on the prevalence and severity of this issue by including an aggregate data set, augmented by all possible circular shifts of a head-to-tail cyclic peptide sequence, and comparing it to the parent, single-sequence data set, where sequences are provided to modeling tools as stated in public data sources. In-depth results from the latter data set are shown in Fig. S8 in SI. Across the board, it appears that circularly shifting the sequence does impact performance to some extent, even though all sequence variations are meant to encode the exact same peptide – this, along with the in-training vs. out-of-training (public vs. internal sequence) comparison, implies that the existing models do not fully “understand” cyclic peptides, and instead rely on the limited data present in general protein training sets.

  In addition, there appears to be an incremental improvement between 1st-generation (AF2-based) and 2nd-generation (AF3 and open-source alternatives) tools. With that in mind, we look forward to future work involving tools released after this article's data set was finalized. For example, following the cutoff date for tool inclusion in this study, a third-party modification to Boltz-1 has been released, which allows the combination of amino acid encoding with CCD entries for NCAAs.55 It is our hope that continued development in this field will address this presently outstanding challenge.

  As mentioned in section 3.1, one caveat that applies to the above conclusions, and a limitation of the benchmark in its present form, is that it primarily focuses on the ability of the tested tools to capture a – usually bound – static reference structure, which may not correspond to the dominant conformation, if one exists, in solution, or be representative of the whole conformational ensemble. This limitation is in part a consequence of the scarcity of tools able to predict conformational ensembles at all, and we would like to use this opportunity to stress the importance of development of such tools going forward.

  Overall, while questions remain regarding the utility of model confidence metrics and how well they translate to practical concerns such as protein–peptide binding, the modeling arsenal for cyclic peptide–protein complexes now includes a useful new set of tools, with further improvements on the horizon.
