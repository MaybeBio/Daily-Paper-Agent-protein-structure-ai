# A Shortcut to Structure in AlphaFold 3


## Abstract
  AlphaFold 3 predicts protein structures with remarkable accuracy, yet how structural information emerges within the model remains poorly understood. Here, through causal interventions on internal representations and direct probing of every Pairformer block, we trace the formation of global protein geometry and identify the multiple sequence alignment (MSA) as a structural shortcut to the fold. Removing the MSA largely preserves local secondary structure while disrupting the long-range relationships that define global topology. Restoring the MSA-enriched pair representation at only forty residues recovers most of this lost organization, including at pairs never directly modified. This contribution depends on the detailed direction of the MSA module’s output rather than its magnitude. The Pairformer rapidly converts this signal into global geometry: the final fold becomes recoverable by approximately block 9 of 48 for a majority of proteins, roughly twenty-seven blocks before the model’s decoder can render it, whereas without the MSA it remains inaccessible for most proteins throughout the pass. Which homologs are supplied shapes this trajectory more strongly than which query is supplied; it persists for a designed query that never evolved but collapses for a shuffled sequence. Most importantly, an alignment built for a different protein that shares the fold, supplied only at the structurally corresponding columns, raises the median TM-score against experiment from 0.44 to 0.72, while the same alignment shifted a few residues along the chain performs worse than supplying no alignment at all. What AlphaFold 3 reads from an alignment is therefore a description of the fold itself, transferable between proteins that share one, rather than the query’s own evolutionary history. This explains both its accuracy and the limits of what it has solved.


## Introduction
  The release of AlphaFold 2 in 2021 transformed structural biology. For the first time, it was possible to predict protein structures with near-experimental accuracy for many targets [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Its release also catalyzed a wave of new research combining biological and AI techniques [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. In 2024, AlphaFold 3 extended these capabilities to nucleic acids, small molecules, and biomolecular assemblies, expanding an already powerful tool into one suitable for protein design, drug discovery, and biosecurity [14, 4, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Yet, in spite of these advances, two critical questions have persisted: how is AlphaFold able to transform an amino-acid sequence into an accurate three-dimensional structure, and to what extent does its prediction process reflect the biophysics underlying protein folding?

  In our previous work, we set out to answer these questions [22]. In that work, we examined the internal representations of AlphaFold 3 in a novel mechanistic interpretability study, demonstrating that removing the multiple sequence alignment (MSA), which provides evolutionary information from homologous proteins [14, 23, 24], substantially deteriorates AlphaFold 3’s internal representations, confidence, and structural predictions, while reintroducing as few as one to five sufficiently diverse homologs can recover much of the benefit of a full MSA [22, 25]. However, our findings left several important questions unanswered. The most important among them are: Which structural relationships depend most strongly on evolutionary information, and is this related to the evolutionary history of the protein or the particular fold it adopts? How much of the MSA-enriched representation is required to recover the global fold? And how does the Pairformer transform this information into structural geometry?

  Addressing these questions requires looking beyond the final structures AlphaFold 3 outputs and examining the internal representations and computations that produce them. We proceed in five steps. First, as an observation, we identify which features of a predicted structure depend on evolutionary information and which survive its removal. Second, through causal intervention, we show that restoring a sparse portion of the MSA-enriched representation recovers much of the lost global structure, including relationships never directly restored. Third, to identify the mechanism, we follow this representation through the Pairformer and show that, although the representation entering the Pairformer yields no global fold under linear decoding, the fold becomes accessible within the first ten of its 48 blocks for over half of proteins—whereas without the MSA, it does not emerge for most proteins within the first pass. Fourth, to establish the source of this information, we independently vary the MSA and the query sequence, and examine sequence-divergent proteins that share a fold, revealing that the MSA sets when and along which route the fold emerges, while the query determines whether it emerges at all. Fifth, and most directly, we transplant the structurally aligned columns of one protein’s alignment into a sequentially dissimilar protein that shares its fold, and find that this alone recovers much of the structure, while the same alignment displaced by a few residues performs worse than supplying none. Together, these steps reveal a structural shortcut at the center of this study: what AlphaFold 3 reads from an alignment is a description of the fold, supplied in the correct correspondence, rather than the query’s evolutionary history.


## Results

### MSA information preferentially supports nonlocal structural organization
  We began with the two cohorts of 200 monomeric proteins assembled in our previous work [22]: novel proteins without a match above 40% sequence identity to any sequence deposited before AlphaFold 3’s September 30, 2021 training cutoff, and similar proteins exceeding that threshold. All were themselves deposited after the cutoff (see Methods).

  We generated AlphaFold 3 predictions with and without an MSA and compared them with the corresponding experimental structures from the PDB to quantify the loss in accuracy. Separately, we compared each no-MSA prediction against its corresponding full-MSA prediction, allowing us to identify which features of the model’s preferred structural solution were retained or lost following the removal of what at first glance appears to be evolutionary information. As expected from our previous findings [22], the presence of evolutionary (or perhaps fold) information as encoded in the MSA contributed substantially to predictive accuracy in both cohorts, with the median TM-score [26] against experiment declining from 0.96 to 0.52 in novel proteins and from 0.97 to 0.46 in similar proteins following MSA removal (Supplementary Figure S1A). These results establish the magnitude of the structural deterioration caused by removing the MSA, but do not explain what structural information is lost or why this deterioration occurs.

  To identify which features were lost, we examined their retention following MSA removal and found a pronounced difference between local secondary structure and nonlocal relationships (Supplementary Figure S1B). In the novel cohort, 84% of helical residues and 71% of strand-like residues present in the full-MSA predictions were retained without the MSA. In contrast, exact β-bridge [27] retention declined to only 42%, and contact retention declined with sequence separation, reaching 45%, 40%, and 28% at short, medium, and long range. The similar cohort behaved relatively uniformly throughout (Supplementary Section S1). Note that secondary-structure retention is a per-residue measure and does not require those elements to occupy the same positions within the three-dimensional fold, so a helix may remain recognizable even where it is incorrectly placed in the global structure.

  Thus, although much of the local secondary structure predicted by AlphaFold 3 can survive MSA removal, the much greater loss of exact β-bridge partnerships and long-range contacts is indicative of the fact that the MSA is of immense importance when establishing the nonlocal relationships required to organize protein structural elements into a geometrically consistent three-dimensional fold.

  This distinction is illustrated by the representative novel protein 8R5N. Its full-MSA prediction closely reproduced the experimental structure (TM-score = 0.984), whereas removing the MSA reduced the TM-score to 0.261 (Figure 2A). Although recognizable local elements remained present, the prediction failed to recover the correct β-sheet organization, losing every exact β-bridge partnership present in the full-MSA prediction and substantially disrupting long-range residue relationships (Figure 2B). The full-MSA prediction contained 136 long-range contacts, closely matching the 131 present in the experimental structure. The no-MSA prediction contained only 40.


### Sparse restoration of MSA-enriched information recovers the global fold
  We next asked whether recovering the nonlocal relationships lost following MSA removal required the entire MSA-enriched representation or whether restoring only a portion would suffice. To investigate this, we performed activation patching at checkpoint B, immediately after the MSA module and before the Pairformer. For each protein, we obtained pair representations from both a full-MSA and a no-MSA run, and calculated their difference, denoted  ΔB=PBMSA−PBnoMSA.

  We selected residues, termed anchors, and restored their corresponding rows and columns from the full-MSA pair representation within the no-MSA state. Computation then proceeded normally through the Pairformer and diffusion module (see Methods). Critically, selecting a single anchor does not restore information associated with that residue alone, but also restores its pairwise representations with every other residue in the protein. Hence, our aforementioned intervention restores part of the broader representational context in which the selected residue exists, rather than simply inserting information about an isolated structural feature or contact.

  Structural recovery increased with the number of anchors restored, whether specified as a fixed count or as a proportion of sequence length, and under every selection strategy we tested (Supplementary Section S2). Remarkably, restoring the pair information associated with only 40 contact-selected residues recovered a median of 71% of the available experimental TM-score gap between the no-MSA and full-MSA predictions among the 328 proteins for which that gap exceeded 0.1. The improvement extended beyond global structural similarity to the nonlocal relationships most sensitive to MSA removal: in the novel cohort, long-range-contact retention rose from 28% without the MSA to 73% with 40 contact-selected anchors, relative to the full-MSA prediction. It also extended beyond the pairs directly restored, reaching those residue pairs whose representations were never explicitly enriched (Supplementary Section S2).

  The success of these sparse interventions led us to ask whether recovery depended on a particular, privileged collection of anchor residues, or whether several entirely different subsets could provide sufficient information to reconstruct the fold. To investigate this, we constructed four mutually disjoint sets of 40 residues for each protein, using both contact-based and conservation-based selection, and independently repeated the restoration intervention for each set (Supplementary Figure S1D; see Methods). Despite containing different residues, the four contact-selected sets produced comparable TM-scores of 0.80, 0.81, 0.81, and 0.82 against experiment, while the four conserved sets produced 0.78, 0.80, 0.79, and 0.80. Recovery therefore did not depend on one indispensable anchor set. The information the MSA supplies is distributed and partially redundant, and a small fraction of it suffices to rebuild the fold.


### The MSA module writes a distributed evolutionary representation that influences structural prediction
  Having shown that sparse restoration of the MSA-enriched state can causally rebuild the fold, we next asked what the MSA module actually writes into the pair representation.

  Our anchor interventions used the full-MSA minus no-MSA difference at checkpoint B, but this difference may also include contributions inherited from earlier stages. We therefore calculated the module updates within each run and defined their difference as the MSA-specific module contribution,  M=PBMSA−PAMSA−PBnoMSA−PAnoMSA,  distinct from the total checkpoint-B difference ΔB used above (see Methods). Across the 400 proteins examined, the median projection coefficient of M onto ΔB was 0.995, against 0.005 for the difference already present at checkpoint A, and the two contributions were nearly orthogonal. The MSA module writes essentially all of what distinguishes the two conditions.

  Two properties of what it writes bear on the results that follow. First, the contribution is organized simply in space, with a median effective rank [28] of 1.15, and larger updates at positions with greater evolutionary variability and alignment coverage (Supplementary Section S3). Second, and more consequentially, what matters structurally is not the strength of this contribution but its direction. Disrupting cross-column relationships by shuffling amino acids within alignment columns left the overall orientation of the representation largely intact (cosine similarity 0.938 after shuffling 99% of eligible columns) while substantially changing the resulting predictions (contact-map Jaccard index 0.740 against the unshuffled prediction). Decomposing the native and shuffled module updates into magnitude and direction and recombining them separates the two: native direction with shuffled magnitude yielded a median TM-score of 0.96 against the full-MSA prediction, identical to the fully native control, whereas native magnitude with shuffled direction yielded only 0.70, barely above the 0.68 of the fully shuffled condition. Per protein, restoring the native direction recovered a median of 1.000 of the available structural difference; by contrast, restoring the native magnitude recovered 0.011 (Supplementary Section S3).

  Representational similarity therefore need not imply functional equivalence: two representations differing by a cosine distance of only 0.06 can yield substantially different structures, because the difference lies in the directions that matter. What the MSA supplies is carried in the detailed pattern of the representation rather than its overall strength.


### The Pairformer transforms the MSA into structural geometry
  If the MSA module writes the information that rescues structure, the next question is how the Pairformer transforms that signal into a representation of geometry.

  We thus followed the pair representation through all 48 blocks of the first Pairformer pass, comparing full-MSA and no-MSA representations at the same block. This allowed us to distinguish the component that remained aligned with the original MSA-specific contribution from changes in representational form, and to examine when structural information became accessible (see Methods).

  We first decomposed the difference  PMSA(l)−PnoMSA(l)  at each block into its projection onto the original MSA-specific contribution, denoted M, and an orthogonal residual, denoted R. This allowed us to distinguish the component that remained aligned with the initial contribution from the remainder of the representation (Figure 3B; see Methods). As established in our previous analysis, the MSA-aligned component declined progressively as we passed through the Pairformer, retaining approximately 54% of its initial value by block 20 and only 8% by block 47. In contrast, the orthogonal component continued to grow and became dominant around block 25.

  We also observed increasing contact enrichment within this residual representation between blocks 20 and 40 (see Methods). Let us briefly note that the decline of the original MSA-aligned component does not necessarily mean that the MSA-encoded information has disappeared, nor does it mean that the orthogonal residual represents information independent of the MSA. Rather, the Pairformer may be transforming the original evolutionary contribution into a different representational form as it processes and enriches the pair representation. These observations demonstrate that substantial representational reorganization occurs well before an accurate structure can be generated.

  As this representation reorganizes (Figure 3C), an important question is when contact information becomes decodable. In other words, at what point do the intermediate pair representations contain information about which residues will be in proximity to one another in the final predicted structure?

  To examine this, we trained contact probes on separate proteins and evaluated their ability to identify contacts present in the final full-MSA prediction from intermediate Pairformer representations (see Methods). We evaluated both overall contact-versus-noncontact discrimination and discrimination between contacts unique to the native and shuffled predictions. For the generic probe, contact AUROC was already approximately 0.86 after block 0 and increased rapidly to approximately 0.99 by block 9. The fold-specific probe initially performed somewhat worse, but its performance converged with that of the generic probe by approximately block 15. Thus, contact information was already substantially decodable during the earliest Pairformer computations, and its accessibility improved considerably over the subsequent blocks.

  However, high contact AUROC does not necessarily establish that the corresponding residues have been placed at the correct distances in a generated structure, nor does it establish that AlphaFold 3 itself uses this information in the same manner as our probes. Contacts are only one part of a protein structure—they do not fully define the geometric relationships required to construct a fold. These results therefore establish when contact information becomes accessible, not when the complete three-dimensional structure has been resolved.

  Next, we returned to the magnitude–direction interventions from the preceding section to examine when the structural consequences of those perturbations became apparent within the pair representation. We used the same four combinations of native and shuffled MSA-module magnitude and direction and applied our contact probe to the representation generated under each condition at every Pairformer block.

  For each protein, we identified two sets of contacts: native-only contacts, which were present in the native-MSA prediction but absent from the shuffled-MSA prediction, and shuffled-only contacts, which were present in the shuffled prediction but absent from the native prediction. We then measured how often the probe assigned a higher score to a native-only contact than to a shuffled-only contact. We matched the compared residue pairs by sequence separation to account for differences in contact-prediction difficulty (see Methods). We refer to this separation-matched ranking statistic as the control preference. A value of 0.5 indicates chance-level discrimination, while a value of 1.0 indicates that native-only contacts are always ranked higher.

  At checkpoint B, the native representation exhibited a preference of only 0.494 (Figure 3D), approximately equal to chance, despite the presence of generally decodable contact information. Following the first Pairformer block (block 0), however, preference increased to 0.714, reached 0.782 after block 1, and approached 1.0 by block 15. The four magnitude–direction conditions also allowed us to examine the contribution of representational direction to this preference. The difference in control preference between the shuffled-magnitude/native-direction and fully shuffled conditions (sc – ss) was 0.159 after block 0 and continued to rise through subsequent blocks (see Methods).

  Hence, even though the native and shuffled MSA-module contributions already contained differences capable of influencing the final structural predictions, the contacts that distinguished those predictions were not preferentially identified by our probe at checkpoint B. This distinction became apparent during the early Pairformer layers and increased substantially thereafter.


### Intermediate diffusion reveals an overcompact proto-fold
  The representational analyses above establish that the MSA-derived state is rapidly reorganized, but not when AlphaFold’s own structural decoder can turn that state into coordinates.

  To investigate this, we independently generated structures from saved representations at seven Pairformer blocks (14, 18, 24, 30, 36, 40, and 44) and two additional checkpoints (C1 and CN) by passing them through the diffusion module, and compared each against the final full-MSA prediction, CN (see Methods).

  The earliest structural predictions were overly compact and poorly organized. At block 14, the radius of gyration was only 48% of that of the final prediction, helix and exact β-bridge recovery were effectively zero, strand recovery was 2%, and global structural agreement was 6%. We call this initial state, as generated by the diffusion module, a proto-fold.

  Through the remainder of the first pass the readouts gradually expanded and recovered recognizable features, but in a strict order: helices first, then strands, and exact β-bridge partnerships last, with the global architecture resolving only near block 44 (Supplementary Section S4). This progression parallels our MSA-removal results, where recognizable local secondary structure can be retained or recovered without the precise nonlocal relationships required for the final fold. The representative protein 8R5N illustrates the same progression (Supplementary Figure S3).


### Probing the pair representation reveals an early, MSA-dependent global fold
  Because the diffusion module was trained on completed representations, however, its failure to render an early structure does not establish that the fold is absent from the representation itself.

  To distinguish between these possibilities, we examined what the representation itself contains. Because contacts are already linearly decodable from the pair representation early in the Pairformer (Figure 3C), we asked whether this decodability extends from individual contacts to the structure as a whole.

  To do so, we trained linear probes on the 200 proteins of the similar cohort to predict the final Cα−Cα distances directly from the pair representation at checkpoints A and B and after each of the 48 Pairformer blocks, with one probe per layer and separate probes for representations generated with and without the MSA (see Methods). Because probing is far less computationally expensive than diffusion, we could examine every block of the pass rather than a selected subset. We then applied each probe to the corresponding representations of the 200 held-out novel proteins and reconstructed three-dimensional structures from the decoded distances using multidimensional scaling [30, 31]. Because the final full-MSA predictions of these proteins closely reproduce their experimental structures (median TM-score 0.96), decoding toward them closely approximates decoding toward experiment.

  These reconstructions are interpolations of the global arrangement implied by the decoded distances, so their local geometry is imperfect and individual residues deviate from their final positions even in the best cases. Against a generic-chain floor of 0.22 and a residue-permutation control that drives the explained variance below zero (Supplementary Section S5), they nonetheless recover residue-specific structural information rather than generic distance statistics.

  The results were striking (Figure 4A). At checkpoint B, the fold was not yet decodable either with or without the MSA, with median TM-scores of 0.23 and 0.22, respectively, practically at the floor. By block 9, however, the median TM-score of structures decoded from the MSA-enriched representation rose to 0.51, and over half of the proteins (52%) exceeded a TM-score of 0.5. Within the first ten blocks, the representation thus reaches the global fold. Decodability continued to rise in a series of steps, reaching 0.70 by block 30 and 0.82 by block 47. The reconstructions also approached the native size from block 9 onward, with the radius of gyration reaching 95% of the final value, in contrast to the 48% observed for the diffused proto-fold at block 14. Although these structures are less refined than the diffused structures, the global fold is present roughly 27 blocks before the diffusion module can produce a structure of comparable accuracy against the same final structure, which it reaches only at block 36.

  What is equally striking is that, without the MSA, the global fold largely does not emerge in the first Pairformer forward pass. The median TM-score was 0.22 at checkpoint B, 0.24 at block 10, and only about 0.29 at block 47. No protein reached a TM-score of 0.5 by block 9, and only 14% did so by block 47, while long-range lDDT remained at or below 0.06 throughout the pass. This is not a failure of the probes. The probe trained on representations without the MSA recovered a TM-score of 0.62 at block 47 when applied to representations generated with the MSA, indicating that it learned features from which the fold can be decoded, but that these features are absent from the representation when the MSA is removed. Without the MSA, the model does eventually approach the correct global fold, but slowly and only approximately. Within the first Pairformer pass the representation almost never yields a decodable fold, ending at 0.29 against the full-MSA prediction, and the diffusion readout at the end of that pass reaches only 0.32. Only through recycling does the prediction approach the fold, reaching 0.45 against the full-MSA prediction and 0.49 against experiment at CN, short of the threshold for a correct fold. With the MSA, the first pass alone reaches 0.98.

  Together, these results suggest the shortcut we have been building toward. Before the Pairformer, the contribution of the MSA is not yet apparent as a decodable fold. Within the first few blocks of the 48-block pass, it becomes so, with the Pairformer turning the MSA’s contribution into a readable global fold within ten blocks of a single pass, whereas without the MSA, the complete pass rarely does. This is particularly notable given that, in practice, the Pairformer is typically applied repeatedly through recycling, which is seemingly unnecessary for monomers with a robust MSA. The representative protein 8R5N illustrates this contrast (Figure 4B–C). Without the MSA, the reconstructed structures remain far from their final positions at every layer, whereas with the MSA, an increasing fraction of residues approaches the final arrangement from block 14 onward.

  Finally, we asked whether any property of the MSA or the structure determined which residues became decodable first. No individual statistic did so strongly: residue burial and alignment coverage showed the strongest associations, covariation and conservation were weak, and all features combined explained little more. Although the earliest-decoded residues were spatially clustered, this clustering was largely explained by their burial, providing no evidence for a distinct folding nucleus (Supplementary Section S6).


### Intermediate representations reveal convergent MSA-dependent inference trajectories
  Having established when the global fold becomes accessible, we next asked which input—the MSA or the query—controls the trajectory by which it emerges.

  In the analysis that follows, we varied two things. The first was the MSA, for which we used the two subsets A and B, each of five sequences. The two subsets were constructed to be evolutionarily divergent: every sequence in one subset shares at most 30% identity with every sequence in the other and with the query, while sequences within a subset share at most 50% identity with one another. The second was the query, which we either kept unchanged (Q), replaced with a homolog drawn from the protein’s own MSA (H), replaced with a structurally equivalent but sequentially divergent sequence designed on the query’s own backbone with ProteinMPNN [32], or replaced with a random shuffle of the query’s own residues. Every variant was folded with the same two subsets, and the resulting trajectories were examined over the same blocks (Table 1).

  The diffusion readouts are of limited use here: before about block 40 both subsets produce compact proto-fold blobs of similar shape and size, so their apparent agreement is inflated (Supplementary Section S7). The probe reconstructions are not subject to this limitation, and they reveal that, with a five-sequence subset, the query hardly matters for the speed of emergence. Q, H, and MPNN move in lockstep, remaining within approximately 0.02 of one another through block 24 and reaching comparable accuracy by the end of the pass, while the shuffled query remains at the floor throughout, as one would expect of a sequence that encodes no structure at all.

  What the MSA sets is the speed at which the final structure emerges. With the full MSA, the median TM-score of the reconstructions reaches 0.69 by block 14, whereas with a five-sequence subset it is only 0.32 at the same block and approaches comparable values only near block 40. Without the MSA, it rarely does. Five distant sequences are therefore sufficient to arrive at the global fold, but at a substantially slower rate.

  The similarity between runs is a truly key result. Changing the MSA while keeping the query separates the trajectories more than changing the query while keeping the MSA: at block 14, two subsets of the same query agree at a TM-score of 0.58, whereas the same subset decoded from two different queries agrees at 0.69 (Wilcoxon signed-rank p=6×10−11), and this gap persists to about block 36 before closing by the end of the pass. The trajectory of emergence is thus dictated more by which homologs are supplied than by which query, so long as the query encodes the same general structure in a manner that is physically sensible.

  Taken together, these analyses indicate that the MSA sets when the fold emerges and what route it takes in doing so, while the query affects whether it emerges at all and affects how accurate the final structure is. The ProteinMPNN designs are instructive in this regard: although they share their MSA with the original query, they yield the most accurate reconstructions of any perturbed condition we examined (0.75 against 0.68 at the end of the pass). We note that these sequences were designed on the query’s own full-MSA backbone, which is also the reference against which they are scored; we would speculate that, having been designed for that backbone directly, they are free of the biological constraints that make native sequences an imperfect encoding of their own structures. And without the MSA, practically nothing emerges at all, once again solidifying its importance and casting light on how powerful it is in determining the fate of a protein structure inside AlphaFold 3.


### Sequence-divergent homologs converge on a shared fold dictated by the MSA
  If the MSA conveys structural rather than strictly sequence-specific information, as the previous results suggest, proteins sharing a fold but little sequence should follow similar trajectories through the Pairformer.

  To test this, we applied the same probes, without retraining, to 98 proteins from Sabmark [33], whose groups comprise structurally related domains of very low sequence identity. Across 542 protein pairs from the same group, with a median sequence identity of 9.4% and a median of 65 structurally aligned residues, we compared the partners’ reconstructions on their aligned residues at every block, both with and without the MSA (Table 2).

  With the full MSA from structurally related proteins, the folds became decodable on approximately the same schedule as in the novel cohort, with a median TM-score of 0.56 by block 9 and 0.79 by block 47. More strikingly, the partners began converging on their shared structure early in the pass. By block 9, their reconstructions agreed at a TM-score of 0.32 on the aligned residues, compared with 0.15 for unrelated proteins. By block 47, this agreement reached 0.45, against a ceiling of 0.57 set by the agreement between their final predicted structures. The partners thus recovered a majority of their shared structural similarity within the first ten blocks and roughly 80% by the end of the first Pairformer pass, while the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) [34] of their aligned residues fell from 5.6 to 3.9 Å.

  Crucially, this relative convergence did not depend strongly on sequence identity. Partners with higher sequence identity attained more similar final structures, but when their agreement was expressed as a fraction of that ceiling, convergence was similar across all identity quartiles.

  Without the MSA, this pattern was absent. Median TM-scores remained below the fold-decoding threshold, reaching only 0.24 by block 9 and 0.27 by block 47, as in the novel cohort. More importantly, the partners showed little evidence of convergence toward their shared structure. Agreement between their reconstructions on the aligned residues exceeded the unrelated-protein baseline by at most 0.04 throughout the pass, compared with 0.17 at block 9 and 0.31 at block 47 with the MSA. Rather than falling, the RMSD of their aligned residues rose from 6.3 to roughly 8 Å. Even the partners’ final no-MSA predictions bore little resemblance to one another, agreeing at an aligned TM-score of only 0.28, approximately half the 0.57 attained with the MSA.

  These results suggest that, within the twilight zone of sequence similarity, convergence toward a shared fold is dependent on the shared structural information made accessible by the MSA. Given sequence alone, AlphaFold 3 largely fails to bring these structurally related but sequence-divergent proteins toward a common structure, suggesting that the MSA provides the necessary structural scaffold to identify structurally convergent proteins.


### The MSA supplies structure rather than evolutionary relatedness to the query
  The preceding sections each revealed something about how AlphaFold 3 converts an alignment into structure, and together they point somewhere specific. With the MSA, the global fold becomes decodable within the first ten blocks of the Pairformer. Which homologs are supplied governs the trajectory of emergence more strongly than which query is supplied, provided the query encodes a compatible structure; indeed, a sequence designed for Q’s backbone by inverse folding with ProteinMPNN [32], which never existed in nature and bears no evolutionary relationship to the alignment accompanying it, yields reconstructions more accurate than the native query does. The information is also remarkably sparse, with the MSA-enriched pair representation of only 40 residues recovering a median of 71% of the accuracy lost when the alignment is removed. And members of the same fold family converge on their shared structure early in the pass, at a rate independent of how much sequence they share, but only when an alignment is present at all.

  What these observations suggest is that the value of an alignment may lie not in its evolutionary relationship to the query, but in the structural constraints it encodes. If that is so, then an alignment assembled for an entirely different protein should serve, provided its columns are placed at the structurally equivalent positions of the target. We refer to this correspondence between the donor’s alignment columns and the target’s residues as the register of the transplanted alignment [35]. The register is what a structural alignment provides and what sequence similarity would otherwise be used to infer.

  We therefore constructed the following test. For two proteins that share a fold at twilight-zone sequence identity, we took every sequence of protein B’s alignment and wrote it onto protein A’s columns through the structural alignment between them, computed with TM-align [26]. Only the structurally aligned columns were retained; every other column of A was gapped, so that the transplanted alignment carries no information about the remainder of A’s chain. A’s own query sequence was left untouched and remains the first row throughout. Three references accompany each transplant: A’s own full alignment, which is the ceiling; A’s own alignment restricted to exactly the same aligned columns, which controls for the coverage the transplant provides; and no alignment at all, which is the floor (see Methods).

  We first applied this to the Sabmark pairs examined in the preceding section: 47 structurally similar pairs, 94 targets in both directions, drawn from the same groups examined above, but with each protein used in at most one pair so that the transplants are independent, sharing a median of 9.4% sequence identity. The result is remarkable. Without an alignment, these proteins reach a median TM-score of 0.442 against experiment. Given only the structurally aligned columns of a different protein’s alignment, with every other column gapped and no sequence in the alignment sharing more than 19.5% identity with the query, they reach 0.717 p=7×10−9. Supplying the target’s own alignment over the same columns yields 0.836, and its complete alignment 0.951. Extending the transplant to fill A’s unaligned columns with the donor’s intervening residues adds little (0.743), confirming that the effect resides in the structurally corresponding columns rather than in the amount of donor sequence supplied. All of this holds with a single diffusion sample and no recycling.

  The nulls establish that it is the register, and not the mere presence of a deep alignment, that carries the effect (Figure 5; Supplementary Tables S7 and S9). Each null supplies the same donor alignment at the same depth, occupying the same number of columns, and alters only where those columns land. Sliding the mapping three or seven residues along the target chain yields 0.425 and 0.397; scrambling it, so that the donor’s columns land on a random permutation of the target’s aligned columns, yields 0.399. All three fall at or below the no-alignment floor, several of them significantly so. A further ablation makes the same point from the opposite direction: overwriting only the structurally aligned columns of the target’s own working alignment with residues drawn from a structurally irrelevant region of the donor reduces it from 0.836 to 0.519. Gapping those same columns, rather than corrupting them, yields 0.570, so the loss is not merely the loss of coverage. Those columns are where the structural information resides, and a wrong answer placed in them is worse than no answer at all. Remaining nulls are reported in the Supplementary Information.

  Because every Sabmark protein predates AlphaFold 3’s training cutoff and may therefore have been encountered during training, we repeated the experiment on proteins that could not have been. From the novel cohort we identified, by all-against-all structural alignment, 19 pairs of proteins sharing a fold (TM-score ≥ 0.5) at no more than 30% sequence identity and with substantial β content, giving 38 targets (see Methods). The same pattern appears. Without an alignment these proteins reach a TM-score of 0.568; given the partner’s aligned columns alone they reach 0.762, against 0.884 for their own alignment over the same columns. The nulls behave as before, with the register-shifted and scrambled conditions at 0.538 and 0.456 and the corrupted-column ablation at 0.479, all at or below the floor. Training exposure therefore does not explain the effect. If anything the novel proteins are easier without an alignment, and the transplant improves them nonetheless.

  The emergence trajectories confirm that a transplanted alignment enters the same early pathway as the protein’s own. Where the structural alignment covers most of the chain, the transplant is nearly indistinguishable from the target’s own alignment through the first twenty-four blocks (0.433 against 0.456 at block 9, and 0.518 against 0.516 at block 24) before falling behind as the pass proceeds. The nulls, by contrast, remain flat from the first block to the last, never rising above the no-alignment trajectory and yielding no decodable fold in any protein before block 27. They do not produce a delayed fold; they produce no early structural signal whatsoever. The benefit of the transplant scales with how much of the target’s fold the donor’s alignment covers, rising from a median TM-score of 0.567 in the least-covered quartile to 0.856 in the best-covered (Spearman ρ=+0.51,p=1×10−7; Supplementary Table S11). Sequence identity also correlates with transplant accuracy, but more weakly, and coverage remains the stronger predictor when the two are separated.

  We note that the probe reconstructions at block 47 fall well below the diffusion readouts of the same representations (Supplementary Tables S8–S10). This gap is larger for the transplants than for unmodified alignments, and we attribute it to the probes, which were trained on representations generated from ordinary complete alignments rather than from the heavily gapped and sequence-mismatched alignments used here. The diffusion module, which is the model’s own decoder, recovers the fold from these representations without difficulty, and it is the structures it produces that we score against experiment.

  The simplest account of these results is that what AlphaFold 3 extracts from an alignment is a description of structure. It does not require sequences related to the query; it requires sequences whose relationship to one another, and to the query’s residues, is structural. The alignment need not carry the query’s own evolutionary history at all. An alignment assembled for a different family recovers most of the fold, while that same alignment displaced by three residues performs worse than supplying nothing. What the model needs is structural information in the correct register, and when that is supplied, the fold follows.


## Discussion
  AlphaFold 3 uses the MSA as a shortcut to predict nonlocal structural features. Without the MSA, the model retains much of the predicted local secondary structure but fails to predict the long-range contacts and partnerships required to organize a correct fold. Restoring the MSA-enriched pair representations of just 40 residues—roughly 10–15% of a typical chain—recovers a median of 71% of the lost experimental accuracy. The necessary information in the MSA is therefore distributed, partially redundant, and remarkably sparse. It does not reside in a privileged set of residues, nor does it require the complete MSA-enriched state to attain high recovery.

  The Pairformer subsequently reorganizes this MSA-derived contribution. By block 25, a component wholly orthogonal to the original MSA-aligned component dominates the internal representation. Yet this reorganization does not prevent the emergence of the fold; rather, it accompanies it. Probing the pair representation directly shows that, with the MSA, the global fold becomes decodable by block 9 of the 48-block pass in aggregate.

  Without the MSA, no protein has an emergent fold by that point, and only 14% do by the end of the first Pairformer pass. The Pairformer, thus, does not merely propagate the MSA’s contribution. It rapidly transforms a compact evolutionary signal into an extractable representation of the predicted global fold, which the remainder of the pass then refines, and which the diffusion module ultimately decodes into coordinates.

  The intermediate diffusion readouts show what this transformation looks like to the model’s own structural decoder. The earliest diffusion structures are overcompact and featureless, with helices appearing before strands and exact β-bridge partnerships emerging last—in effect mirroring, within a single forward pass, the same hierarchy of MSA dependence observed when evolutionary information is removed entirely. This compaction, however, belongs to the diffusion model rather than to the representation, as the same pair representations already encode a near-native-sized global fold roughly 27 blocks before the diffusion module can render it.

  With the full MSA, the global fold emerges within roughly the first ten blocks. With small, evolutionarily diverse subsets, it takes about thirty-six blocks, even though both arrive at the correct-fold regime by the end of the first pass; the subsets primarily miss some of the finer relationships supplied by the complete alignment. The MSA also influences the initial direction of the trajectory: different sets of homologs generate distinct early emergence pathways much more readily than different query sequences do. This is consistent with previous work showing that MSA subsampling can direct AlphaFold toward alternative conformations in fold-switching proteins, but extends that observation to non-fold-switching proteins and helps localize where in the computation the effect acts. When the query is instead redesigned to suit the backbone, emergence proceeds at much the same pace, but the final structure is more accurate. When the query is shuffled, by contrast, nothing emerges at all. Similarly, as we showed previously, supplying a query with an MSA belonging to a structurally different protein yields an incorrect structure [22], meaning the query and its alignment must be compatible for the shortcut to operate.

  Taken together, these observations led us to a hypothesis. If the query sequence matters chiefly by encoding a structure, if different sets of homologs separate the trajectory more than different queries do, and if members of a fold family converge only when an alignment is present, then what the model draws from the alignment may not be the evolutionary relationship between those sequences and the query at all, but the structure that relationship happens to encode. If that were so, an alignment assembled for an entirely different protein should serve just as well, provided the two proteins share a fold and the alignment is supplied at the target’s structurally corresponding residues.

  It does. Supplying a protein with only the structurally aligned columns of a different protein’s alignment, with every other column gapped, raises the median TM-score against experiment from 0.442 to 0.717 in Sabmark pairs sharing 9% sequence identity, and from 0.568 to 0.762 in post-cutoff proteins that could not have been seen during training. The same alignment displaced three residues along the chain, or scrambled across the same columns, performs worse than supplying no alignment at all. Overwriting only those columns in a protein’s own working alignment is similarly destructive.

  What the model requires is therefore structural information in the correct register. The alignment is not merely evolutionary evidence from which AlphaFold infers a fold, but a transferable description of structure that the model can read directly when supplied in the correct register.

  Our results suggest that the conventional reading of AlphaFold’s success, that the model has largely solved structure prediction and that the alignment is a helpful but secondary input, therefore has it backwards. The alignment does not seem to be a hint that the model exploits. Rather, it is where the fold comes from. Without it, the model does not merely do worse—it fails to arrive at a global fold at all within a complete Pairformer pass, and even ten rounds of recycling leave it short of the threshold for a correct fold [22, 25]. With it, the model need not search. It does not propose an arrangement of secondary structure and test whether the arrangement is consistent. It reads the arrangement from its input and spends the remainder of the computation refining what it was given. Seen this way the behavior is not surprising. A model trained to minimize structural error, given inputs that reliably encode the answer, has no reason to learn to derive from sequence what it can read from an alignment.

  This is the titular shortcut: not a shortcut from evolutionary history to structure, but a shortcut from the structure already latent in an alignment to the structure the model outputs. It is extraordinarily effective, and nothing in these results diminishes that. But its effectiveness is also the reason the problem is not closed. Frontier models such as ESMFold2 [36] and OpenDDE [37] still perform best when supplied with evolutionary information, which suggests that the shortcut has not been superseded so much as inherited. Until a model can recover protein structure confidently and accurately from sequence alone, without an input that already encodes much of the fold, we do not believe the underlying problem has been solved.

  If the model is indeed reading structure from the alignment, the finding also changes what one might do with that fact. Alignment depth has long been treated as a fundamental constraint, and orphan proteins, designed sequences, and fast-evolving families are hard cases because few homologs can be found. Our results suggest the constraint is structural rather than evolutionary, meaning that the provided alignment need not be the protein’s own evolutionary history but some other protein’s with a similar fold. Where a structural hypothesis exists—from a biophysical argument, a partial model, or a low-resolution experiment—it may be possible to construct an alignment that conveys it. The same logic applies to conformational heterogeneity. MSA subsampling is currently used to coax alternative conformations from the model by removing sequences and observing what changes [38, 39]. If these results generalize, one might instead supply, at the regions expected to switch, the aligned columns of a protein adopting the alternative fold. We have not attempted either here, and both would require care that our experiments do not supply. But they follow from the mechanism, and they are not available under the view that the alignment’s value lies in its evolutionary relationship to the query.

  These findings also carry a lesson beyond structure prediction. Predictive accuracy alone does not reveal the computation that produces it, and a model may achieve accurate outputs by exploiting information already present in its inputs rather than by reproducing the process it is used to study. Causal interventions on internal representations, together with readouts of intermediate states, make it possible to distinguish the two, and we expect them to be equally informative for other biological models whose accuracy has outpaced our understanding of how it is achieved.


## Methods

### Protein cohorts
  In this study, we examined two cohorts of 200 monomeric proteins each, assembled in our previous work [22]. Proteins in the novel cohort had no detectable sequence match exceeding 40% identity to any sequence deposited in the PDB before September 30, 2021, the cutoff date for structures potentially available during AlphaFold 3 training. Proteins in the similar cohort exceeded this threshold for at least one such sequence. All proteins examined in this study were deposited after the cutoff date. Thus, the two cohorts differ in their degree of sequence similarity to proteins whose structures may have been available during training, allowing us to examine whether this distinction influences AlphaFold 3’s predictions (see our previous work for cohort construction).


### AlphaFold 3 predictions
  All predictions in this study were generated using AlphaFold 3 [2], with the forward pass modified to allow the extraction of internal representations. We introduced hooks to save these representations at selected checkpoints throughout the model, without modifying the underlying architecture or the computations performed during structure prediction. Full-MSA predictions used the complete alignment generated by the standard AlphaFold 3 data pipeline, while no-MSA predictions used an alignment containing only the query sequence. The number of recycling iterations and diffusion samples varied by experiment, as specified in the corresponding Methods subsections. To isolate the influence of the MSA alone, no structural templates were used.

  We augmented the forward pass to save the pair representation P∈RN×N×128 and the single representation S∈RN×ds at checkpoints A and B, and following each complete Pairformer pass from C1 through CN. Checkpoint A was extracted following sequence initialization and before the MSA module, while checkpoint B was extracted immediately after the MSA module and before the Pairformer. The Pairformer comprises 48 blocks, numbered 0 through 47 throughout this paper [2]. The checkpoints C1 through CN refer to representations extracted after complete Pairformer passes, rather than individual blocks. In particular, C1 corresponds to the representation following block 47 of the first Pairformer pass.

  In addition, we extracted representations following each of the 48 blocks during the first Pairformer pass for our representational analyses. For the intermediate diffusion experiments, we selected seven of these blocks (14, 18, 24, 30, 36, 40, and 44) and independently generated structural predictions from their saved representations. Together with C1 and CN, this provided nine structural checkpoints per protein under the full-MSA condition. For the corresponding no-MSA analysis, we examined blocks 18, 24, 30, 36, 40, and 44, together with C1 and CN, giving eight checkpoints per protein.


### Structural comparison metrics
  Structural comparisons involving structures generated by AlphaFold 3 were performed using OpenStructure (OST) [40]. Unless otherwise stated, the reference was the experimental structure deposited in the PDB; where comparisons were instead made against the full-MSA prediction or another reference structure, this is specified in the corresponding Methods section, Results section, or figure caption. Comparisons involving structures reconstructed from probe-decoded distances, whose residues correspond to the reference by position rather than by sequence alignment, used the TM-score procedure defined in Section 4.16.


#### Contacts.
  A residue pair (i,j) was classified as a contact if its Cα−Cα distance in the reference structure was less than 8 Å. Pairs separated by fewer than six residues in the sequence were excluded as trivial neighbors [22, 41]. The remaining contacts were partitioned according to their sequence separation into short-range (6≤|i−j|<12), medium-range (12≤|i−j|<24), and long-range (|i−j|≥24) contacts. Contact retention was calculated as the fraction of reference contacts preserved in the evaluated prediction. The contact probes described below used Cβ distances, with Cα used for glycine, which lacks a Cβ atom. Cβ provides a measure of residue proximity that also incorporates the position of the side chain. Apart from the contact probes and the category-specific recovery analysis, all contact comparisons used Cα exclusively.


#### Secondary structure.
  Secondary-structure assignments were computed using DSSP [42, 43], implemented through mkdssp [42], which was applied independently to each structure. Chain identifiers longer than one character, as produced by AlphaFold 3, were first renamed to single characters. Helices comprised residues assigned H, G, or I, while strands comprised residues assigned E or B [42, 44]. Retention was calculated as the fraction of residues assigned to a given class in the reference structure that retained the same classification in the evaluated prediction. This is a per-residue comparison and does not require the corresponding structural elements to occupy the same three-dimensional positions.


#### Exact β-bridge partnerships.
  A β-bridge is a specific hydrogen-bonding pattern between two residues, as defined by DSSP [27, 42, 45]. We identified all bridge partnerships in the reference structure and calculated the fraction for which the same residue pair was also assigned a bridge in the evaluated prediction. This is stricter than strand retention because it requires the recovery of the correct pairing partner, rather than simply the preservation of a strand assignment.


#### Global structural agreement.
  We computed a superposition-free geometric agreement metric, defined as the fraction of all upper-triangular Cα−Cα pairwise distances in the evaluated structure that differed from their corresponding distances in the reference structure by no more than 2.0 Å. This measures the preservation of three-dimensional relationships between residue pairs without requiring structural superposition or secondary-structure assignment. Unlike TM-score, which summarizes structural similarity following alignment, this metric directly measures the fraction of pairwise distances that agree within a specified tolerance.


#### Radius of gyration.
  We computed the radius of gyration from Cα coordinates and reported it as a ratio relative to the final full-MSA prediction, CN. Values below 1.0 indicate that the evaluated structure is more compact than the final full-MSA prediction.


### Activation patching at checkpoint B
  To test whether partial restoration of the MSA-enriched pair representation could recover lost structural relationships, we performed activation patching at checkpoint B, immediately after the MSA module but before the Pairformer. For each protein, we obtained the pair representations from both a full-MSA and a no-MSA forward pass, denoted PBMSA and PBnoMSA, respectively. We then defined the MSA-enriched difference as  ΔB=PBMSA−PBnoMSA.

  Given a set of selected anchor residues, 𝒜, we constructed a patched representation by replacing the rows and columns corresponding to these residues in the no-MSA representation with their full-MSA values:  P~B,ij=PB,ijMSA,ifi∈𝒜orj∈𝒜,PB,ijnoMSA,otherwise.

  This patched representation was then passed through the Pairformer and diffusion module to generate a predicted structure. Because selecting a single anchor restores its pairwise relationships with every other residue in the protein, our intervention reintroduces the broader representational context of that residue, rather than simply restoring its isolated local properties.


### Anchor selection strategies
  Anchors were selected according to one of three strategies:


#### Contact-selected.
  Residues were ranked by the number of long-range contacts in which they participated within the full-MSA prediction, and the top k residues were selected, with ties broken arbitrarily.


#### Conservation-selected.
  Residues were ranked by increasing column entropy in the MSA, with lower entropy indicating greater sequence conservation, and the top k residues were selected [46].


#### Random.
  Residues were selected uniformly at random without replacement.

  For the disjoint-set analysis (Supplementary Figure S1D), we partitioned each ranked list into four mutually exclusive sets of 40 residues and performed the patching experiment independently for each set. The titration across anchor counts and selection strategies is described in Supplementary Methods, Section S8.


### MSA-module contribution
  We isolated the MSA-specific contribution of the MSA module by calculating the change in the pair representation between checkpoints A and B under both the full-MSA and no-MSA conditions:  UMSA=PBMSA−PAMSA,   UnoMSA=PBnoMSA−PAnoMSA.

  We then defined the MSA-specific module contribution as the difference between these two updates:  M=UMSA−UnoMSA.

  Writing the difference already present at checkpoint A as  ΔA=PAMSA−PAnoMSA,  we obtain the exact identity  ΔB=ΔA+M.

  This identity was verified numerically for every protein, with a residual below 10−6.

  To determine how much of the difference at checkpoint B was attributable to the MSA-specific module contribution, we calculated the projection coefficient:  M,ΔBΔBF2.

  The reported contribution of 99.5% corresponds to this coefficient. We also calculated the corresponding coefficient for ΔA and the cosine similarity between M and ΔA, after flattening the representations into vectors.


### Magnitude–direction decomposition
  For each residue pair (i,j), we decomposed the 128-dimensional MSA-specific module contribution, Mij, into its magnitude and unit direction:  mij=Mij2,   M^ij=MijMij2.

  We used this decomposition to characterize the spatial organization of the MSA-specific contribution. We also used the same magnitude–direction decomposition for the swap experiments described below, although those experiments decomposed the native and shuffled module updates separately rather than decomposing their difference, M.

  To examine how the magnitude was distributed across residue pairs, we formed an N×N matrix containing the magnitude of each pairwise contribution. We then computed its singular-value decomposition and calculated the effective rank [28]:  erank=exp(H),  where  H=−∑kpklogpk,pk=σk2∑jσj2.

  Here, σk denotes the kth singular value, and pk is its normalized contribution to the squared singular-value spectrum.


### Within-column MSA shuffling
  To disrupt cross-column evolutionary relationships while preserving the amino-acid composition of individual alignment columns, we independently shuffled selected MSA columns, following the protocol described in our previous study [22]. Within each selected column, we randomly permuted the amino-acid identities of homologous sequences while keeping the query sequence and all gap positions fixed. This preserved the amino-acid frequencies and gap pattern of each column while disrupting relationships between amino acids occurring together across different columns.

  We applied this perturbation at seven shuffle fractions: 10%, 20%, 40%, 70%, 90%, 95%, and 99% of the eligible columns. At each fraction, we generated a new AlphaFold 3 prediction and extracted the corresponding MSA-specific module contribution, M′.

  To quantify the representational changes caused by shuffling, we calculated the cosine similarity and relative perturbation between the native and shuffled contributions:  cosM,M′=M,M′‖M‖F‖M′‖F,   Relativeperturbation=M−M′F‖M‖F.

  These calculations were performed after flattening the representations into vectors. We also measured the Jaccard index between the Cα-based contact maps of the native and shuffled structural predictions.


### Magnitude–direction swap experiments
  To determine whether the structural consequences of the MSA-module contribution depended on its magnitude, its direction, or both, we calculated the module updates under the native and 99%-shuffled conditions:  Uc=PBc−PAc,   Us=PBs−PAs.

  Here, c denotes the native alignment and s denotes the shuffled alignment. These are the module updates within each condition, rather than the MSA-specific difference M defined above.

  We independently decomposed these two updates into magnitude and direction for each residue pair and constructed four conditions by recombining their corresponding components. For each residue pair (i,j), we defined  cc:Uc,ij2U^c,ij,nativemagnitude,nativedirection,   sc:Us,ij2U^c,ij,shuffledmagnitude,nativedirection,   cs:Uc,ij2U^s,ijnativemagnitude,shuffleddirection,   ss:Us,ij2U^s,ij,shuffledmagnitude,shuffleddirection.

  For each hybrid update, Uh, we constructed the target checkpoint-B representation by adding the hybrid to the native checkpoint-A representation:  PBtarget=PAc+Uh.

  Because computation proceeded using a no-MSA forward pass, the live representation at checkpoint B was initially PBnoMSA. We therefore added the following increment to the live representation:  Δinject=PBtarget−PBnoMSA.

  This procedure replaces the live checkpoint-B state with the intended hybrid representation. Under the control condition, the target becomes  PAc+Uc=PAc+PBc−PAc=PBc,  exactly reproducing the native checkpoint-B representation. Computation then proceeded normally through the Pairformer and diffusion module, and the resulting structures were compared against the full-MSA prediction using TM-score.

  For each protein, we defined the direction rescue as  Rdirection=Tsc−TssTcc−Tss,  and the magnitude rescue as  Rmagnitude=Tcs−TssTcc−Tss,  where T denotes the TM-score against the full-MSA prediction.

  A rescue value of 1.0 indicates that restoring the corresponding component recovers the control structural prediction to the same extent as the native contribution, while a value of 0.0 indicates no recovery relative to the shuffled condition.

  To avoid dividing by a near-zero denominator, we required  Tcc−Tss>0.1.

  Of the 200 novel proteins examined, 43 did not pass this filter and were excluded from the rescue-ratio analysis.


### MSA-module persistence across Pairformer blocks
  To track how much of the original MSA-specific module contribution remained aligned with the evolving pair representation, we projected the difference between the full-MSA and no-MSA representations at each Pairformer block onto the original contribution, M.

  For each block l, we defined the MSA-dependent component as  P~(l)=PMSA(l)−PnoMSA(l).

  Both representations were extracted at the same block, allowing us to isolate the difference between the two conditions at that depth. We then calculated the projection coefficient  projM(l)=P~(l),M‖M‖F2.

  This scalar measures how much of the original contribution remains aligned with the evolving representation. These values are projection coefficients, rather than normalized component magnitudes.

  We further decomposed the representation into the component aligned with M and an orthogonal residual:  PM(l)=projM(l)M,   R(l)=P~(l)−PM(l).

  To compare the relative magnitudes of these components, we calculated  PM(l)FP~(l)FandR(l)FP~(l)F.

  These normalized component magnitudes are distinct from the projection coefficient itself.


### Contact probes across Pairformer blocks
  To determine when contact information became decodable from the pair representation, we trained ridge-regression [47] probes and evaluated them at each of the 48 Pairformer blocks and checkpoint B, giving 49 probes in total.


#### Training.
  Each probe was a standardized ridge-regression classifier operating on the 128-dimensional pairwise feature vector Pij. Features were standardized to zero mean and unit variance, and the regression was fitted with a regularization parameter of λ=10.0.

  We assigned a binary target of +1 to residue pairs with a Cβ−Cβ distance below 8 Å, using Cα for glycine, and −1 to pairs with a distance of at least 10 Å. Pairs between 8 and 10 Å were excluded to separate the positive and negative classes more clearly. Only long-range residue pairs, with sequence separation of at least 24 residues, were included.

  For probe training, we used 100 similar-cohort proteins and reserved another 40 for generic evaluation. All 200 novel-cohort proteins were excluded from both sets and reserved for the shuffling and hybrid analyses. The three groups were mutually disjoint, and the same trained probe weights were used for all subsequent evaluations.

  Performance was measured using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) [29].


#### Native-versus-shuffled contact preference.
  We then applied the trained probes, without refitting them, to the four magnitude–direction conditions at each Pairformer block.

  For each protein, we identified native-only contacts, which were present in the native prediction but absent from the shuffled prediction, and shuffled-only contacts, which were present in the shuffled prediction but absent from the native prediction.

  We measured how frequently the probe assigned a higher contact score to a native-only pair than to a shuffled-only pair, matching the compared pairs by sequence separation within a tolerance of four residues.

  We refer to this separation-matched ranking statistic as the control preference. A value of 0.5 corresponds to chance-level discrimination, while a value of 1.0 indicates that native-only contacts were always ranked higher. Ties were assigned a value of 0.5.


#### Generic versus fold-specific contact evaluation.
  Generic AUROC measured contact versus non-contact discrimination on the 40 held-out similar-cohort proteins. Fold-specific AUROC measured discrimination between contacts unique to the native and shuffled predictions on the 200 reserved novel-cohort proteins, using the same frozen probe weights and sequence-separation-matched comparisons. The hybrid-preference analysis also used these 200 novel proteins.


### Intermediate diffusion readouts
  To determine when the evolving pair representation could support accurate coordinate generation, we generated independent structural predictions from saved intermediate Pairformer states.

  At each checkpoint, we passed the saved pair and single representations to the unmodified diffusion module and generated a single diffusion sample. Under the full-MSA condition, we examined seven Pairformer blocks (14, 18, 24, 30, 36, 40, and 44), together with C1 and CN. This provided nine structural checkpoints for each of the 400 proteins.

  Under the no-MSA condition, we examined the corresponding checkpoints except for block 14, providing eight structural checkpoints for the 200 novel proteins.

  Although these representations were collected from successive stages of the same Pairformer computation, the resulting structures were generated through separate diffusion runs. They therefore represent independent structural readouts of the evolving representation, rather than successive coordinate states from a single diffusion trajectory.

  Each intermediate structure was compared against the experimental structure using TM-score. We also compared the intermediate structures against the final full-MSA prediction using the structural metrics described above.

  Because the diffusion module was trained to generate coordinates from the completed representation, its behavior on intermediate representations may differ from its behavior during a standard prediction. These intermediate structures should therefore be interpreted as readouts of the structural information accessible to the diffusion module at each checkpoint, rather than as optimized structural predictions or physical folding intermediates.


### Disjoint-MSA subsets
  To test whether the structural trajectory through the Pairformer depended on which homologs were provided, we constructed two evolutionarily independent MSA subsets for each qualifying protein in the novel cohort.

  From each protein’s full MSA, we pooled every homolog sharing ≤ 30% identity with the query and covering ≥ 50% of the query-aligned columns. Identity between any two sequences was computed over query-aligned columns, excluding positions where either sequence carried a gap; pairs sharing fewer than 20% of query columns were treated as unverifiable and rejected. From this pool we drew two disjoint subsets of five sequences each by greedy selection from a random permutation, adding a candidate to subset A only if it shared ≤ 50% identity with every sequence already in A, and to subset B only if it additionally shared ≤ 30% identity with every sequence in A. Selection was restarted with a new permutation if either subset could not be completed. Of the 200 novel proteins, 100 satisfied all constraints; across these, the maximum cross-subset identity was 0.300 and the median 0.289.

  Each protein was folded twice—once with each subset—using 0 recycles, a single diffusion sample, no templates, and intermediate saving at all 48 Pairformer blocks, with the model seed held fixed. Intermediate structures were generated from blocks 14, 18, 24, 30, 36, 40, 44, and the final output (CN) using the diffusion protocol described above.


### Query substitution
  To separate the contribution of the query sequence from that of the alignment, we replaced the query while holding the subsets fixed. Each variant was folded with both subsets under the settings above, giving four conditions per subset: the original query (Q), a natural homolog (H), a designed sequence (MPNN), and a shuffled sequence.


#### Homolog queries (H).
  From each protein’s full MSA we selected a homolog sharing 30–90% identity with the query and covering ≥ 90% of its columns, excluding any sequence belonging to subset A or B, and preferring homologs with an experimental structure in the PDB (at least 95% identity over at least 90% of their length); among the remainder we took the candidate closest to 60% identity. Each subset was then re-anchored onto the homolog: using the homolog’s own row in the query’s alignment, columns occupied by the homolog were retained as match states, columns where it carried a gap became insertions, and its own insertions became new columns. Every re-anchored row was verified to preserve its residues exactly and to contain one match state per homolog residue. Of the 100 proteins, 96 had an eligible homolog; the median identity to the query was 0.60.


#### Designed queries (MPNN).
  We used ProteinMPNN [32] to design a new sequence for each protein’s own predicted backbone, taken from its final full-MSA prediction. We generated one sequence per backbone at a sampling temperature of 0.3 with a fixed random seed, with no positions fixed, no residues omitted, and no bias applied. The resulting designs shared a median of 45% identity with the native query (range 25–57%) and, by construction, matched its length exactly.


#### Shuffled queries.
  We randomly permuted the residues of the query sequence, preserving its length and amino-acid composition while destroying any structural signal it carried. The median identity to the native query was 5%.

  Because the designed and shuffled queries preserve the length of the original, they occupy the same alignment columns, and only the query row of each subset was replaced. For the homolog queries, residues were mapped back to the original numbering through the re-anchoring described above.


### Probing the pair representation
  To determine what structural information the pair representation contains, independently of the diffusion module, we trained linear probes to decode final Cα−Cα distances from the pair representation at each stage of the first Pairformer pass. We used the representations at checkpoints A and B and after each of the 48 Pairformer blocks, giving 50 layers per protein, from runs with the full MSA and from runs with the query sequence alone.

  For each unordered residue pair (i,j) at layer ℓ, the input was the concatenation of the two directional pair embeddings and a constant term,  xij(ℓ)=Pij(ℓ)Pji(ℓ)1∈R257,  and the target was the Cα−Cα distance yij between residues i and j in the protein’s final full-MSA prediction. The target was therefore held fixed across layers, and only the representation varied. A separate ridge-regression probe was fitted at each layer by minimizing a protein-equal-weighted squared error,  ℒ=1P∑p=1P1np∑(i,j)∈pypij−xpij⊤β2+λ‖β‖2,λ=1,  where P is the number of training proteins and np is the number of residue pairs in protein p, so that longer proteins did not dominate the fit. Probes were trained exclusively on the 200 proteins of the similar cohort and evaluated exclusively on the 200 proteins of the novel cohort, so that no evaluated protein was seen during training. Two families of probes were trained: one on representations generated with the full MSA, and one on representations generated without it. Each condition was decoded by the probe trained on representations of the same type. As a control for differences in representational format, each probe was also applied to representations of the other type. Negative decoded distances were set to zero. The same full-MSA probe was applied to the disjoint-subset and query-substitution runs.


### Reconstruction and evaluation of decoded structures
  For each protein and layer, the decoded distances were assembled into a symmetric matrix Dˆ and converted into three-dimensional Cα coordinates by classical multidimensional scaling [31]. The squared distance matrix was double-centered,  B=−12JDˆ(2)J,  where J is the centering matrix, and coordinates were obtained from the eigenvectors of the three largest eigenvalues of B, scaled by the square roots of those eigenvalues. Because classical multidimensional scaling determines coordinates only up to reflection, both the reconstruction and its mirror image were evaluated, and the higher score was retained.

  Each reconstruction was compared against the final full-MSA prediction using TM-score with the residue correspondence fixed by sequence position,  TM=1L∑i=1L11+di/d02,d0=1.24(L−15)1/3−1.8,  where di is the distance between corresponding Cα atoms after superposition and L is the number of residues. The superposition was chosen to maximize TM-score, searching over superpositions seeded from contiguous fragments of the chain and refined iteratively.

  We additionally computed four measures directly from the decoded distances or reconstructions: the coefficient of determination R2 between decoded and final distances over all residue pairs; per-residue lDDT computed from the decoded distances, defined for residue i as the fraction of partners j within 15 Å in the final structure whose decoded distance lay within 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 Å of the final distance, averaged over the four thresholds, and calculated both over all partners and over long-range partners alone (|i−j|≥24); the ratio of the reconstruction’s radius of gyration to that of the final structure; and the Jaccard index between the long-range contact maps (Cα−Cα distance below 8 Å, |i−j|≥24) of the reconstruction and the final structure. A protein was considered to have a decodable fold at a given layer if its reconstruction reached a TM-score of at least 0.5 [26]. All measures were computed per protein and reported as medians across the novel cohort, with interquartile ranges. Control baselines are described in Supplementary Section S5.

  For comparison with the diffusion readouts (Section 4.12), the readouts generated with and without the MSA were evaluated against the same final full-MSA prediction, using the same TM-score procedure.


### Trajectory comparison
  Trajectories were compared in two ways. Progress toward the fold was the TM-score of each run’s probe reconstruction against the original query’s final full-MSA prediction, computed as described above; for homolog queries, residues were first mapped back to the original numbering, and the comparison was restricted to mapped positions. Similarity between runs was the TM-score between the probe reconstructions of two runs of the same protein at the same layer, with residue correspondence fixed by position, taking the mean of the two normalizations and retaining the better of the two mirror images. Runs differing in query were compared within the same MSA subset.


### Display of reconstructed structures
  For the structures shown in Figure 4B–C, each classical reconstruction was refined against its decoded distances to improve local geometry for display. Coordinates were optimized by minimizing the weighted squared difference between reconstructed and decoded distances, with weights 1/maxDˆij,3.82 so that short distances were fitted more closely, together with a harmonic restraint holding consecutive Cα atoms at 3.8 Å and a penalty on any Cα pair closer than 3.8 Å. Optimization used L-BFGS for up to 300 iterations. Each refined reconstruction, and its mirror image, was superimposed onto the final structure by the TM-score-maximizing superposition described above, and the orientation with the higher TM-score was retained. Full backbones were then rebuilt from the Cα traces with PULCHRA [48] for visualization. Residues were colored by the distance between their reconstructed and final Cα positions. This refinement was used only for display; all reported measures were computed from the unrefined classical reconstructions.


### MSA transplantation
  To test whether the structural information AlphaFold 3 extracts from an alignment depends on that alignment’s evolutionary relationship to the query, we supplied each target protein with the alignment of a different protein that shares its fold.


#### Pair selection.
  For Sabmark, structural alignments between domains of the same group were taken from the benchmark’s own correspondences. Pairs were required to share at least 30 aligned residues, and each protein was used in at most one pair. From the same 98 proteins examined above, this gave 47 pairs and 94 ordered transplants, each protein serving once as target and once as donor. No pair exceeds 19.5% sequence identity and the median is 9.4%, so no donor could be retrieved by a sequence search starting from its target. We verified this directly: across all 47 pairs, not one sequence appears in both the donor’s and the target’s alignment, from alignments averaging roughly 2,000 sequences each, and the highest identity between a donor’s query and any sequence in its target’s alignment is 27.3% in the median, against 25.2% for proteins drawn from different structural groups. By this measure the pairs are indistinguishable from unrelated proteins, and whatever structural information a transplant supplies was unavailable to the target through its own homology search. Classified against SCOP 1.65, the release on which Sabmark is built, 32 of the 47 pairs fall in different families and 15 of those in different superfamilies, where the superfamily boundary reflects a judgement about the evidence for common descent rather than a fixed threshold, so separation at that level does not exclude remote homology, and our interpretation does not rest on it.

  For the novel cohort, no such correspondences exist, so we computed all pairwise structural alignments among the 200 final full-MSA predictions with TM-align [26] and took the residue correspondence from the resulting alignment. We retained pairs with a TM-score of at least 0.5 normalized by the shorter chain, no more than 30% sequence identity over the aligned residues, alignment coverage of at least 0.5 in both partners, and a DSSP strand fraction of at least 0.15 in both, so that the folds examined were not helical bundles. Using each protein once gave 19 pairs and 38 ordered transplants, spanning 6.7–23.7% sequence identity and TM-scores of 0.50–0.69. To our knowledge, these proteins postdate SCOPe’s coverage and are unclassified, so their pairs were selected on structural similarity and sequence divergence alone, without reference to any assertion about their evolutionary relationship. Alignment coverage of a target is the number of aligned residues divided by the length of that target’s chain; for pair-level filters we used the smaller of the two partners’ coverages.


#### Transplant construction.
  Let (i,j) denote the residue pairs of the structural alignment between target A and donor B. Every sequence of B’s unpaired alignment was rewritten onto A’s columns by placing the residue at B’s column j into A’s column i; insertions, which occupy no column of A, were discarded. In the gapped transplant, every other column of A was set to a gap, so that the transplanted alignment describes only those positions whose structural correspondence is established. In the ungapped transplant, A’s unaligned columns were additionally filled from B’s intervening residues, with any surplus trimmed from the middle of each stretch. A’s own query sequence was retained unchanged as the first row of every condition, and the paired alignment was left empty throughout, including in the controls, so that all conditions rely on the unpaired alignment alone. Rows retaining fewer than ten residues after mapping were discarded.


#### Controls and ablations.
  The coverage-matched control retains A’s own alignment but gaps every column outside the aligned set, so that it supplies the same positions as the transplant from the target’s own homologs. The register-shift ablations displace the mapping k residues along A(i→i+k,k=3,7), preserving donor residue adjacency while destroying structural correspondence; the scramble ablation sends the donor’s columns to a random permutation of A’s aligned columns, destroying both. The corrupted-column ablation retains A’s own alignment everywhere except the aligned columns, which are overwritten row by row with residues drawn from structurally unaligned positions of the donor; the masked ablation gaps those same columns instead, separating the loss of correct information from the introduction of incorrect information. The donor-complement ablation supplies only the donor content falling outside the aligned columns. Each ablation supplies the same donor alignment at the same depth, and the register-shift and scramble ablations occupy the same number of columns as the transplant itself, so that they differ from it only in where those columns land.


#### Prediction and scoring.
  All transplants, controls and ablations were folded with 0 recycles, a single diffusion sample, no templates, a fixed model seed, and intermediate saving at all 48 Pairformer blocks. Final structures were compared against the experimental structure, taking the chain named by the domain identifier for Sabmark domains and the longest chain for the novel cohort, with TM-score normalized by the predicted structure so that a domain is scored within its experimental chain; they were also compared against the target’s own final full-MSA prediction. Intermediate representations were decoded with the full-MSA distance probes of Section 4.15, without retraining, and reconstructed as described in Section 4.16.


#### Contiguous-block transplant.
  As a variant of the gapped transplant, we copied only runs of at least three consecutive aligned residue pairs, discarding fragmented single-residue correspondences and preserving the donor’s own residue adjacency within each block. On the higher-coverage Sabmark pairs this produced alignments identical to the gapped transplant, since those structural alignments contain no runs shorter than three residues, and it was therefore not extended to the remaining pairs.


### Sabmark trajectories
  We applied the distance probes described above (Section 4.15), without retraining, to the 48-block pair representations of 98 Sabmark [33] proteins folded with and without their full MSAs, decoding each condition with the probe trained on representations of the same type and using each protein’s final full-MSA prediction as its reference. Residue correspondence between proteins of the same group was taken from their structural alignments. For each same-group pair with at least 30 aligned residues, we computed at every block the TM-score between the two reconstructions on their aligned residues, taking the mean of the two normalizations and retaining the better of the two mirror images, together with the Cα RMSD of the aligned residues after optimal superposition. As a ceiling, the same measures were computed between the partners’ final predicted structures, with and without the MSA. As a baseline, each protein was paired with a protein from a different group, and the two were aligned by position over the same number of residues. To relate convergence to sequence identity, we divided pairs into identity quartiles and compared both the raw agreement and the agreement expressed as a fraction of the full-MSA ceiling.


### Recycling
  Except where stated, all predictions in this study used a single Pairformer pass with no recycling and one diffusion sample, so that each analysis reflects a single forward computation. The exception is the no-MSA condition in the novel cohort, for which we additionally generated structures at every recycling iteration from C1 through C10, allowing us to establish how far repeated passes carry a prediction made without evolutionary information.


### Statistical reporting
  All reported medians, fractions, and counts were calculated across the relevant set of proteins: the 200 novel proteins, the 200 similar proteins, the 400 proteins pooled, the 100 novel proteins used in the disjoint-subset and query-substitution analyses (96 for homolog queries), the 94 Sabmark targets and 542 same-group pairs used in the fold-partner and transplant analyses, or the 38 novel-cohort targets of the transplant replication, as specified in each analysis. Measures from the two MSA subsets were pooled when reporting medians; for paired comparisons, values were averaged over the two subsets within each protein. Where a condition was run on only part of a set, the number of proteins contributing is given with the result. Shaded bands in figures—whether in the manuscript or Supplementary Information—denote interquartile ranges across proteins.

  We used Wilcoxon signed-rank tests [49] for paired comparisons between conditions within the same proteins. All statistical tests were two-sided, and the corresponding p-values are reported with the relevant results.
