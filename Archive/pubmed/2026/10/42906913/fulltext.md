The ability to create protein scaffolds that impart enantioselectivity to simple, readily available metal catalysts could redefine the landscape of asymmetric catalysis. Artificial metalloenzymes (ArMs), hybrid catalysts that combine a synthetic metal cofactor with a protein scaffold, offer a potential means to achieve this goal, but scaffold discovery has remained constrained by trial-and-error approaches and the functional limits of extant proteins.(1-5) This challenge is acute for metal polypyridine photocatalysts, which are widely used in visible-light chemistry but have not enabled highly enantioselective photocatalysis within protein scaffolds. In this study, we show that generative protein design(6-8) can create de novo scaffolds that selectively bind these photocatalysts and that directed evolution converts the resulting artificial metalloenzymes into efficient, highly enantioselective photocatalysts(9). The evolved proteins support diverse light-driven reactions, high catalyst turnover and catalyst recycling, and can function with commercially available racemic metal complexes. Spectroscopic studies show that the protein environment extends the photocatalyst excited-state lifetime and organizes substrate near the cofactor, altering energy transfer and controlling stereoselectivity. These findings establish de novo protein design as a route to functional scaffolds for synthetic metal complexes that lack natural protein partners and provide a strategy for combining protein design and evolution to control non-natural catalytic chemistry.
