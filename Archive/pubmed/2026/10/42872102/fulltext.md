# A pseudo-labeling approach for automated assignment of unlabeled proteins exceeding 100 amino acid residues


## Abstract
  In the era of AI-driven protein structure prediction, there is an urgent demand for rapid, accessible experimental approaches to validate computationally predicted structural models or to improve the accuracy of AI-based structure determination. As a key experimental technique for determining protein structures in solution, conventional biomolecular NMR spectroscopy relies heavily on proteins labeled with 13C and 15N isotopes, a requirement that entails high experimental costs, lengthy sample preparation times, and steep technical barriers, thereby limiting its broader application. Building on our previous work, we have developed a pseudo-labeling strategy for the automated resonance assignment of unlabeled proteins. Using high-field NMR spectrometers equipped with cryoprobes, we acquired natural-abundance 1H–13C and 1H–15N heteronuclear correlation spectra directly from unlabeled samples. By integrating predicted chemical shifts of HN, Hα, 13Cα, and 15N from three complementary AI tools into an iterative simulated annealing framework with an alternating scoring scheme, our method effectively resolves severe cross-peak overlap during the automated assignment process by introducing heteronuclear dimensional constraints. This approach enables reliable automated backbone resonance assignment for unlabeled proteins exceeding 100 amino acid residues. We anticipate that this labeling-free strategy will lower the technical barrier to biomolecular NMR, enabling researchers without specialized NMR expertise to employ NMR spectroscopy in fundamental life science research and drug development.


## Introduction
  The three-dimensional structure of proteins is fundamental to deciphering their biological functions and molecular mechanisms [1,2]. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, X-ray crystallography, and cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) remain the three major experimental techniques for determining protein structures [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) approaches have revolutionized protein structure prediction: models such as AlphaFold can accurately predict the folding of most proteins from sequence alone and even enable de novo design of proteins for specific functional needs [7,8].

  Despite these computational advances, experimental validation remains indispensable, and experimental methods such as NMR spectroscopy play a critical role in evaluating and improving AI-derived structural models [9,10]. Previous work has demonstrated that even for small, well-folded globular proteins, AlphaFold may produce high-confidence yet topologically incorrect structures, which can only be identified and corrected by experimentally determined NMR structures [11]. Beyond static structure validation, NMR also captures protein conformational dynamics [[12], [13], [14]]. Researchers have further shown that NMR experimental restraints can be incorporated into AI generative frameworks to produce structural ensembles consistent with experimental measurements, overcoming a key limitation of AI models, which typically output only a single dominant conformation [15].

  Among the three major techniques, solution NMR offers two prominent advantages. First, it requires only straightforward sample preparation without crystallization or vitrification [16,17], and measurements can be conducted under near-physiological buffer conditions. Second, it is well suited for investigating proteins with molecular weights below 30 kDa, a size range that remains challenging for cryo-EM to resolve [18,19]. These combined strengths make it ideal for the rapid validation of AI-predicted structures. However, for proteins with more than 50 residues, conventional solution NMR structure determination relies heavily on 15N and 13C isotope labeling [20,21]. Switching protein expression to isotope-labeled minimal media often results in low or undetectable protein yields, necessitating extensive optimization of induction conditions and medium composition [22]. The subsequent spectral assignment and structure calculation also present technical hurdles for researchers without specialized NMR expertise. These obstacles limit the widespread application of solution NMR in the life sciences and drug development. Therefore, developing fast, low-cost, and accessible NMR workflows to bridge AI prediction and experimental verification is a pressing need in structural biology research.

  In the 1980s and 1990s, researchers relied on the main chain direct (MCD) method to assign two-dimensional NMR spectra of unlabeled proteins, which was extremely time-consuming and labor-intensive [23,24]. Although several computer-assisted methods were developed during this period, they did not become routine tools, mainly because of the limited computational power and algorithmic sophistication available at the time [24,25]. Today, the development of high-field NMR spectrometers with improved resolution and sensitivity, together with rapid advances in AI, has created new opportunities to revisit this long-standing problem.

  Previously, we developed a method combining the MCD method and chemical shifts of HN and Hα predicted by AI tools to assign two-dimensional spectra of unlabeled human ubiquitin [23,26]. However, this method was only applicable to proteins less than 100 amino acid residues. Herein, we present a pseudo-labeling approach that enables spectral assignment of unlabeled proteins with over 100 amino acid residues by incorporating 15N and 13Cα chemical shifts generated by multiple AI prediction software packages.

  In our previous studies on automated assignment of two-dimensional NMR spectra of unlabeled human ubiquitin, cross-peaks in the fingerprint region (HN-Hα) of the 1H–1H correlated spectroscopy (COSY) were identified and grouped into discrete spin systems, each corresponding to one amino acid residue of target protein. For each spin system, all associated proton resonances were assigned based on correlation patterns in the 1H–1H total correlation spectroscopy (TOCSY), and corresponding nuclear Overhauser effect (NOE) cross-peaks with consistent HN chemical shifts were extracted from the 1H–1H nuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy (NOESY) to provide sequential connectivity constraints.

  Simulated annealing (SA) [27] coupled with Monte Carlo (MC) [28] sampling is a stochastic global optimization framework tailored for combinatorial optimization problems with exponentially large solution spaces, such as mapping NMR spin systems to amino acid residues along a protein sequence. In this hybrid approach, MC sampling acts as the core perturbation engine: it generates new candidate configurations by introducing random, targeted rearrangements to the current assignment state—typically by swapping the sequence positions of two unfixed spin systems to produce a novel permutation of spin system-to-residue mapping. Each newly generated permutation is then evaluated against a predefined scoring function that integrates multiple constraints, including amino acid type compatibility, NOE connectivity consistency, and chemical shift similarity.

  The overall scoring function is composed of three weighted terms, each derived from independent experimental characterization or computational prediction. The first term is the amino acid type score, generated by a combination of random forest (RF) [29] and support vector machine (SVM) [30] models trained on protein chemical shift data from the Biological Magnetic Resonance Data Bank (BMRB) database [31]. For each spin system, the combined model outputs a confidence score for multiple standard amino acid types, reflecting how well the proton chemical shift profile of the spin system matches the established statistical distribution of the corresponding residue. The second term assesses NOE connectivity consistency, extracted from the 1H–1H NOESY spectrum. This term rewards assignment configurations where sequentially adjacent spin systems exhibit characteristic sequential NOE cross-peaks, as these inter-residue proton correlations verify the spatial proximity of neighboring residues along the polypeptide backbone. The third term measures chemical shift similarity, based on HN and Hα chemical shifts predicted by the UCBShift2.0 software using solution NMR structure of human ubiquitin from Protein Data Bank [[32], [33], [34]]. Through principal component analysis (PCA) [35], this term computes the distance between the experimental HN-Hα chemical shift vector of each spin system and that predicted for each candidate residue in the reduced principal component space. Distances below the threshold of 0.5 are converted into a matching score, with smaller distances yielding higher scores to quantify the structural agreement between the spin system and its assigned residue position.

  The SA framework is employed to search for the optimal assignment that maximizes the overall scoring function over all possible mappings from spin systems to residue positions. To avoid being trapped in local maxima, the algorithm does not perform a simple greedy ascent; instead, it probabilistically accepts solutions that decrease the total score (i.e., worsen the objective) according to the Metropolis criterion [36]: a transition that lowers the score by ΔS < 0 is accepted with probability exp(ΔS/T), where ΔS denotes the change in the score decrease and T is the current temperature parameter. Better assignments that increase the total score are always accepted unconditionally. At the initial high temperature, the algorithm tolerates moderately inferior assignments, enabling broad and unbiased exploration of the entire solution space. As the temperature is gradually reduced following a predefined cooling schedule, the acceptance probability for score-decreasing moves steadily declines, progressively steering the search toward the global maximum. In each round, the algorithm performs 100 independent replicate runs. Any spin system whose assignment appears in the same position in more than 90% of these independent runs is considered highly confident and is fixed in subsequent rounds, which effectively reduces the dimensionality of the search space and accelerates convergence in later optimization cycles.

  While the method described above enables successful automated assignment of two-dimensional NMR spectra for unlabeled human ubiquitin (76 aa), its performance deteriorates markedly when applied to proteins with more than 100 amino acid residues, where the algorithm suffers from severe convergence failure. Specifically, after a few rounds of iterative refinement, no spin system is assigned to the same sequence position in over 90% of independent replicate runs, meaning that the workflow cannot advance to subsequent rounds.

  Two primary factors account for this convergence breakdown. First, for larger proteins, AI chemical shift prediction software produces considerably larger deviations for a subset of amino acid residues. These inaccuracies weaken the discriminatory power of the chemical shift similarity term in the scoring function, thereby increasing ambiguity throughout the assignment search process. Second, for proteins exceeding 100 residues, peak overlap in two-dimensional homonuclear NMR spectra becomes markedly severe. Such severe spectral overlap compromises the accuracy of spin system grouping and the reliability of extracted NOE cross-peak constraints, further amplifying the combinatorial complexity of the assignment problem and preventing the algorithm from reaching stable convergence.


## Methods
  To overcome the convergence failure and achieve fully automated two-dimensional NMR resonance assignment for unlabeled proteins exceeding 100 amino acid residues, we introduce three targeted enhancements to the previous assignment framework. First, chemical shift predictions from multiple independent AI tools are integrated to mitigate systematic prediction deviations and improve the robustness of the chemical shift similarity term in the scoring function. Second, a pseudo-labeling strategy is developed, wherein AI-predicted 13Cα and 15N chemical shifts of the target protein are incorporated into the scoring function as auxiliary dimensional constraints, thereby helping to solve assignment ambiguities stemming from severe peak overlap in homonuclear two-dimensional spectra. Third, the overall computational workflow and iterative optimization protocol are refined to improve the search efficiency and convergence stability of the simulated annealing algorithm when handling the drastically expanded combinatorial space of larger proteins. The detailed implementation of the proposed methodology is described in the following sections, using hen egg white lysozyme (HEWL) [37] as the model system to illustrate the complete workflow.


### NMR spectroscopy
  HEWL NMR samples were prepared at a final concentration of approximately 0.5 mM in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 5.0) containing 10% (v/v) D2O. All NMR experiments, including 1H–1H NOESY, 1H–1H COSY, 1H–1H TOCSY, 1H–13C heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC), and selective optimized flip-angle short transient (SOFAST) 1H–15N heteronuclear multiple quantum correlation (HMQC), were acquired on a Bruker AVANCE III HD 950 MHz spectrometer equipped with a TCI cryoprobe at 308 K [[38], [39], [40]]. Water suppression for the 1H–1H COSY spectrum was achieved using a 3-9-19 pulse sequence, whereas the 1H–1H TOCSY and 1H–1H NOESY experiments employed selective excitation combined with gradient pulses for water suppression [41,42]. Given that the protein was not isotopically labeled, the 1H–13C HSQC spectrum was acquired with 128 scans, and the SOFAST 1H–15N HMQC spectrum was acquired with 2048 scans. Both heteronuclear experiments required acquisition times of over 14 h to achieve good signal-to-noise ratios.


### Pseudo-labelling approach
  As the initial step of the assignment workflow, spin systems are extracted from the acquired NMR spectra to generate the corresponding computational input files. Specifically, HN-Hα spin systems are identified based on 1H–1H COSY and 1H–1H TOCSY correlations, Hα-Cα spin systems are identified based on 1H–13C HSQC spectra, and HN–N spin systems are confirmed using SOFAST 1H–15N HMQC spectra. Theoretically, each HN-Hα, Hα-Cα, and HN–N spin system maps to one corresponding amino acid residue of the target protein. Nevertheless, the exact residue-wise mapping remains undetermined at this stage, so all spin systems are initially assigned sequential random identifiers (1, 2, 3, 4, …) (Fig. 1).Fig. 12D 1H–1H TOCSY(A), 1H–15N SOFAST HMQC(B) and 1H–13C HSQC(C) spectra of HEWL. Peaks in the three spectra were assigned to HN-Hα, HN–N, and Hα-Cα based spin systems respectively, and labeled with random numerical identifiers.

  Backbone chemical shifts of HEWL, including HN, Hα, Cα, and N resonances of each residue, are predicted using AI-based chemical shift prediction tools. Here, UCBShift2.0 is used as a representative example to illustrate the workflow, while the full pipeline integrates predictions from UCBShift2.0, EFG-CS [43], and PLMs [44], as described later. From these predicted chemical shifts, the expected positions of HN-Hα, Hα-Cα, and HN–N cross-peaks are generated for every residue of the target protein. PCA is then applied to calculate the distance between experimentally detected cross-peaks and their predicted counterparts in the PCA-transformed space (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Heat map illustrating the distribution of pairwise distances in principal component space between experimental resonances of HN-Hα(A), HN–N(B) and Hα-Cα(C) spin systems (vertical axis) and predicted residue-specific chemical shifts of HEWL (horizontal axis). The color bar transitions from yellow to red for distances 0 to 0.5, with all distances exceeding 0.5 rendered in blue.

  To normalize chemical shift dynamic ranges and ensure dimensional comparability between each heteronuclear nucleus and its coupled proton resonance, scaling factors are applied prior to distance calculation. Specifically, 15N chemical shifts coupled with HN protons are scaled by a factor of 0.14 [45], whereas 13C chemical shifts coupled with Hα protons are scaled by a factor of 0.1.

  The computed PCA distances are further converted into matching scores according to a predefined tiered scoring scheme. For instance, an experimental HN–Hα cross-peak with a PCA distance below 0.1 from its predicted residue counterpart yields a score of 50; a distance between 0.4 and 0.5 yields a score of 10, and other intermediate distance intervals are scored accordingly; distances greater than 0.5 are regarded as non-matching and assigned a score of 0.

  To further improve the accuracy of resonance assignment, two additional AI-based tools, EFG-CS and PLMs, were employed to independently predict the positions of HN–Hα, Hα–Cα, and HN–N cross-peaks for HEWL. For each tool, the distances between experimentally observed cross-peaks and their predicted counterparts were calculated in the same PCA-transformed space, and the resulting PCA distances were converted into matching scores following the same tiered scoring scheme. The matching scores from UCBShift2.0, EFG-CS, and PLMs were then summed to generate a final consensus score. In this ensemble approach, if a given spin system is consistently matched to the same residue across multiple predictors, the corresponding scores accumulate, which effectively enhances the discriminative power of the scoring function for correct residue assignments.

  To address the spectral overlap and chemical shift degeneracy inherent to larger proteins, the assignment workflow was initiated using HN–Hα spin systems as core anchors. First, all HN–N spin systems with matching HN chemical shifts were linked to each HN–Hα spin system via their shared HN resonances; correspondingly, all Hα–Cα spin systems with matching Hα chemical shifts were associated with the same HN–Hα spin system via their common Hα resonances. Because of chemical shift degeneracy, multiple HN–N or Hα–Cα spin systems may be linked to a single HN–Hα anchor; all such linked spin systems are retained without prior discrimination. When the predicted 13Cα and 15N chemical shifts were required in the SA calculations, candidate residue matches were collected from every linked spin system, and the intersection of these candidate sets was extracted, with the matching scores for the shared candidate residues summed. If no residue was common to all three spin-system types, pairwise intersections among the candidate sets were adopted instead, with the corresponding matching scores accumulated accordingly (Fig. 3). The disambiguation is achieved entirely through this computational intersection operation: a candidate residue accumulates a high score only when it is consistently supported by predicted shifts across all three linked spin-system types (the triple-intersection region highlighted in red in Fig. 3), whereas pairwise intersections (yellow regions) serve as a fallback that still integrates evidence from two independent spin-system types. This strategy allows AI-predicted 13Cα and 15N chemical shift information to facilitate NMR resonance assignment in the computational pipeline together with predicted HN and Hα chemical shifts, without the need for isotopic 13C or 15N labeling of the target protein.Fig. 3Schematic diagram depicting the pseudo-labeling strategy. With HN-Hα spin systems as the anchor, HN–N spin systems are correlated through matched HN chemical shifts, and Hα-Cα spin systems are subsequently connected via matched Hα chemical shifts. When computing the chemical shift similarity encompassing HN, Hα, 13Cα, and 15N nuclei, the scores assigned to the same predicted residue from the three associated spin systems are aggregated(red). If no residue is common to all three spin systems, scores derived from pairwise intersections of the three spin systems are employed (yellow).


### Assignment workflow
  The overall computational workflow is illustrated in Fig. 4. The calculation started with the loading of all initial input files containing pre-extracted spin system data of three types and their combined chemical shift similarity files generated from three AI tools, followed by SA optimization that explored candidate assignment permutations between spin systems and amino acid residues of the target protein. The scoring function integrated three core components to evaluate each permutation: amino acid type compatibility of individual spin systems, the distribution pattern of NOE cross-peaks, and chemical shift similarity between experimental spin systems and the corresponding predicted residues of the target protein. The SA optimization proceeded iteratively until the assignment permutation yielding the highest global score was identified for each independent run.Fig. 4Flowchart of the iterative simulated annealing workflow for NMR spectral assignment of unlabeled proteins.

  For each iteration round, 100 parallel independent SA runs were performed. Upon completion of each round, spin systems converging to the same residue position in more than 90 out of the 100 runs were defined as confidently assigned and fixed in all subsequent iterations. The remaining ambiguous spin systems were carried forward to the next round of optimization. This iterative process continued until over 95% of all HN–Hα spin systems were successfully assigned.

  A key modification relative to our previous work is the alternating use of two complementary scoring strategies across successive iteration rounds. In odd-numbered rounds (1st, 3rd, 5th, …), the chemical shifts of Hα, HN, Cα, and N nuclei derived from the pseudo-labeling strategy were adopted, while in even-numbered rounds (2nd, 4th, 6th, …), only the HN and Hα chemical shift similarity score was incorporated into the scoring function.

  This alternating design was motivated by the heterogeneous nature of peak overlap across different spectral dimensions. Severely overlapped resonances in the homonuclear HN–Hα 2D spectrum may be well resolved along the 13C or 15N heteronuclear dimensions, and vice versa. This complementary scoring scheme effectively alleviated assignment ambiguity caused by spectral crowding: when one set of predictive features lacked sufficient discriminative power to resolve ambiguous spin system assignments, the complementary dimensional information from the alternate round provided additional resolving power to facilitate reliable residue assignment.


## Results and discussions
  After six iterative rounds of optimization, complete assignment was achieved for all 103 HN–Hα cross-peaks of HEWL. Among these assignments, 87 were consistent with the reference assignment deposited in BMRB, yielding an overall assignment accuracy of 84%.

  Building on these HN and Hα assignments and the AI-predicted 13Cα and 15N chemical shifts, the corresponding Hα–Cα cross-peaks in the 1H–13C HSQC spectrum and HN–N cross-peaks in the SOFAST 1H–15N HMQC spectrum could be further assigned, without requiring additional isotopic labeling experiments (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Final assignment of the HαHN region in1H–1H TOCSY (A), HNN region in 1H–15N SOFAST HMQC (B), and HαCα region in 1H–13C HSQC (C) spectra of HEWL, with peaks labeled by one-letter amino acid code followed by residue number.

  The generalizability of the proposed method was further evaluated on two additional unlabeled proteins: thioredoxin (TRX, 107aa) [46] and arsenate reductase (ArsC, 139aa) [47]. The accuracies of subsequent rounds, however, showed no consistent trend and varied considerably across rounds and proteins. This distinctive feature makes the first-round assignment particularly well suited for applications where a subset of high-confidence assignments is sufficient. One possible application is the validation of AI-predicted protein–ligand binding sites: researchers first use AI-based tools such as AF2BIND to predict candidate interaction residues [48], and then employ NMR chemical shift titration experiments to verify these predictions. In this workflow, confirmation is achieved when the residues undergoing significant chemical shift perturbations coincide with the AI-predicted binding site, which provides a convenient and reliable means of interaction-site cross-validation.

  To further evaluate the contribution of AI-predicted chemical shifts to assignment performance, we performed independent assignment calculations using chemical shifts predicted individually by each of the three tools —UCBShift2.0, EFG-CS, and PLMs—and compared the results with those obtained using the integrated predictions from all three tools (Table 2). In general, assignment accuracies achieved with any single predictor were markedly lower than those obtained with the integrated strategy. Moreover, the performance of individual predictors was strongly protein-dependent: PLMs achieved the highest accuracy on TRX (85%) but performed poorly on HEWL (7%), whereas EFG-CS was the best predictor for HEWL (68%) but yielded only 30% on ArsC.   Assignment errors in the integrated three-AI-tool pipeline arise primarily from two coupled effects associated with protein molecular weight. First, as the number of residues increases, spectral crowding in 2D 1H–1H NOESY spectra becomes progressively more severe. The severe peak overlap introduces substantial ambiguity into NOE-derived connectivity restraints, which serve as the primary experimental basis for establishing sequential ordering of spin systems along the polypeptide chain. This ambiguity substantially increases the probability of incorrect sequential arrangement of spin systems during the calculation process. Second, larger proteins generally contain a higher fraction of conformationally heterogeneous regions, including flexible loops, interdomain interfaces, and dynamic regions. The prediction accuracy of AI-based chemical shift predictors degrades noticeably in these regions, primarily because chemical shift data for them in the BMRB database are frequently compromised by severe spectral crowding, incomplete resonance assignments, and a greater probability of incorrect peak assignments. Thus, for larger proteins, the assignment pipeline becomes more dependent on chemical shift predictions with inherently lower accuracy, while the experimental NOE restraints become less reliable due to severe spectral overlap. This synergistic effect accounts for the observed trend that assignment accuracy decreases markedly with increasing protein molecular weight.

  The limitations in assignment accuracy described above may be addressed by incorporating three-dimensional (3D) protein structural models into the assignment workflow. Specifically, after preliminary resonance assignments are generated using the pipeline developed in this study, the assigned NOESY cross-peaks can be cross-validated against interpreting distance restraints derived from 3D structural models. An iterative refinement protocol can then be applied, in which candidate assignments are adjusted and re-evaluated over successive cycles against both chemical shift similarity scores and structure-based distance restraints, thereby maximizing assignment accuracy. This will be a direction for future improvement of our work.


## Conclusion
  This work introduces a computational strategy for protein backbone NMR resonance assignment that operates entirely on unlabeled protein samples, integrating multi-model AI chemical shift prediction with an iterative alternating-scoring simulated annealing algorithm. A core innovation of this strategy lies in its pseudo-labeling framework, which uses AI-predicted 13Cα and 15N chemical shifts to incorporate heteronuclear dimensional constraints into the assignment pipeline. Conventional backbone assignment protocols rely heavily on uniformly 15N- and 13C-labeled protein samples, which impose high experimental costs and long preparation cycles; for many protein systems, lengthy optimization of expression conditions in minimal inorganic media is required to produce isotope-labeled proteins with satisfactory yield. Our method circumvents these experimental barriers by utilizing high-field NMR spectrometers (≥800 MHz) equipped with cryoprobes to acquire natural-abundance 1H–13C and 1H–15N correlation spectra from unlabeled protein samples. By integrating these experimentally measured heteronuclear spectral data with AI-predicted 13Cα and 15N chemical shifts, we achieve multidimensional assignment constraints analogous to isotopic labeling at the computational level. By eliminating the need for isotope labeling and reducing both sample preparation and instrument acquisition time, our approach offers substantial savings in both time and financial cost compared with conventional NMR assignment workflows.

  We anticipate that the developed pipeline for unlabeled protein resonance assignment will significantly broaden the applicability of NMR spectroscopy in fundamental life science research and drug discovery. By circumventing the need for isotopic labeling and lowering the technical barrier to spectral analysis through automated assignment, this strategy enables researchers without specialized expertise in biomolecular NMR to employ NMR for a wide range of protein studies, including structural analysis, protein–ligand binding characterization, and the exploration of structure–function relationships. We further expect that this approach will extend the reach of biomolecular NMR investigations beyond dedicated structural biology laboratories, serving as a robust and cost-effective analytical tool to support advances in biochemical research, drug target validation, and other areas of the life sciences.
