# Microbial Rhodopsins: Structural–Functional Mechanisms and the Design of Optogenetic Silencing Tools—With Emphasis on Halorubrum Archaeorhodopsins


## Simple Summary
  Many microscopic organisms, such as salt-loving microbes, use light-sensitive proteins called rhodopsins to capture sunlight and turn it into usable energy. Scientists have borrowed these natural proteins to build tools that can switch brain cells on or off with light, a technique known as optogenetics. While tools that activate cells work well, tools that quiet cells have lagged behind, mainly because the natural proteins they come from have not been studied closely enough. This review examines how these light-driven proteins work, focusing on a new one called HeAR, found in microbes living in a salt lake in Inner Mongolia, China. HeAR absorbs reddish light and returns to its dark-adapted state only slowly. Both are measured properties of the purified protein; whether they make HeAR useful for gently silencing cells over long periods is a hypothesis that still has to be tested in neurons. We also describe how researchers are using computer predictions, laboratory evolution, and artificial intelligence to design better silencing tools. We state explicitly where the evidence ends: every quantitative HeAR value quoted here comes from heterologous expression in Escherichia coli and from in vitro biophysics of the purified protein. There are no electrophysiological recordings from neurons, no analysis of membrane trafficking in mammalian cells, no phototoxicity tests, no dose–response silencing curves and no head-to-head comparison with established silencing tools. HeAR is therefore presented as a protein with interesting measured properties and testable potential, not as a demonstrated silencing tool. Finally, we keep optogenetic applications that have entered clinical testing—to date all of them retinal therapies built on animal-type opsins or engineered channelrhodopsins—clearly separate from the much longer-term prospect of using newly characterized archaeorhodopsins such as HeAR.


## 1. Introduction
  Microbial rhodopsins (type I rhodopsins) are light-sensitive membrane proteins that use retinal as a covalent chromophore and adopt a seven-transmembrane α-helical (7TM) fold. They are widely distributed in archaea, bacteria, algae, and fungi and mediate diverse biological functions, including light-driven ion transport, light-gated ion conduction, and photosensory signaling [1,2]. In 1971, Oesterhelt and Stoeckenius discovered bacteriorhodopsin (BR) in the purple membrane of extremely halophilic archaea and demonstrated that it pumps protons out of the cell in response to light [3]. In 1977, Hartmann and Oesterhelt further showed that the light-driven proton gradient established by the purple membrane is sufficient to drive ATP synthesis via ATP synthase, constituting one of the simplest photosynthetic energy-conversion systems in nature [4]. BR thus became a paradigm for biological membrane energetics and was among the first membrane proteins solved at near-atomic resolution, helping to establish the field of membrane protein structural biology [5,6].

  Over the following half-century, the microbial rhodopsin family expanded rapidly. Members including the light-driven chloride pump halorhodopsin (HR) [7,8], the light-driven sodium pump (sodium-pumping rhodopsin, NaR) [9], the light-gated cation channel channelrhodopsin (ChR) [10], the natural anion channelrhodopsin (ACR) [11], the potassium-selective channelrhodopsin (kalium channelrhodopsin, KCR) [12], and the inverted-topology heliorhodopsin [13] were identified. In 2005, Boyden et al. expressed ChR2 from Chlamydomonas reinhardtii in mammalian neurons and achieved millisecond-scale optical control of neuronal excitation, marking the beginning of the optogenetics era [14,15]. Over the next decade, optogenetics was rapidly applied to neural circuit dissection, cardiac pacing, auditory research, and retinal repair [16,17,18,19].

  However, compared with excitatory tools centered on ChR2 and its engineered variants, inhibitory tools have developed more slowly. In a systematic comparison in 2010, Chow et al. noted that halide and proton pumps available at the time were limited in photosensitivity, membrane localization, and silencing efficiency [20]. Wiegert et al. and Rost et al. further summarized the application bottlenecks of inhibitory tools in demanding contexts such as presynaptic terminals [21,22]. A widely recognized but still unresolved issue is that the physicochemical properties of silencing tools—pumping rate, ion selectivity, spectral window, and phototoxicity—are directly constrained by the structural–functional features of their microbial source proteins. Therefore, the discovery and in-depth characterization of new natural rhodopsins remain an essential source of tool innovation.

  The salt lakes of northern China harbor abundant halophilic archaeal resources. Since 2015, the authors’ team has carried out systematic resource mining of halophilic archaea from Ejinoor Salt Lake in Inner Mongolia and has identified a series of microbial rhodopsins from multiple Halorubrum strains [23]. On this basis, a novel archaeorhodopsin, HeAR, encoded by a strain highly homologous to H. ejinorense was recently subjected to systematic expression profiling and functional characterization. This work provided a structural–functional dataset covering absorption spectra, photocycle kinetics, counterion proton affinity, retinal isomerization, and energy-coupling efficiency [24], offering direct experimental evidence for evaluating its potential as an optogenetic tool. Because part of that evidence base comes from the authors’ own work on haloarchaea isolated from Ejinoor Salt Lake, we declare this emphasis explicitly and return to it in Section 4, where HeAR is compared critically with AR3 and AR4 rather than presented as a privileged case.

  This review focuses on ion-pumping archaeorhodopsins. It first outlines the family classification and structural basis of microbial rhodopsins, then discusses photocycle and ion-pumping mechanisms, and further compares the diversity of Halorubrum archaeorhodopsins and the structural–functional features of HeAR. Subsequently, it reviews the evolution and representative applications of optogenetic silencing tools according to their silencing mechanisms, and finally discusses structure-guided rational design strategies and current challenges. The aim is to provide a comparative parameter framework for researchers working on microbial rhodopsins and optogenetic tool development. Throughout, animal (type II) opsins, non-rhodopsin photosensory proteins and device-level solutions appear only as explicitly labeled benchmarks; examples representing genuine microbial rhodopsin strategies are always identified as such.


### Literature Selection
  This paper is a narrative review, not a systematic one, and the way its literature was selected should therefore be stated explicitly. Candidate papers were identified in PubMed, the Web of Science Core Collection and Google Scholar using combinations of the terms “microbial rhodopsin”, “archaeorhodopsin”, “bacteriorhodopsin”, “proteorhodopsin”, “retinal”, “photocycle”, “optogenetics”, “neural silencing”, “cryo-EM”, “XFEL”, “directed evolution” and “machine learning”, with no lower date limit so that foundational work from the 1970s onwards remained eligible. Records were kept if they were peer-reviewed primary articles or authoritative reviews reporting quantitative structural, spectroscopic, kinetic or optogenetic data on microbial rhodopsins, or quantitative comparisons between optogenetic tools. Recent major reviews and tool-comparison studies were deliberately prioritized, because they allow individual parameter values to be cross-checked, together with the primary reports on which each number rests. Work on animal-type opsins, non-rhodopsin photosensory proteins and device-level optogenetics was consulted only where a benchmark was needed, and such examples are labeled as such wherever they appear. Two limitations follow directly from this design and are stated here rather than deferred to the discussion: screening was performed at the title and abstract level, so relevant work will have been missed, and because no predefined protocol was registered, the review carries no quantitative synthesis of its own. Every reference added during revision was checked against its PubMed record, and DOIs are given in the reference list.


## 2. Microbial Rhodopsin Family: Classification, Structure, and Evolution

### 2.1. Functional Classification: Four Major Groups
  According to their biological output after photoactivation, microbial rhodopsins can be broadly divided into four groups: ion pumps, ion channels, photosensors, and enzyme rhodopsins (Figure 1). Ion-pumping members are subdivided by transported ion and by the direction in which the substrate is moved. Outward pumps export their substrate and are represented by the proton pump BR [3,25,26] and the sodium pump KR2 [9,27]. Inward pumps import their substrate and are represented by the chloride pumps: halorhodopsin (HR) carries Cl− into the cell [7,28], and halorhodopsin-like Cl− pumps are also encoded by several marine bacteria [29]. Transport direction is not, however, fixed by the ion that is transported. Xenorhodopsins, exemplified by the rhodopsin of the deep-ocean bacterium Parvularcula oceani, are natural light-driven inward H+ pumps that keep the canonical seven-transmembrane fold; inward direction is set by the strength of the electrostatic interaction between the primary proton donor and its acceptor rather than by a different overall architecture [30]. Schizorhodopsins, discovered in Asgard archaea, form a second inward H+-pumping family: their crystal structure showed that the cytoplasmic halves of helices 2, 6 and 7 are shortened so that the proton acceptor lies close to the cytosol, and identified residues responsible for color tuning [31], whereas time-resolved Raman spectroscopy showed that a cis–trans thermal re-isomerization preceding Schiff-base reprotonation is common to the whole family [32]. Because an inward H+ pump moves positive charge into the cell, it depolarizes rather than hyperpolarizes the membrane, and this class is therefore not among the silencing mechanisms surveyed in Section 5. It is nevertheless retained here, because transport direction is a defining property of an ion pump, and because these natural inward pumps show that the canonical seven-transmembrane scaffold supports either direction—a point taken up again in Section 6.3. Ion-channel members include the early discovered cation channels ChR1/ChR2 [10,14], natural anion channelrhodopsins (ACRs) [11], potassium channels (KCRs) [12], sodium-selective ChRs [33], and the red-shifted channel ChRmine, which combines pump- and channel-like features [34]. Photosensory members are represented by SRII, which senses blue light and regulates phototactic behavior through a transmembrane transducer and a histidine kinase cascade [35,36]. Enzyme rhodopsins, discovered in recent years, contain C-terminal guanylate cyclase or histidine kinase domains [37,38]. In addition, heliorhodopsins identified in 2018 have a membrane orientation opposite to that of classical members, and their functions are still being resolved [13,39,40]. Extended discussion of these groups can be found in recent reviews [41,42,43,44].


### 2.2. Seven-Transmembrane Structure: Common Scaffold and Functional Sites
  All microbial rhodopsins share a 7TM α-helical bundle formed by seven-transmembrane α-helices (A–G). All-trans-retinal is covalently linked to a conserved Lys on helix G via a protonated Schiff base (PSB) and is deeply buried in the hydrophobic core of the protein [5,6,42]. High-resolution structures of BR revealed the core components of ion pumping: two negatively charged counterion residues on the extracellular side (Asp85/Asp212) stabilize the positive charge of the PSB; Asp96 on the cytoplasmic side serves as the proton donor to the Schiff base in the M state; and the Glu194/Glu204 proton-release complex near the extracellular exit completes the final proton release [6,25,44]. Structures of HR and NaR showed that ion-pumping members retain the 7TM scaffold while achieving selective transport of different substrates such as Cl− and Na+ by remodeling the ion-transport-pathway residues that line the translocation route, rather than by providing a single preformed Na+ binding site [45,46,47]. The structure of KCR revealed a potassium-selectivity mechanism independent of the canonical K+ channel selectivity filter [48]. Notably, rhodopsins from different species differ markedly in oligomeric assembly: BR forms two-dimensional lattice-like trimers [49], viral rhodopsins display unique structural elements [50], and ω-rhodopsins exhibit distinct functional module organization [51]; engineered mirror-image proteins further demonstrate the plasticity of the scaffold [52]. Oligomeric state has also been measured directly and without crystallization: high-speed atomic force microscopy combined with circular dichroism showed that archaeal rhodopsins are predominantly trimers whereas eubacterial rhodopsins are predominantly pentamers, the two classes giving inverted exciton couplets of the retinal chromophore [53]. Oligomeric assembly therefore co-varies with phylogeny and should be regarded as a design variable rather than a fixed property.


### 2.3. Evolutionary History and Horizontal Gene Transfer
  Phylogenetic analyses indicate that microbial rhodopsins constitute an ancient protein family whose genes have undergone multiple horizontal transfers between archaea and bacteria [56,57,58]. Based on large-scale structural comparisons, Zabelskii et al. proposed that the rhodopsin superfamily can be divided into several major branches, suggesting that its functional diversity may far exceed current understanding [59]. Kojima and Sudo argued, from a photochemical perspective, that animal type II rhodopsins and microbial type I rhodopsins underwent convergent evolution [1]. Rhodopsin gene content is ecologically structured in hypersaline and marine systems. Environmental PCR surveys across a single solar saltern recovered several distinct bacteriorhodopsin-like lineages whose distribution tracked the salinity gradient rather than the taxonomy of the host [60], and a synthesis of bacterial and archaeal ion-pumping rhodopsins showed that different pump types support different light-dependent fitness strategies and display clear depth- and light-related distribution patterns at sea [61]. These field data are consistent with, but do not by themselves demonstrate, the specific proposition that pump-type and sensory-type rhodopsin genes are retained under different light regimes within haloarchaea; we therefore present that proposition as an interpretation to be tested rather than an established result. This evolutionary information provides an important lesson for tool design: natural diversity itself can be regarded as a “sequence–function mapping library” awaiting exploitation [62].


## 3. Photocycle Dynamics and Ion-Pumping Mechanisms

### 3.1. General Picture of the Photocycle
  After photoexcitation, rhodopsins generally complete a photocycle in the order K→L→M→N→O→recovery. All-trans-retinal isomerizes to 13-cis within subpicoseconds (K state), followed by protein backbone relaxation that drives formation of the L state. The Schiff base transfers a proton to the extracellular counterion residue, forming the deprotonated M state. Subsequently, a cytoplasmic proton donor reprotonates the Schiff base (M→N), and retinal re-isomerizes to all-trans in the O state, releasing the substrate ion [25,44,49]. The rate-limiting steps of pumping turnover usually lie in M/N decay and dark adaptation [63]. Time-resolved UV-Vis spectroscopy, transient infrared spectroscopy, and solid-state NMR provide complementary observation windows for resolving these intermediates [64,65,66].


### 3.2. Chromophore Isomerization and Dark/Light Adaptation
  Dark-adapted rhodopsins typically contain all-trans and 13-cis chromophore isomers in a characteristic ratio. Classical HPLC analysis showed that the all-trans:13-cis ratios of BR in the dark-adapted and light-adapted states are approximately 1:1 and 6:1, respectively [67]. Measurements on HeAR showed all-trans:13-cis ratios of 2:1 and 6:1 in the dark-adapted and light-adapted states, respectively, indicating a lower enrichment of the 13-cis isomer in the dark state than in BR [24]. More notably, the dark-adaptation half-time of HeAR is as long as 160 min, approximately twice that of BR (80 min). Two distinct timescales must not be conflated. “Slow recovery” in what follows refers to light–dark adaptation, i.e., the re-equilibration between the all-trans and 13-cis chromophore pools of the dark-adapted state (half-time 160 min; Figure 2C,D); the duration of one photocycle is a separate quantity, 100 ms for HeAR (Figure 2A,B). Slow dark adaptation means that a larger fraction of the population stays light-adapted between illumination episodes, but because it concerns the isomeric composition of the dark-adapted pool rather than occupancy of a photocycle intermediate, it does not by itself increase per-molecule charge turnover. The proposal that it improves low-light silencing therefore needs direct measurement; Section 4.3 and Table 1 set out what has and has not been determined.


### 3.3. Proton-Transfer Cascade and Pumping Mechanism
  As summarized in the lower panel of Figure 1, the structural chemistry of BR proton pumping has been elucidated in considerable detail: protonation and deprotonation of Asp85 are tightly regulated by its acid–base equilibrium [68]; the proton-release complex and imino acid residues jointly determine release timing [69,70]; and the water network in the cytoplasmic half-channel controls the energy barrier for proton uptake [71]. In recent years, room-temperature serial crystallography has directly captured water-network conformations during proton uptake [72], and computational studies have proposed long-range proton-transfer models such as the “proton cage” [73] and hydroxide-mediated transfer [74]. Femtosecond X-ray lasers have revealed the earliest structural responses of chloride pumping [75], and direct electrical measurements on proteoliposomes have enabled real-time quantification of transmembrane charge displacement [76]. These methodological advances provide a standardized toolbox for evaluating the mechanistic fidelity of newly identified pump-type rhodopsins.


### 3.4. Energy Coupling: From Proton Gradient to ATP Synthesis
  The physiological significance of light-driven proton pumps lies in converting light energy into a transmembrane proton electrochemical gradient, i.e., the proton motive force, which then drives ATP synthase. This principle was demonstrated in vivo in halophilic archaea as early as 1977 [4]. Gene complementation experiments showed that the intracellular pH homeostasis and swimming behavior of purple-membrane-deficient Halobacterium mutants under illumination depend directly on BR expression [77]. In heterologous expression systems, HeAR exhibits clear energy-coupling capacity (Figure 2E,F). In Escherichia coli BL21(DE3), photoactivation of HeAR drives a net proton extrusion rate of approximately 0.1 ng H+/(mg·s). In the presence of the ATP synthase inhibitor DCCD, the net extrusion rate increases to 0.3 ng H+/(mg·s) because proton leakage pathways are blocked. Meanwhile, illumination for 30 s raises the intracellular ATP level to a steady state of approximately 0.3 nmol/mg protein, whereas the dark ATP hydrolysis rate is only 0.02 nmol/(mg·s) [24]. These results indicate that HeAR is not only a subject for structural studies but may also serve as a light-energy conversion module in synthetic biology.


### 3.5. Spectral Tuning and Accessory Pigment Antennae
  The absorption wavelength of microbial rhodopsins can be finely tuned by the electrostatic environment of the PSB, the water network, and hydrogen-bond strength [78,79,80]. Xanthorhodopsin (XR) has further evolved a carotenoid accessory pigment antenna that extends absorption into the blue-green region and enables efficient energy transfer to retinal [81,82]. This antenna mechanism also exists in the cyanobacterial Gloeobacter violaceus rhodopsin [83], and its expression in diatoms can significantly increase biomass production in iron-limited surface oceans [84]. Expression optimization and thermostability engineering of XR in E. coli have laid the foundation for industrial applications [85,86], and the energy-coupling mechanism between carotenoids and retinal has been continuously refined through theoretical and experimental studies [87,88,89,90].


## 4. Diversity of Halorubrum Archaeorhodopsins and Structural–Functional Characterization of HeAR

### 4.1. Halorubrum: A Rich Source of Rhodopsins in Salt Lake Ecosystems
  Halorubrum is one of the most diverse genera of halophilic archaea, with members distributed in neutral salt lakes, alkaline salt lakes, and ancient salt mines [91,92]. Notably, in several species of this genus, rhodopsin expression is coupled with light-dependent regulation of carotenoid (bacterioruberin) synthesis, suggesting that rhodopsin-mediated photosynthetic energy metabolism may participate in the overall adaptive strategy of the cell [93,94]. Within the archaeorhodopsin (AR) family, AR1 and AR2 were first cloned from Halorubrum chaoviator (formerly Halobacterium sp. aus-1) and Halorubrum sp. aus-2, respectively [95,96]. Mutational analysis of AR2 first identified a stretch of polar residues at the end of helix E that is critical for light-driven proton pumping [96]. Lukashev et al. systematically characterized the pH-dependent spectral transitions of archaeorhodopsins [97]. AR4 (HxBR) from H. xinjiangense was cloned and functionally expressed, with the recombinant purple membrane showing light-dependent photoelectric responses [98], whereas AR3 (Arch) has become the most extensively studied archaeorhodopsin because of its broad use in optogenetics [20,99].


### 4.2. Halorubrum Resources from Ejinoor Salt Lake
  In 2015, the authors’ team isolated multiple halophilic archaeal strains from Ejinoor Salt Lake in Inner Mongolia and identified several microbial rhodopsin genes in its Halorubrum members, confirming that this salt lake is a natural enrichment site for archaeorhodopsin resources [23]. One strain showed 99.0% 16S rRNA sequence similarity to H. ejinorense, and its genome encodes three rhodopsin homologs: HeAR, HeHR, and HeSRII. In contrast, H. xinjiangense has so far been reported to express only an AR4-like protein, suggesting that different strains within the same genus may follow distinct evolutionary trajectories in phototrophic metabolism [24].


### 4.3. Structural–Functional Features of HeAR
  Sequence and phylogenetic analyses show that HeAR has the highest homology to AR4 (95%), followed by AR1 (94%), AR3 (93%), and AR2 (86%), and only 57% identity to BR. Despite this sequence divergence, Halorubrum archaeorhodopsins are strictly conserved in 11 key proton-pumping residues, including the Schiff-base binding site and residues in the proton release/uptake channels, indicating strong evolutionary constraint on the functional core [24]. Site-directed mutagenesis of the same protein is consistent with that picture: replacing the consecutive polar residues Thr164 and Ser165 at the end of helix E prolonged the overall photocycle and accelerated M decay, so residues outside the retinal pocket also set the turnover rate [100]. The comparison must, however, be read with care: within this genus HeAR differs from AR4 by only 5% of its residues and from AR3 by only 7% in the transmembrane region, so properties highlighted as characteristic of HeAR are very likely to be shared, at least qualitatively, by its close relatives; a parameter-by-parameter assessment of what is genuinely distinctive is given in Table 1. Functionally, HeAR recombinantly expressed in E. coli forms a stable 129 kDa trimer, with detergent-binding properties similar to those of BR [24]. Its absorption maximum is 550 nm in the dark-adapted state and 560 nm in the light-adapted state; the 10 nm bathochromic shift that accompanies light adaptation is a comparison within HeAR itself, and BR shows the same 10 nm shift (568 nm dark-adapted, 578 nm light-adapted) measured in parallel [24]. Relative to BR, HeAR is therefore hypsochromically shifted by ≈18 nm in both adaptation states (550 vs. 568 nm; 560 vs. 578 nm). AR3 absorbs at 559 nm (pH 6, E. coli membranes) [101], i.e., ≈9 nm to the red of dark-adapted HeAR but within ≈1 nm of light-adapted HeAR; because the adaptation state was not specified for the steady-state spectrum in that study, and because pH, host membrane and solubilization conditions also differ, the two proteins should be regarded as having essentially the same absorption maximum, with no red shift in HeAR relative to AR3. The same holds against AR4: it absorbs at ≈565 nm in the light-adapted claret membrane, and the homologous protein of Halorubrum xinjiangense BD-1ᵀ expressed in Escherichia coli forms a purple membrane with λmax = 555 nm, so HeAR is again slightly to the blue of its closest relative rather than to the red (Table 1). The apparent pKa of the counterion Asp-95 is 3.5 ± 0.1, significantly higher than that of BR Asp-85 (2.6) and of AR3 Asp-95 (2.5 ± 0.2; pH-dependent absorption, isomer composition and excited-state lifetimes in E. coli-expressed protein) [102], indicating evolutionary optimization of the electrostatic microenvironment of the retinal binding pocket [24]. AR4, however, matches it: its counterion Asp-85 titrates at pKa 3.6 (Henderson–Hasselbalch fit, n = 0.9), and every other ionizable group measured in that study is likewise higher than in BR—the proton-release complex at ≈10.4 in the ground state, ≈8.4 in M and ≈6.5 in O, and Asp-96 at ≈9.2 during the N→O transition—so a raised counterion pKa is a property that HeAR shares with AR4 and that separates both from BR and from AR3, rather than a feature peculiar to HeAR (Table 1). Kinetically, the complete photocycle of HeAR is approximately 100 ms, close to those of the Halorubrum homologs AR1/AR2 (85 ms) and slower than that of BR (11 ms). However, its M state saturates within 60 µs (BR, approximately 0.2 ms) and decays completely within 3.3 ms (BR, approximately 11 ms), meaning that both M-state formation and decay are about three times faster than in BR, whereas the overall cycle and dark-adaptation recovery are markedly prolonged. This kinetic signature holds only against BR; against AR3 it reverses. In detergent-solubilized AR3 the M state forms within < 50 µs and decays with τ ≈ 390 µs, the O state decays with τ ≈ 14 ms, and ground-state repopulation recovers with τ ≈ 32 ms [103], so HeAR’s M decay is roughly an order of magnitude slower than AR3’s and its overall cycle roughly three- to ten-fold longer. Because excitation wavelength and intensity, pH, temperature, detergent and the fitting model all differ between these studies, these ratios are indicative rather than exact. The corresponding measurements for AR4, made in the claret membrane at pH 7.1 and 20 °C, give an M decay with time constants of 0.55 and 3 ms, O formation and decay with 1.5 and 10 ms and an overall cycle of ≈10 ms—about four times faster than BR and about ten times faster than HeAR; the 565 nm maximum quoted above comes from the same study [104] and the 555 nm maximum of the Escherichia coli-expressed Halorubrum xinjiangense homolog from [98]. Detergent is not a neutral variable in such a comparison: solubilizing AR4 in Triton X-100 lowers the pKa of the proton-release complex below 7.0 and reverses the order of proton release and uptake at neutral pH [105], so the physical form in which the protein is prepared can change the very property being measured. AR4 can nevertheless be produced recombinantly in Halobacterium salinarum L33 at yields of up to 20 mg L−1 with its trimeric packing and its proton-pumping kinetics retained [106]. We deliberately avoid the term bistable here. Bistability in optogenetics denotes photo-interconversion between two thermally stable states that persists in the dark, demonstrated by direct bidirectional photoswitching; HeAR has not been shown to behave in this way. What has been measured is simply fast intermediate kinetics combined with a slow overall cycle in detergent-solubilized protein, which is a different phenomenon. Because these pumps transfer one proton per photocycle, the longer cycle lowers maximal per-molecule turnover relative to BR, so any low-light advantage must be measured rather than inferred (Table 1). Overall, of the three properties originally highlighted, only the prolonged overall photocycle sets HeAR apart from all three comparators: ≈100 ms against 11 ms for BR, ≈10 ms for AR4 [104] and an O-decay of τ ≈ 14 ms for AR3. The raised counterion pKa is shared with AR4 (3.5 ± 0.1 against 3.6 [104]) and separates both proteins from BR (2.6) and AR3 (2.5 ± 0.2), and the “red-shifted absorption” holds only against excitatory channelrhodopsins (≈470 nm): HeAR is ≈18 nm blue-shifted relative to BR, has essentially the same maximum as AR3, and is ≈5–15 nm to the blue of AR4 (Table 1).


### 4.4. Tool Development from Archaeorhodopsins: Lessons from AR3
  AR3 is currently the most widely used archaeorhodopsin tool. Structural studies have revealed that its sensitization process is closely related to disorder of the internal water network [99]. Red-shifted and near-infrared analogs based on AR3 have extended its spectral window [107]. Its excellent fluorescence properties make it an attractive template for engineered fluorescent reporters [101], and derived voltage indicators have enabled optoelectronic imaging in tissue [108,109]. Its light-driven proton extrusion has also been used to silence synaptic transmission by altering extracellular pH [110] and even to trigger tumor cell apoptosis [111]. The success of AR3 suggests that “atypical” biophysical properties of source proteins—such as fluorescence, pH effects, and slow recovery—are often precisely the source of their value as tools. ## 5. Evolution of Optogenetic Silencing Tools

### 5.1. Functional Requirements for Silencing Tools
  Optogenetic silencing requires clamping the membrane potential below threshold with millisecond temporal precision without damaging the cell. Systematic reviews have pointed out that an ideal silencing tool should simultaneously meet several requirements: high unitary conductance, low light-intensity demand, ion selectivity, and good membrane localization [19,21]. In regions highly sensitive to ion homeostasis, such as axon terminals, the efficacy and side effects of existing tools remain particularly problematic [22].


### 5.2. Light-Driven Inward Halide Pumps
  The first genetically encoded optical silencing tool was HR from Natronomonas pharaonis (NpHR), which pumps Cl− into the cell and causes hyperpolarization [113]. The 1.8 Å crystal structure of NpHR clarified the structural basis of chloride pumping [45,46], but this tool is limited by poor membrane localization and dependence on intracellular Cl− homeostasis [20]; its transport kinetics are also strictly constrained by the chloride gradient [114]. In 2010, Chow et al. developed eNpHR3.0 by adding a membrane trafficking signal sequence, greatly improving membrane localization and silencing efficiency [20].


### 5.3. Light-Driven Outward Proton Pumps
  During the same period, AR3 (Arch) from H. sodomense and ArchT from another halophilic archaeal strain were introduced into the silencing toolbox as outward proton pumps [20,115]. Proton pumps have the advantage of being driven by a single gene and requiring low light intensity; however, they cause extracellular pH shifts and consequent confounding effects on synaptic transmission [110]. The need for red-shifted and deep-tissue light delivery has also continued to drive spectral engineering [116].


### 5.4. Light-Gated Anion Channels
  Guided by ChR crystal structures, Berndt et al. converted a cation channel into a chloride-selective channel (iC1C2) by site-directed mutagenesis [117]. Klapoetke et al. further developed the high-conductance variant iC++, which eliminates residual proton currents [118]. Meanwhile, Govorunova et al. discovered natural anion channels GtACR1/2 in cryptophytes, which exhibit excellent Cl− selectivity without engineering [11]. Structure-guided channel engineering has since become a paradigm [119,120,121]. Mechanistic studies of anion channels showed that their proton-transfer pathway determines rectification properties [122]. The pump-like channel ChRmine is cited here only as a structural comparator, not as an inhibitory tool: it conducts cations and is used for excitation, but by sequence and structure it occupies an intermediate position between pumps and channels and therefore delimits what mutation within the shared seven-transmembrane bundle can achieve [34].


### 5.5. Potassium-Selective Channels, Metabotropic Strategies, and Enzyme-Coupled Rhodopsins
  K+ is a central ion in neuronal electrophysiology, but natural light-gated K+ channels remained elusive for a long time. In 2022, Govorunova et al. discovered KCRs (HcKCR1/2) in the stramenopile microorganism Hyphochytrium catenoides, a relative of filamentous fungi. These channels achieve a K+ selectivity of P(K)/P(Na) ≈ 23 through an independently evolved mechanism and mediate strong hyperpolarization within 1 ms after illumination [12]. Subsequent cryo-EM structures revealed their unique ion selectivity filter [48], providing a structural basis for engineering [123]. Earlier, strategies based on fusing natural K+ channels with light-controlled subunits had already demonstrated the feasibility of K+ conductance-based silencing [124]. Sodium-selective ChRs are also being developed [33]. Three further tools are listed for completeness, with their molecular class stated explicitly because none of them is a microbial rhodopsin ion transporter. Two are metabotropic and rely on animal-type GPCR cascades rather than on rhodopsin-mediated transmembrane charge transfer: the bistable tool optoGPCR enables multiplexed circuit silencing [125]—it is “bistable” in the strict sense, because interconversion between two thermally stable photostates was demonstrated directly, a term we do not extend to HeAR—and eOPN3, an opsin of insect origin that, when targeted to presynaptic terminals, suppresses transmitter release through the Gi/o pathway rather than by transporting protons across the membrane [126]. The third is a genuine microbial rhodopsin strategy and is therefore the most relevant comparator for this review: RoCK couples a light-activated rhodopsin-guanylyl cyclase to an engineered cGMP-gated K+ channel, so that illumination opens a potassium conductance through an enzymatic second messenger instead of through direct ion pumping [127]. This illustrates that proton-pumping archaeorhodopsins are not the only microbial rhodopsin route to silencing. Finally, the two-color bidirectional tool BiPOLES enables precise combined excitation and inhibition control [128].


### 5.6. Application Landscape
  Silencing tools have broadly empowered neuroscience and translational research. In epilepsy, optogenetic stimulation can establish an epilepsy-resistant state [129] and precisely control epileptiform activity [130,131,132,133]. In cardiology, optogenetic vagus nerve stimulation improves heart failure [134,135], and cardiac optical pacing and arrhythmia studies continue to advance [136,137,138]. In hearing research, optical stimulation of spiral ganglion neurons has paved the way for optical cochlear implants [139,140]. In pain research, sustained and reversible pain suppression has been achieved [141,142]. In sleep–wake regulation, orexinergic, REM-related, and dopaminergic circuit mechanisms have been dissected [143,144,145,146]. The most translationally promising area is vision restoration: from early proof-of-concept studies [147,148,149] to high-sensitivity visual restoration by ectopic expression of human opsins in human retina [150], and further to synthetic opsin therapy and MCO-010 therapy showing safety and visual improvement in patients with advanced retinal degeneration [151,152,153], optogenetics is accelerating toward clinical application [154,155,156,157]. Table 3 and Figure 3 systematically compare the landscape of silencing tools, including Jaws, a red-shifted cruxhalorhodopsin that noninvasively mediates transcranial inhibition of neurons deep in the brains of awake mice [158].


## 6. Structure-Guided Rational Design Strategies

### 6.1. Structure Prediction and Comparative Structural Biology
  AlphaFold has advanced single-chain structure prediction to near-experimental accuracy, significantly lowering the modeling barrier for new rhodopsins, including HeAR [159]. Visualization platforms such as ChimeraX support large-scale structural comparison [160]. High-resolution structure series represented by KR2 have revealed the molecular mechanism of sodium pumping [27,47]. Comparative studies of NaR have systematically resolved red-shifted mutations [161], the DTG motif for novel Li+/Na+ dual-substrate pumping [162], direct detection of substrate uptake and release [163], and solid-state NMR conformational analysis [164], and have revealed the functional diversity of the NaR family [165] and the applicability of nanodisc-reconstituted electrophysiology [166]. Comparable information exists for the other major functional classes and is not confined to NaR: near-atomic models of BR were among the first membrane protein structures solved [5,6]; high-resolution structures of the inward chloride pump NpHR [45,46] show how the shared bundle is adapted to an anionic substrate, while heliorhodopsin structures revealed a scaffold compatible with inverted membrane topology and an unexpected retinal orientation [13]; the potassium-selective channelrhodopsins revealed a selectivity filter unrelated to that of canonical K+ channels [48]; and structures of the pump-like cation channel ChRmine [34] show how a pump-like active site is converted into a passive conduction pathway. Cryo-electron microscopy has become central rather than exceptional in this area: the 2.0 Å ChRmine structure [34] was used directly to design variants with altered spectral and kinetic properties, establishing structure-guided engineering of a microbial opsin rather than merely its structure determination; cryo-EM of ChRmine reconstituted in lipid nanodiscs then resolved the retinal-bound and apo states and yielded variants with ten-fold slower and two-fold faster closing kinetics [167], and cryo-EM of GtCCR4 linked an outwardly bent transmembrane helix to its unusually high light sensitivity [168]. Because the stated aim is tool design, these classes are compared by what each reveals for engineering rather than ranked by the number of structures available.


### 6.2. Computational Simulation and Dynamic Structural Biology
  Molecular dynamics simulation is a core tool linking static structures to function. The continuous development of high-performance simulation frameworks such as GROMACS has made microsecond-scale simulation of complete photocycles possible [169,170]. Molecular docking supports virtual screening of chromophore analogs [171]. Structural methods are more accurately classified by X-ray source than by method name: synchrotron-based room-temperature serial crystallography and XFEL-based serial femtosecond crystallography (SFX) [75,172], together with computational studies of pathological mutations in animal-type rhodopsins [173], have enriched the analytical dimensions of dynamic structural biology. Examples taken from animal rhodopsins are retained only where the insight transfers: the extracellular proton-release domain of BR and helix rearrangements in GPCRs pose the same mechanistic question of how a seven-transmembrane bundle couples chromophore isomerization to an asymmetric output, and the transferable lesson is methodological—that retinal-pocket electrostatics can be predicted and then tested by mutagenesis—rather than one of residue identity, because the two families share no detectable sequence homology. The same logic is illustrated by atomistically designed variants of microbial opsins whose absorption was blue-shifted by up to about 100 nm and then confirmed by spectroscopy, X-ray crystallography and electrophysiology [174], a case in which computation predicted a property and experiment verified it.


### 6.3. Directed Evolution and Semi-Rational Engineering
  Directed evolution traverses the protein fitness landscape through iterative mutation and screening [175] and has yielded far-red fluorescent variants [176,177,178] and non-natural conformations such as soluble BR in the rhodopsin field [179]. Ion specificity can be switched by very few mutations: replacing the Schiff-base counterion Asp85 of BR with threonine converted this proton pump into a light-driven chloride pump, an early demonstration that a single residue sets ionic specificity [180]. The milestone case of functional inversion—converting a bacterial sensory rhodopsin into an inward proton pump [181]—demonstrates the high plasticity of the 7TM scaffold at the mechanistic level, and together with the naturally occurring inward H+ pumps described in Section 2.1 [30,31] it indicates that transport direction is itself an engineerable property.


### 6.4. Machine Learning-Assisted Design
  Data-driven methods are reshaping the paradigm of rhodopsin engineering. Bedbrook et al. used machine learning to guide ChR variant design and achieved efficient optical control at low light intensity [182]. Toolchains including big-data-based color-tuning rules and absorption-wavelength prediction models [183,184], genotype–phenotype mapping databases [62,185], and ion-pump classifiers [186] have enabled an initial inverse-design workflow in which desired spectra and functions are input and candidate sequences are output. Machine learning has also been used to optimize non-Kasha photochemical kinetics [187].


### 6.5. Functional Engineering from Structure to Application
  Structure–function information has also enabled diverse application-oriented engineering, including BR–titanium dioxide hybrid systems for photocatalytic hydrogen production [188], unidirectional electron injection in biogenic p–n junctions [189] and industrial-scale high-yield BR fermentation [190]. Three qualifications are necessary. First, the BR–lipid studies did not set out to engineer a rhodopsin: they characterized how native archaeal lipids and detergents modulate trimer dynamics and proton release/uptake [191,192]; we cite them only for the design insight they afford, which is our interpretation of their results and not their stated aim. Second, intracellular rhodopsin-based control of metabolism and cell signaling is indeed a microbial rhodopsin technology [193,194,195]; that list deliberately excludes Ref. [196], which describes optogenetic control through LOV-domain photoswitches and therefore belongs to a different protein family (see Section 5.5). Third, molecular engineering is separated from device engineering: wireless near-infrared-powered implantable devices are device-level solutions that extend the toolchain without altering the rhodopsin itself and are treated in Section 7.2 [197].


### 6.6. Implications of HeAR for Silencing Tool Design
  Based on the available data and progress in the field, HeAR offers at least three implications for silencing tool design. First, the coupling between M-state kinetics and silencing efficacy merits reassessment. The M-state formation (60 µs) and decay (3.3 ms) of HeAR are both about three times faster than those of BR, whereas its overall photocycle (100 ms) and dark adaptation (160 min) are markedly prolonged. Whether this combination actually enables sustained steady-state pumping at low light doses is not established: a pump moves a fixed, small number of charges per photocycle, and the prolonged overall cycle lowers the maximal per-molecule turnover, so low-light performance has to be measured rather than inferred from the kinetic signature (see Section 4.3 and Table 1) [24]. Second, the high proton affinity of Asp-95 (pKa 3.5) implies that the counterion of HeAR remains deprotonated within the physiological pH range, which may help maintain spectral and kinetic stability under extracellular environmental fluctuations [24]. Third, the 550–560 nm activation window of HeAR lies ≈80–90 nm to the red of mainstream excitatory tools (around 470 nm), providing spectral compatibility for multichannel applications [24]. This separation, and not any red shift relative to BR (568/578 nm) or AR3 (559 nm [101]), is the spectral property on which that compatibility rests: relative to BR, HeAR is blue-shifted by ≈18 nm, and relative to AR3 the two absorption maxima coincide to within a few nanometers. These properties make HeAR another archaeorhodopsin scaffold with engineering potential after the Arch family. The route is not without precedent within the genus: AR4, which is 95% identical to HeAR, has been produced recombinantly in Halobacterium salinarum L33 at yields of up to 20 mg L−1 with its trimeric packing and proton-pumping kinetics retained, and characterized in situ by two-dimensional solid-state NMR [106], so the expression, stabilization and spectroscopic characterization steps that a HeAR-based tool would require have already been demonstrated for a protein of the same genus. Future work should focus on enhancing unitary conductance and optimizing membrane targeting based on its structure.


## 7. Challenges and Perspectives

### 7.1. Phototoxicity, Chromophore Homeostasis, and Tool Stability
  Illumination inevitably introduces risks of oxidative stress and thermal damage. Intelligent illumination strategies such as closed-loop optical control can significantly reduce cumulative light dose [198]. Chromophore homeostasis is another often underestimated constraint: ChR2 degrades faster under retinal-deficient conditions [199]; intracellular accumulation of all-trans-retinal is cytotoxic and requires pharmacological protection [200]; and opsin folding efficiency can be improved by molecular chaperone strategies [201]. In addition, the expression and functional maintenance of tool proteins under physiological states such as aging require systematic evaluation [202].


### 7.2. Light Delivery and Deep-Tissue Applications
  Strong scattering and absorption of visible light by mammalian tissue limit the in vivo application of optogenetic tools. Strategies to address this include red-shifted and far-red tools [116,203], upconversion nanoparticle-mediated near-infrared activation [204,205,206], bioluminescence-driven optogenetics without external light sources [207], two-photon excitation [208], and integrated optrode–electrode recording devices [209]. Engineering innovations are also advancing rapidly in photosensitive tumor therapy [210], smooth muscle optical control [211], all-optical readout [212], voltage indicator optimization [213,214], and simplified mRNA delivery [215]. Systematic translational assessment can be found in Ref. [216]. These are device-level rather than molecular-level solutions, and we classify them as such: in photovoltaic-powered implants the near-infrared light serves only as a power source for a local blue-light emitter that in turn activates ChR2, so the rhodopsin is not itself excited by near-infrared light [197].


### 7.3. Opportunities and Barriers in Clinical Translation
  Clinical validation of optogenetics has entered a critical window. MCO-010 therapy for Stargardt disease has completed the first open-label safety and efficacy study in the United States [152]; synthetic opsin therapy has restored functional vision in patients with advanced retinal degeneration [151]; and efficient and sustained sensory and cardiac optical control elements have been validated in small-animal systems [137]. However, dose control of gene vectors, long-term immune and safety evaluation, and establishment of regulatory pathways remain practical barriers to translation [154,216]. Two features of this clinical record must be kept in view. Every construct tested in humans so far is an animal-type opsin or an engineered channelrhodopsin; no archaeorhodopsin has entered clinical testing. None of the newly characterized archaeorhodopsins discussed here, HeAR included, has been validated even in neurons. Any prospective HeAR application therefore inherits all the generic barriers above plus several additional layers of untested assumption, and cannot reasonably be placed on the same timeline as the retinal therapies described here. Optogenetic brain–computer interfaces and expanded applications in plant and Drosophila model systems offer broader possibilities [217,218,219].


### 7.4. Halophilic Archaeal Resources: An Underexploited Tool Reservoir
  Although metagenomics continues to produce new rhodopsin sequences [220], including contributions from Antarctic phototrophic ecosystems [221], flotillin-related rhodopsins carrying pigment-free binding sites [222], and the ω-family [51], functional characterization based on culturable halophilic archaea remains irreplaceable. Northern China contains numerous salt lakes that are natural enrichment sites for microbial rhodopsins. The authors’ team’s work on Ejinoor Salt Lake shows that in-depth characterization of a single strain can yield a complete structural–functional parameter set with tool value [23,24]. Combining Raman spectroscopy-based single-cell typing [223], culturomics, and functional genomics should enable large-scale discovery of new pump-type rhodopsins with unique spectral windows, salt–alkali stability, and high pumping efficiency, providing a continuous source of molecular templates for rational silencing tool design.


## 8. Conclusions
  Microbial rhodopsin research has spanned more than half a century: from the phenomenon of light-energy conversion in the purple membrane, through the chemical elucidation of the atomic structure and ion-pumping mechanism of BR, to the explosive expansion of the optogenetic toolbox. This trajectory exemplifies the classic path from basic research to technological translation. At present, the iterative frontier of silencing tools is advancing along three directions: diversification of ion selectivity, customization of spectral windows, and precision of kinetics. Each generation of tools has been enabled by a deeper understanding of the structure–function relationships of natural rhodopsins. The continued discovery and in-depth characterization of Halorubrum archaeorhodopsins—from AR3 to HeAR, systematically characterized by the authors’ team—is a vivid example of this source supply. With the integration of structure prediction, machine learning and automated functional characterization, a rational design loop “from salt lake resources to customized tools” is taking shape. Its near-term value lies in basic neuroscience, where better silencing tools are still needed; the clinical horizon remains confined to retinal optogenetic therapies based on animal-type opsins and engineered channelrhodopsins, and no archaeorhodopsin—HeAR included—currently has the neuronal validation that would be required before clinical application could be considered.
