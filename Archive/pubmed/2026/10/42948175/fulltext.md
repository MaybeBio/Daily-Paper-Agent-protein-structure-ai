# Future of cardiac diffusion magnetic resonance imaging: a bibliometric analysis


## Background
  Cardiac diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (cDMRI) has become a valuable noninvasive tool for assessing cardiac microstructure.


## Objective
  To perform a systematic and comprehensive bibliometric analysis of cDMRI to explore current status, research hotspots and potential future directions.


## Material and methods
  A systematic search of Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) and PubMed was conducted to identify articles on cDMRI published between 2005 and 2024. All retrieved records were independently screened by two reviewers to exclude the articles unrelated to the topic about cDMRI. Co-citation, collaboration networks, burst keywords, and reference analyses were performed.


## Results
  There were 359 articles searched in WoSCC and 88 articles in PubMed. Finally, 367 articles met the search criteria in both databases. The cardiac “diffusion tensor imaging” (cDTI) emerged as the most prominent keyword, with the highest occurrence count (191.0) and strongest citation bursts (6.8). The study by Nielles-Vallespin et al. showed the strongest citation burst (26.17), accumulating 163 total citations and an average of 20.38 citations per year, highlighting its substantial influence on the development of cDMRI research. The top-cited articles with the strongest citation bursts concentrated on the clinical application of cDTI, its sequence design, and cardiac electrophysiology based on cDTI. The trend topic analysis further revealed “deep learning” as an emerging research focus in cDMRI since 2022.


## Conclusions
  To our knowledge, this is the first dedicated bibliometric analysis focusing on cDMRI. By systematically analyzing the current status, research hotspots, and future trends of cDMRI, it provided valuable insights to guide future investigations in this field.


## Introduction
  Cardiac diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (cDMRI) has emerged as a promising noninvasive tool for assessing in-vivo myocardial microstructure (1–3). Its derived parameters characterize water diffusion and myocardial tissue organization, reflecting the alignment of cardiomyocytes and sheetlets. For example, apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps derived from cardiac diffusion-weighted imaging (cDWI) have demonstrated reliable performance in detecting acute myocardial edema (4, 5). cDWI can also detect myocardial fibrosis and assess its extents in various heart diseases without contrast agents (6–8). Cardiac diffusion tensor imaging (cDTI) has been used to evaluate myocardial microstructural dynamics, providing insight into cardiomyocyte organization (9–12). More recently, cDTI models have been applied to investigate cardiac electromechanics (13), analyzing cardiomyocyte strain or myofiber strain as a potential method for early detection of cardiac dysfunction (14, 15). Moreover, cDTI has been employed to simulate cardiac electrical activation, representing a novel direction in cDMRI research (16, 17). Additionally, cDMRI has enabled the measurement of cardiomyocyte size, representing a novel capability in cardiovascular MRI (18, 19). Together, these advancements demonstrate the clinical utility and promise of cDMRI.

  Bibliometrics employs both qualitative and quantitative methods to analyze large scientific datasets, effectively identifying research hotspots and trends (20, 21). In contrast to conventional narrative reviews, bibliometrics provides a more systematic and objective analysis of research topics. This study utilizes bibliometric methods to comprehensively analyze research on cDMRI, sourced from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) in the past 20 years. The analysis encompasses original research and reviews across multiple domains, including clinical applications, MRI methodology, medical image analysis, and preclinical technologies.

  The present study aimed to conduct a systematic and comprehensive bibliometric analysis of cDMRI to identify current status, research hotspots and potential future directions.

#### Article screening
  The inclusion criteria of cDMRI articles were as follows: (a) Article types, original research and reviews; (b) Publication date, from January 1, 2005 to June 30, 2024; (c) Article language, English. The exclusion criteria were as follows: The articles unrelated to cDMRI primarily consisted of studies that performed diffusion MRI examinations on participants with cardiac diseases, but for organs other than the heart (e.g., the brain).

  All downloaded records were independently screened by two reviewers to exclude the articles unrelated to cDMRI. Any disagreements were resolved by a third independent reviewer. Duplicate articles retrieved from both databases were identified and manually removed by the two reviewers. As all data were directly retrieved from the databases, no ethical approval was required for this study.


### Data analysis and visualization
  Bibliometric analysis and visualization were performed using Citespace 6.2.R6, VOSviewer 1.6.20, and the R package “bibliometrix”(version 4.4.1) (https://www.bibliometrix.org).

  The bursts of keywords and references were using Citespace with the parameters configured as follows: (a) Time slice from 2005 to 2024 with each slice representing one year; (b) Selection criteria defined as Top N = 25; (c) Pruning methods performed using the pathfinder and pruning sliced networks (22). Additionally, VOSviewer was employed to construct and analyze knowledge maps, facilitating the identification of collaborative networks. The R package “bibliometrix” was used for visualizing topic trends in cDMRI.

### Analysis of keywords and trend topics
  In terms of co-occurrence keyword clustering, it could be roughly divided into 4 clusters of various colors in the present study. Cluster 2 focused on the cDTI image processing aspects of cDMRI, exemplified by keywords such as “mean diffusivity”, “fractional anisotropy”, “helix angle”, “image registration”, and “image reconstruction”. Cluster 3 was centered on clinical applications in various cardiac diseases, covering keywords like “heart failure”, “dilated cardiomyopathy”, “congenital heart disease”, “ventricular arrhythmias”, and “ischemia”. ## Discussion

We summarized the general information, research hotspots, and future trends in cDMRI research. Figure 7 provided a graphical summary of the main bibliometric findings and research trends.

  Based on the systematic and comprehensive bibliometric analysis of cDMRI research from 2005 to 2024, cDMRI has been confirmed as a valuable noninvasive tool and a novel direction for assessing cardiac microstructure. The annual distribution of publications indicated a clear upward trend of cDMRI articles, reflecting increasing research interest and growing recognition of cDMRI's potential in cardiovascular imaging. ### Research hotspots
  A bibliometric analysis commonly utilizes frequent keywords, citation and co-citation analyses of references to determine notable literature and evaluate research progression (22). Articles with a high number of citations typically represent high-quality research characterized by substantial innovation and remarkable influence within their respective fields.

  As revealed by the frequent keywords and highly cited studies, the “diffusion tensor imaging” ranked as the most prominent keyword with the most occurrences (191.0) and strongest citation bursts (6.8). Analysis of highly cited studies in cardiac “diffusion tensor imaging revealed” that: the research hotspots in cDTI appeared to consist of three main areas, including clinical application of cDTI, sequence design of cDTI, and cardiac electrophysiology based on cDTI.


#### Clinical application of cDTI
  The results have demonstrated that, exploring cDTI's clinical applications is probably the main direction of cDMRI. The articles by Sonia Nielles-Vallespin et al. in 2017 had the strongest citation burst, which validated the ability of in-vivo cDTI to characterize myocardial microstructure dynamics and emphasized its clinical relevance for heart disease assessment (10). Ming-Ting Wu et al. in 2006, the study with most citations in cDMRI, used in-vivo cDTI as markers of microstructural integrity and viability to investigate myocardial fiber remodeling after infarction. In addition, the presence of “myocardial infarction” (MI) and “hypertrophic cardiomyopathy” (HCM) among the top 10 keywords highlights that cDMRI has become a valuable diagnostic and prognostic tool, particularly in MI and HCM.

  The in-vivo evaluation of myocardial microstructural dynamics has been a useful tool for assessing in-vivo myocardial microstructure (1–3), which can be evaluated by cDTI (9–12). The sheetlets reorientation mechanism, quantified by sheetlet angles (E2A), plays a crucial role in left ventricular wall thickening (10). Nielles-Vallespin et al. validated cDTI-derived microstructure indices in vivo and observed significant variations between systole and diastole, with E2A increasing from 18° (diastole) to 65°(systole) (10). In addition, McGill et al. indicated that E2A values varied across different myocardial slices in both phases, further supporting its role in cardiac function assessment (27). Apart from sheetlet dynamics, other cDTI parameters such as helical angle (HA), fractional anisotropy (FA), and mean diffusivity (MD) provide insights into cardiac fiber orientation (9, 27). McGill et al. indicated that FA decreased from 0.56(diastole) to 0.47 (systole), while MD decreased from 1.11 × 103 mm2/s to 0.93 × 103 mm2/s (27), reflecting the structural reorganization during cardiac contraction and relaxation.

  The bibliometric analysis identified MI as one of the major clinical application areas of cDTI, supported by several highly cited studies demonstrating its diagnostic and prognostic value. The cDTI effectively detects MI in both infarcted and non-infarcted myocardium, aligning with histological findings (11, 28, 29). In Wu et al. reported increased MD, decreased FA, and decreased HA in infarct-adjacent zones, with sequential changes over time (29). Das et al. found that FA in acute infarcted myocardium was an independent predictor of 3-month left ventricular ejection fraction, demonstrating its prognostic value (11). Longitudinal studies indicated that FA and E2A changes predicted adverse LV remodeling over a 12-month follow-up (28).

  HCM was recognized as another major clinical application areas of cDTI revealed by the bibliometric analysis. The HCM patients exhibited abnormal E2A values, particularly in diastole, where E2A remained elevated in hypertrophic regions (9, 10). Ferreira et al. found that E2A values in HCM patients were elevated in both systole and diastole, with abnormally high angulation retained in hypertrophic segments (9). Ariga et al, reported significantly reduced FA in HCM patients, with mid-wall FA disruption linked to ventricular arrhythmias (30).

  However, the lack of standardization and interpretation of cDMRI protocols remains a major obstacle to its clinical adoption. Table 5 showed that, the diversity of cDMRI sequences, acquisition parameters, and MRI hardware systems across previous studies has compromised the comparability and interpretability of cDTI indices. Factors such as sequence type (e.g., STEAM vs. M2C), acquisition parameters (e.g., acquisition resolution, b-values, or cardiac phases), and MRI hardware (e.g., manufacturer, field strength, and coil equipment) all influence the SNR of cDMRI images. It has been demonstrated that SNR significantly affects derived cDMRI parameters, including ADC, FA, MD, HA, and E2A. Additionally, the frequent use of in-house post-processing software in prior studies has introduced variability in cDTI indices, highlighting the need for a standardized analysis tool for cDTI. Furthermore, large-scale, multi-center, multi-vendor longitudinal studies are urgently required to confirm the clinical reproducibility and applicability of cDMRI. Aside from Ferreira et al. and Huang et al., no other studies had a sample size larger than 500 (31, 32). After that, the normal reference ranges for cDMRI indices can be establish, and thereby advance the clinical translation of cDMRI. Therefore, several recommendations can be offered to guide future research based on our findings: First, cDMRI protocols adapted to various MRI hardware systems should be standardized. Second, a standardized analysis tool for cDTI should be developed. Third, a large-scale, multi-center, multi-vendor longitudinal study should be performed. Fourth, the SNR of cDMRI images should be reported, and corresponding normal reference ranges for cDMRI indices should be established.


#### Sequence design of cDTI
  As revealed by the references with strong citation bursts, innovations in sequence design have played a pivotal role in the development of cDTI. Sonia Nielles-Vallespin et al. implemented a novel STEAM method that incorporated a modified prospective navigator technique and a biofeedback mechanism (25). Their article achieved the second-strongest citation burst (20.86). The third-strongest citation bursts (15.55) was recorded for Christian T. Stoeck et al, who pioneered the implementation of M2C cDTI for in-vivo human heart imaging on a clinical MRI scanner (26). In addition, the keywords “motion compensation” and “compressed sensing” presented in the top 20 keywords. Also, it has turned out that “Magnetic Resonance in Medicine” the most productive and highly cited journal, within which the sequence optimization stands out as an important research focus.

  The cDMRI has been challenging to probe in in-vivo hearts because of bulk motion and myocardial strain during diffusion encoding. As revealed by the references with strong citation bursts, sequence design has been the main direction to overcome these drawbacks in in-vivo cDMRI. In the article with second-highest burst strength, Sonia Nielles-Vallespin et al. implemented a novel STEAM method, which was combined with a modified prospective navigator technique and a biofeedback mechanism (25). Their comparison of initial and repeat scans demonstrated good reproducibility for both multiple breath-hold and navigator techniques. The proposed method showed potential for free-breathing acquisition using STEAM and delivering high-resolution in-vivo 3D cDTI tractography of the whole heart. However, the main disadvantages of STEAM are lower Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) and acquisition efficiency (3). In the article with third-highest burst strength, Stoeck et al. proposed the M2C sequence to improve the SNR and acquisition efficiency (33). It commonly uses second-order motion compensation to address signal loss from cardiac motion. It offers higher SNR and acquisition efficiency compared to STEAM (26, 33), the growing interest has been found in recent studies (4, 11). Unlike STEAM, M2C acquires diffusion data in one heartbeat, offering higher acquisition efficiency, but it requires high-performance gradient systems (34, 35). M2C is better suited for free-breathing acquisition, arrhythmias patients, and whole heart coverage (3). However, probably due to differences in motion sensitivity and longer echo time, M2C performs better in the systole phase (36, 37), while STEAM is more reliable in the diastole phase (36, 37). Hence, the M2C and STEAM each possess distinct strengths and limitations.


#### Cardiac electrophysiology based on cDTI
  Notably, the cardiac electrophysiology based on cDTI is probably one of the most important direction in the future based on the current evidences. J.D. Bayer et al. in 2012 in Annals of Biomedical Engineering led in total citations, annual citations, and normalized annual citations with a value of 346.0, 26.62, 4.85, respectively. Their proposed LDRB algorithm is ideally applicable to cardiac electrophysiology and electrical activation simulations in heart models (23). In addition, the keywords “diffusion tensor imaging”, “cardiac electrophysiology”, “cardiac fiber” and “cardiac microstructure” appeared in the top 20 keywords in cDMRI research. cDTI provides critical insights into myocardial fiber orientation, which is essential for electrical activation and contraction of the heart (17, 38).

  Arrhythmic risk assessment is crucial for cardiological evaluation and requires advanced diagnostic methods to identify which patients are at high risk of structural heart disease and sudden cardiac death (39). Cardiac electrophysiology studies employing simulations of electrical activation in cDTI-based heart models have been recognized as an important direction for arrhythmic risk assessment. J.D. Bayer et al. proposed a novel LDRB algorithm for producing cDTI-based myocardial fiber orientation in computational heart models with speed, precision, and high usability (23). The high quality of cDTI-based myocardial fiber orientation enables the improvement of the precision of electrical activation simulations in these models. Moreover, the finite element models of cardiac electrophysiology have been constructed using ex-vivo cDTI data (13). These models analyze electrical activity by segmenting the ventricles, generating finite element meshes, and simulating electrical properties (13). Recent studies have also explored new computational frameworks for cardiac electrophysiology simulations, aiming to enhance our understanding of conduction patterns and arrhythmogenesis (17, 40).

  However, the cardiac electrophysiology simulations based on cDTI have yet to be implemented in in-vivo human studies, due to lower SNR, extended acquisition time, and limited spatial resolution. Future advancements in high-performance gradient systems and motion-robust acquisition techniques may enable electrophysiological modeling in in-vivo human hearts. Based on our findings, several recommendations can be offered to guide future cDMRI research: First, cDTI data used for cardiac electrophysiology models should be acquired from high-performance MRI hardware systems. Second, future applications of cardiac electrophysiology models will benefit from optimized cDMRI sequences with higher SNR and shorter acquisition times.


### Future trends
  Bibliometric trend topic analysis offers a valuable means of anticipating future research directions. As demonstrated in the results of trend topics, the “deep learning” has emerged as the most prominent keyword in recent years, indicating its establishment as a key tool in cDMRI research. Since low SNR and prolonged acquisition time remain major limitations of cDTI, the bibliometric findings suggest that incooperating deep learning-based reconstruction and denoising methods is an emerging research area with increasing scientific interest. The need for multiple diffusion encoding measurements to robustly reconstruct the self-diffusion tensor leads to prolonged acquisition times, while signal loss from T2 decay and diffusion attenuation results in a lower SNR (41). Deep learning could reduce data requirements in STEAM acquisition maintaining similar image quality (42). Phipps et al. developed and assessed a denoising convolutional neural network based on residual deep learning, achieving at least a twofold acceleration for in-vivo cDTI (42). Phipps et al. quantified the SNR, structural similarity index, MD, FA, and helix angle transmurality, and found no significant differences (42). To address motion-induced signal loss, Zhu et al. proposed an unsupervised dense-encoder-fusion-decoder network for cDWI (43). The cooperation of these networks could investigate the in-vivo myocardial structure more accurately. In addition, utilizing deep learning to reconstruct cDTI index maps from just six raw cDWI images could achieve lower error rates than traditional methods. Liu et al. developed a deep learning framework, FG-Net, to obtain high-quality cDTI parameter maps from six cDWI images by combining deep-learning-based image generation with tensor fitting. They indicated that FG-Net could generate inter-directional cDWIs from six input cDWIs to supplement the missing information and improve the estimation accuracy of cDTI parameters (44). Ferreira et al. found that diffusion tensor prediction using a trained networks is a promising approach for reducing the number of breath-holds required in cDTI studies. Their proposed U-Net method reduced the cDTI acquisition from approximately 12 breath-holds to just 3 (31).

  Though deep learning could be used a novel tool for cDMRI, the bias in reconstructing diffusion tensor maps inevitably exist. Whether this bias caused by deep learning-based methods were acceptable remained unknown. In addition, the evaluation and validation of the deep-learning based methods in cDMRI have not been established yet. Recent studies have recognized the cDTI indices derived from the images acquired from non-deep-learning-based method as the reference standards. Though it is reasonable, however, the accuracy and reproducibility of cDTI are still needed to be confirmed so far. The cDTI indices derived from deep-learning based methods are encouraged to be validated against histology, similar with Nielles-Vallespin et al. in 2017. Moreover, the generalization ability of deep learning-based methods should be validated using external test sets in recent studies. However, no cDMRI studies employing deep learning methods have utilized external test sets to date. This may be attributed to the lack of standardization and interpretability of cDMRI protocols, which severely limits the inclusion of external test set cohorts. Hence, there are several recommendations provided to the cDMRI researchers: First, the cDTI indices derived from deep-learning based methods are encouraged to be validated against histology if possible. Second, these methods are also encouraged to be validated in a large-scale, multi-center, multi-vendor studies.


### Limitations
  Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. This study only included English-language publications indexed in the WoSCC and PubMed, which, although widely used for bibliometric analysis, may exclude relevant research from other databases (e.g., Scopus or Embase) and non-English sources. Additionally, the search period from 2005 to 2024 means that the most recent articles may have been missed. Citation based analyses inherently favour older publications, whereas recently published high-quality studies may not yet have accumulated sufficient citations. The self-citations were included in the citation analyses, which led to self-citation effects. Citation bias may also exist, as self-citations were included in the citation analysis and a small number of highly cited landmark studies may exert disproportionate influence on the identified research hotspots and trends. The keyword bursts and trend topic analyses should not be considered as direct predictors of future cDMRI's development. Other limitations include the inability of bibliometric analyses to assess methodological quality and the disproportionate influence of highly cited landmark publications. Despite these limitations, our comprehensive dataset provides an up-to-date, systematic overview of the research landscape in cDMRI, offering valuable insights to guide future investigations.


## Conclusion
  To our knowledge, this is the first dedicated bibliometric analysis focusing on cDMRI research. The present study primarily analyzed the research hotspots and future trends in cDMRI. The “diffusion tensor imaging” ranked as the most prominent keyword with the highest occurrence count and strongest citation bursts. The top-cited articles with the strongest citation bursts focused on three main areas, including clinical application of cDTI, sequence design of cDMRI, and cardiac electrophysiology based on cDTI. Trend topic analysis further identified “deep learning” as an emerging research focus in cDMRI since 2022.
