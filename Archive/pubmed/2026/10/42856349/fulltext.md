# Design-Evolution Synergy Enables New-to-Nature Enzyme Chemistry


## Abstract
  Directed evolution and machine-learning-assisted evolution have transformed biocatalysis by enabling rapid optimisation of catalytic performance within compatible protein scaffolds. In parallel, advances in deep-learning-driven de novo protein design have expanded accessible protein architectural space, enabling the generation of functional catalysts for increasingly challenging transformations. We herein report a synergistic design-evolution strategy to encode new-to-nature regio- and enantioselective C–H amination reactions using engineered de novo haemproteins to synthesise chiral piperidines, a privileged pharmacophore in drug discovery. By integrating motif scaffolding, computational re-design, and directed evolution, we demonstrate de novo enzyme design can enable challenging, high-energy-barrier enzyme chemistry with programmable regio- and enantioselectivity, high catalytic efficiency, excellent enzyme stability, and scaffold diversity.


## Results and Discussion:

### Reaction design and directed evolution from a natural protoglobin scaffold
  Intramolecular C–H amination through metal-catalysed nitrene transfer has emerged as a powerful strategy for the synthesis of N-heterocycles (38–43). Substantial advances in both small-molecule catalysis (38–42) and biocatalysis (44–51) have been achieved in the construction of cyclic sultams (44–47), sulfamides (48), carbamates (49), lactams (50), and indolines (51) from electronically stabilised nitrenoid intermediates. By contrast, synthesis of chiral saturated N-heterocycles prevalent in pharmaceuticals and bioactive molecules (37) via alkyl nitrenoid C–H amination remains underdeveloped (43, 52–57), with asymmetric catalysis strategies limited to benzylic C–H amination leading to thermodynamically and kinetically favoured 2-arylpyrrolidines (51, 58–60).

  Extending alkyl nitrene chemistry beyond systems with unambiguous regioselectivity to those involving competing C–H amination pathways (Fig. 2) presents several fundamental challenges. Beyond the intrinsic instability of alkyl nitrenoid intermediates (61–62), regioselective amination of the benzylic versus homobenzylic C–H bond via competing 1,6- versus 1,5-hydrogen atom transfer, respectively, is complicated by the conformational flexibility of the alkyl chain and the absence of directing functionalities (63–66). Consequently, no precedents exist for catalyst-controlled regiodivergent C–H amination via an alkyl nitrenoid intermediate, nor for the enantioselective synthesis of either regioisomer. Surveying first-row transition-metal catalysts capable of intramolecular alkyl nitrene transfer (Scheme S3) further revealed that most systems adopt relatively planar coordination geometries with open axial access to the reactive intermediate. Unlike small-molecule catalysts, however, enzymes operate within DNA-encoded three-dimensional catalytic architectures capable of exerting precise control over substrate positioning and reactive-intermediate geometry. We therefore envisioned that a tailored enzyme scaffold could simultaneously govern regioselectivity and enantioselectivity in alkyl nitrenoid-mediated C–H amination.

  Using (5-azidopentyl)benzene (1a, Fig. 2) as a model substrate, we first screened a panel of wild-type and engineered protoglobins to identify evolutionary starting points for regio- and enantioselective C–H amination (67). Several protoglobin homologues catalysed the desired intramolecular cyclisation, with the highest combined activity toward both regioisomeric products 2a and 3a (Fig. 2) observed in a collection of wild-type protoglobins found in various thermophilic archeae and bacteria. Starting from TmPgb-wt from Thermus amyloliquefaciens, directed evolution afforded pyrrolidine synthase TmPgb-PYR-G2–5441, which delivered pyrrolidine 3a with high regio- and enantioselectivity (67). During the initial round of evolution (Fig. 2a–c), mutation I148T emerged as a key determinant of regioselectivity, shifting the product distribution from 3:97 to 45:55 (2a:3a). The pronounced evolvability of the TmPgb scaffold prompted us to investigate whether directed evolution could further access regiodivergent C–H amination pathways. After seven rounds of evolution, TmPgb-PIP-G7–5448, bearing 11 mutations (S17G, C30S, F62W, S65G, H66E, G78A, S141N, I148T, Y170H, Q180S and G192S), was obtained. This variant catalysed formation of piperidine 2a in 6% assay yield with 84:16 rr and >99% ee under whole E. coli cell conditions, corresponding to an ~80-fold improvement in activity together with reversal of regioselectivity toward 2a. Further optimisation under purified-enzyme conditions increased the yield to 12.3%.

  Together, these results demonstrate that directed evolution can steer the regioselectivity from an intrinsically biased starting point toward a complementary reaction pathway. This raises a deeper question: how do we choose the optimal starting point? Selecting a starting scaffold is essentially a hypothesis about its evolvability rather than an assessment of its present catalytic capabilities. The objective is therefore not to exhaustively search existing libraries for a single optimal scaffold, but to use target-informed criteria to identify or create promising starting points across natural and designed protein space.


### De novo enzyme design of nitrene transferase for regio- and enantioselective C–H amination reaction using AP Novo
  Building on the evolutionary trajectory of TmPgb-PIP-G7, we surmised that the observed regioselectivity is primarily dictated by substrate positioning within scaffold-dependent active-site geometries. Although enzymes are inherently programmable through their genetic encoding, the locally accessible catalytic landscape could be constrained by the structural scaffolds available from natural evolution. De novo enzyme design aims to explore beyond local optima by constructing tailored evolutionary starting points that access properties beyond those readily attainable from nature.

  De novo haemoprotein design has served as a benchmark for evaluating emergent catalytic function in artificial enzymes. Early studies largely focused on transformations with established small-molecule catalytic precedents. These reactions, such as styrene cyclopropanation (21–22), are already catalysed to an appreciable extent by free haem. In contrast, the desired regioselective C–H amination via biocatalytic nitrene transfer is an enzyme-enabled reaction, and represents a substantially more demanding reaction manifold involving high-energy reactive intermediates and stringent control over competing pathways. Such reactivity and selectivity have not been explored by de novo enzyme design, nor have they been addressed by small-molecule catalysis. This reaction can thus serve as a cornerstone demonstration of how de novo enzymes can extend beyond benchmark reactivity toward sophisticated abiological transformations representing real-world synthetic challenges (Fig. 3a).

  With these considerations in mind, we selected this transformation as an ideal application of AP Novo (36). AP Novo improves upon the in silico benchmark performance of other generative models and the resulting enzymes demonstrate high in vitro catalytic efficiency on model reactions such as Kemp elimination and ester hydrolysis. Using AP Novo, piperidine synthases with novel scaffolds and starting-point activities were generated. Notably, despite modest activity (up to 22 TTN, 99:1 rr, 94% ee), multiple de novo enzymes achieved higher regioselectivity for 2a than the active wild-type protoglobin starting points (36). To address catalytic activity limitations, we selected two initial designs, GDM-NT-121 (1.6 TTN, 94:6 rr, −40% ee) and GDM-NT-151 (0.9 TTN, 91:9 rr, 66% ee), for refinement via partial diffusion, a process that slightly re-noises and then denoises a structure to sample the local structural landscape (Fig. 3b–3d). We term the outputs of this process “recycled-scaffold” designs, as they are generated by optimizing parent designs with the same scaffold.

  The set of “recycled-scaffold” designs exhibited markedly improved activity, including GDM-NT-226 (1.5% yield, 99:1 rr, 29% ee), representing an 18-fold increase in yield relative to its parent GDM-NT-121, and GDM-NT-362 (0.45% yield, 99:1 rr, 88.5% ee) with 15-fold greater yield than GDM-NT-151 (Fig. 3d). In this design round, we introduced new filters which were predictive of activity in the first round (36), including one which evaluates the near-attack positions of the bond-forming carbon and nitrogen atoms (see Supplementary Information). Retrospectively, the distance between the nitrenoid nitrogen center (N1) and the benzylic carbon (C5) was shown to discriminate hits from less optimal designs in the ‘recycled-scaffold’ set based on GDM-NT-151 (Fig. 3c), consistent with the proximity of these atoms in the hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) transition state (TS) of the proposed mechanism (see Fig. 5a and Supplementary Information, Fig. S14). Compared to natural haemoproteins, including those extensively explored for new-to-nature catalysis (cytochromes P450, cytochromes c, and globins (4)), these designs adopt architecturally distinct folds with differently positioned haem-binding pockets, with the closest structural match exhibiting a TM-score <0.5 with any protein in the PDB (Fig. 3b). With the discovery of these improved variants situated in a different structural space, we hypothesized that further improvements could be found by exploring this new local landscape by directed evolution.

  We thus evaluated the evolvability of the de novo enzymes through random mutagenesis and high-throughput plate-based activity screening under whole-cell conditions. Libraries derived from both GDM-NT-226 and GDM-NT-362 yielded multiple variants exhibiting greater than two-fold improvements in activity (see Supplementary Information, Fig. S10, S11), suggesting that these de novo scaffolds are readily evolvable. Among these lineages, directed evolution of GDM-NT-362 proved particularly productive (Fig. 3e–f), furnishing variants with more than two orders of magnitude enhancement in normalised activity, with GDM-NT-399 (GDM-NT-362_V53L-I68V-E99G-H155L) giving the best result (19% yield, >99:1 rr, 90% ee). Sequence-structure analysis of the highest activity variants revealed a consistent mutation pattern at the axial His155 residue from the input motif for design. Despite this, the haem cofactor’s predicted position was unchanged, consistent with the sustained high regioselectivity. Prompted by the robustness to mutation in the proximal haem pocket, we further engineered site 155 and adjacent position 156.

  To our delight, a collection of variants with different combinations of site 155/156 residues all yielded high activity toward 2a production (see Supplementary Information, Table S15). On a plate-based screen, GDM-NT-400 (GDM-NT-362_H155M-M156L) featuring methionine as the presumed axial ligand gave 2a in 26% yield, while the best performing variant, GDM-NT-401 (GDM-NT-362_V53L-I68V-E99G-H155M-M156L) bearing three additional mutations incorporated from random mutagenesis further increased the yield to 36%. This result was further optimised under normalized whole-cell conditions, giving 2a in 66% yield, 1200 TTN, >99:1 rr, and 95% ee. This brief directed evolution campaign from a de novo enzyme scaffold suggests that building on a well-defined evolution starting point, in this case a highly regioselective parent design, directed evolution can be drastically accelerated.

  Although enantioselectivity was not an explicit design objective, partial diffusion revealed pronounced enantiodivergence, with catalysts favouring both product enantiomers emerging in the second round of design (Fig. 3g). In contrast to small-molecule catalysis, where reversed enantioselectivity can be achieved through straightforward application of a mirror-image catalyst, enantiodivergence in enzyme engineering is considerably more challenging and typically arises through serendipitous mutations identified during directed evolution. The emergence of complementary stereochemical outcomes through local exploration of de novo sequence-structure space highlights the unique potential of partial-diffusion-based redesign for optimizing not only activity but otherwise elusive enantioselectivity.


### Stability and synthetic applicability of GDM-NT-401
  We next evaluated GDM-NT-401 under a range of stress conditions. To our delight, this enzyme remains highly productive under high substrate concentrations similar to those used for small-molecule transition-metal catalysts, giving 2a in 70% yield, 1700 TTN at 50 mM substrate loading and 52% yield, 2600 TTN at 100 mM substrate loading (Fig. 3h). T50 measurements were conducted to demonstrate the high thermostability of the designed enzyme. No detectable loss of catalytic activity was observed for GDM-NT-401 across the tested temperature range (30–85 °C), whereas TmPgb-PIP-G7 lost half of its activity at around 65 °C (Fig. 3i). GDM-NT-401 also demonstrated remarkable tolerance toward organic solvents. Reactions containing up to 60% ethanol still afforded 2a in 46% yield while maintaining high regio- and enantioselectivity (Fig. 3j). Other commonly used water-miscible co-solvents, including methanol, acetonitrile, dimethyl sulfoxide, N,N-dimethylformamide, and acetone, were likewise tolerated at concentrations as high as 40% v/v (Fig. 3k). These properties highlight the capacity of de novo protein design to access physicochemical properties beyond those typically associated with naturally evolved haemoproteins.


### Design-evolution synergy

#### From design to evolution:
  Approximately 90% of natural haem-binding proteins use histidine or cysteine as the protein-derived axial ligand (68, see Supplementary Information, Figure. S4). On the other hand, noncanonical replacements of axial ligands are known to arise in nature (69) and, more importantly, have proved critical in previous directed evolution campaigns for new-to-nature enzyme activities (68, 70–71). To test whether the de novo design pipeline is able to propose these unconventional solutions, we generated “no-protein motif” designs with input motifs consisting only of the haem cofactor and N-heterocyclic products, removing the explicit axial constraint and found that the designed residues at the conventional axial position strongly favored histidine, reflecting its dominance in natural sequences (see Supplementary Information, Figure. S4). Thus, without further conditioning, the design model remains biased towards the distribution of the natural scaffolds it was trained on. This reveals a complementary relationship between design and directed evolution: design establishes architecturally permissive starting scaffolds encoding the desired regioselectivity, while directed evolution experimentally interrogates solutions outside the training distribution of current design methods.


#### From evolution to design:
  Moreover, the design-evolution relationship is not a one-way pipeline for generating new evolution starting points, but rather offers reciprocal synergy. We surmised that leveraging this reverse flow of information could be vital for improving initial hit rates, increasing starting-point activities, and generating scaffold diversity where new-to-nature chemistry may emerge. We therefore initiated a third round of novel-scaffold design using “refined motifs” extracted from empirically identified endpoints of directed evolution. Haem-bound piperidine poses and their corresponding proximal axial residues were extracted from AF3-LA predictions of two post-evolution designs GDM-NT-401 (methionine) and GDM-NT-406 (leucine), as well as pre-evolution parent scaffold GDM-NT-362 (histidine) as a control motif (see Supplementary Information, Table S15). While designs derived from GDM-NT-362 control motif yielded standard hit rates comparable to our previous second round of design (36), the evolution-refined-motif approach proved highly effective. Among the designs with refined motifs, 73% derived from GDM-NT-406 and 29% from GDM-NT-401 were active. Under a stringent threshold requiring greater than TmPgb-wt total C–H amination activity, the rates were 33% and 6.5%, respectively (Figure 3l). As a control experiment, simply mutating the axial histidine ligand in protoglobins or earlier designed scaffolds (such as GDM-NT-121 and GDM-NT-270) failed to yield similar improvements, confirming that this activity boost stemmed from generating custom proteins around the refined motif rather than simple ligand substitution. Ultimately, even without further directed evolution, the leading designs GDM-NT-507, GDM-NT-543 and GDM-NT-589 already achieved high activity with strong regio- and enantioselectivity (Fig. 3m) and substantial scaffold diversity compared to natural enzymes (Fig. 3n and Supplementary Information, Figure S2).

  Notably, 13 out of the 28 initial designs described in the AP Novo paper (36), including the most active designs GDM-NT-121 and GDM-NT-151, adopted mixed α/β architectures. This enrichment was also observed in subsequent rounds of designs (see Supplementary Information, Figure S3), expanding beyond the predominantly alpha-helical folds of previous designed (7, 21) and natural haemoproteins, including globins, cytochromes, and peroxidases. This further highlights the capacity of de novo enzyme design to access structural solutions beyond the architectural constraints typically observed in natural haemoproteins, particularly in the context of demanding new-to-nature transformations (21–23). Although the origins of AP Novo’s enrichment of α/β folds remain unclear, the comparatively rigid structural organisation afforded by β-sheets (72) may contribute to the precise substrate positioning required for high regioselectivity.

  Together, these results demonstrate a reciprocal design-evolution synergy in which de novo design searches broad scaffold space to create well-positioned evolutionary starting points, directed evolution interrogates their local sequence-fitness landscapes with only minimal steps required, and the solutions uncovered through evolution, in turn, guide subsequent design campaigns toward better hit rate, higher initial activity, and better scaffold diversity.


### Synthetic application of GDM-NT-401 in biocatalytic regio- and enantioselective C–H amination reactions
  Having identified GDM-NT-401 as an optimal catalyst, we next evaluated the scope and limitations of the biocatalytic piperidine synthesis under whole-cell conditions (Fig. 4a). A range of aryl substituents with diverse steric and electronic properties were investigated. Under standard conditions, 2-phenylpiperidine 2a was obtained in 66% assay yield, corresponding to 1200 TTN, with >99:1 rr and 95% ee. The yield could be further increased to 88% using purified enzyme at 10 μM catalyst loading. Preparative-scale synthesis on 1-mmol scale afforded 2a in 84% isolated yield while maintaining excellent regio- and stereoselectivity (see Supplementary Information for details). The modular substrate synthesis route using NaN3 as nitrogen source also enabled single-atom radiolabeling, furnishing the 15N-labelled product 2b in 59% yield with preserved regio- and enantioselectivity. Substrates bearing para-substitutions gave variable yields from 5% to 55% (2c–2g). We attribute this variation primarily to steric interactions between substituted aryl groups and the protein scaffold, which were tailored for an unsubstituted substrate 1a during the motif scaffolding step. Nevertheless, further engineering in this region could address a specific synthetic demand when desired. Meanwhile, meta-substituted substrates were also tolerated, affording products 2h–2k in moderate yields, while sterically demanding 2-naphthyl substrate furnished piperidine 2l in diminished yield. Notably, excellent regioselectivities were obtained despite substantial variation in steric and electronic properties, highlighting the reliable control over substrate positioning inside the enzyme active site. Good to excellent enantioselectivities were likewise observed throughout the substrate scope, demonstrating great potential in synthetic applicability. As a direct comparison, we performed parallel experiments using purified TmPgb-PIP-G7, the product of directed evolution of a natural protein. Across the examined substrate series, TmPgb-PIP-G7 delivered the corresponding piperidines with consistently excellent enantioselectivities (>99% ee) favouring the opposite (S)-enantiomers, albeit exhibiting overall diminished substrate-dependent yields and regioselectivities. Although additional directed evolution could likely further enhance the activity and substrate promiscuity of this lineage, these results underscore the critical role of scaffold selection in biocatalyst development. As directed evolution remains inherently resource- and time-intensive, the accessible catalytic landscape is often strongly biased by the structural and mechanistic predispositions of the starting scaffold. Consequently, identifying privileged scaffolds capable of supporting productive reactivity at early stages can have profound impact for the eventual catalytic outcome, influencing the attainable levels of activity, selectivity, and substrate compatibility.

  The high catalytic efficiency of GDM-NT-401 prompted us to further evaluate its performance against structurally diverse alkyl and aryl azide substrates (Fig. 4b). Despite these substrates deviating substantially from the original design target, the enzyme retained appreciable catalytic activity across multiple substrate classes, highlighting that de novo enzymes can exhibit significant breadth in substrate range rather than being restricted to an exquisitely crafted model reaction. We speculate that the high intrinsic reactivity of GDM-NT-401 contributes to this behaviour by enabling productive turnover even when substrate-binding geometries are not fully optimised. Using benzylic-substituted substrate 1m, piperidine 2m bearing an α-to-nitrogen quaternary stereocentre was obtained in 23% yield under whole-cell conditions and 45% yield using purified enzyme, albeit with modest enantioselectivity (36% ee). We next challenged the catalyst with substrate 1n, where multiple cyclisation manifolds leading to azepane, piperidine, or pyrrolidine products are all mechanistically feasible. Prior to experimentation, analysis of the AF3-LA model of the putative alkyl nitrene intermediate within the enzyme active site suggested preferential formation of the seven-membered azepane product (see Supplementary Information, Figure S8). Consistent with this prediction, wet-lab experiments furnished 2-phenylazepane 2n with high regioselectivity (89:8:3 azepane:piperidine:pyrrolidine) and 98% ee, demonstrating that the engineered active site can exert substantial control over competing radical cyclisation trajectories even in structurally flexible systems. Shortening the alkyl tether redirected cyclisation toward formation of 2-phenylpyrrolidine 2o, which was obtained in 48% yield and 15% ee. Moreover, the reaction is not restricted to alkyl azides. Substituted aryl azides underwent efficient intramolecular C–H amination to furnish indolines 2p–2q in high yields, whereas formation of the analogous isoindoline 2r from benzyl azide was observed only in trace amounts.


### Computational analysis of TmPgb-PIP-G7 and GDM-NT-401 for the origin of regioselectivity.
  To better understand the origin of the high regioselectivity obtained using the de novo-designed GDM-NT-401, we computationally investigated how GDM-NT-401 and the evolved TmPgb-PIP-G7 variant each control regioselectivity. Although the benzylic ε-C–H bond is 10 kcal mol−1 weaker than the homobenzylic δ-C–H bond (Fig. 5a), previous catalytic systems generally provide only modest regioselectivity (52, 54–55), suggesting that bond dissociation energy (BDE) is not the only factor determining the outcome of the competing 1,6- and 1,5-HAT pathways. Consistent with this view, our density functional theory (DFT) calculations using a minimal iron porphyrin model revealed that the BDE difference is substantially attenuated at the corresponding transition states (ΔΔG‡ = 3.2 kcal/mol, Fig. 5a). More importantly, the two pathways proceed through markedly different transition-state geometries. In the 1,6-HAT TS, the Ph group is placed in proximity to the haem cofactor, whereas the same Ph is allowed to move further away from the haem and adopt a more extended conformation in the homobenzylic 1,5-HAT TS. These distinct geometries provide the protein environment with a structural handle to differentiate the competing pathways through enzyme engineering or de novo design.

  To understand how the active site pockets affect the regioselectivity, three independent replicas of 500 ns classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the Fe(III)–nitrene intermediate were performed in the evolved TmPgb-PIP-G7 and in the de novo-designed GDM-NT-401. In TmPgb-PIP-G7, the alkyl chain of the nitrene favours an extended geometry with most of the rotatable C(sp3)–C(sp3) bonds adopting the trans conformation (θ2, θ3, θ4 ≈ 180°, defined in Fig. 5b). This places the Ph group on the alkyl chain in the hydrophobic pocket surrounded by Y60, W59, and F144 residues, forming stabilising π⋯π and C–H···π interactions. By contrast, in GDM-NT-401, the nitrene alkyl chain favours a bent geometry with most C–C bonds (θ1, θ2, θ3) in either gauche+ or gauche− conformation and the terminal Cδ–Cε bond (θ4) in the trans conformation. These dihedral distributions closely match those of the DFT-computed 1,6-HAT TS, the exception being θ3, which remains flexible and samples multiple conformations in MD simulations. The resemblance between the preferred conformation of the Fe–nitrene intermediate in GDM-NT-401 and the 1,6-HAT TS indicates that the designed pocket, specifically using 2a as part of the input motif, effectively preorganises the intermediate for 1,6-HAT. Additionally, this preorganised geometry is stabilised by the interactions of the Ph group on the alkyl chain in a hydrophobic pocket surrounded by residues V28, L20, L19, and L16, which was first generated by the initial round of AP Novo design (GDM-NT-151) and subsequently refined during partial diffusion (GDM-NT-362, adding L28V mutation), highlighting the complementary benefit of both design strategies.

  To investigate how the binding poses affect the interactions of the nitrene centre with the ε- and δ-C–H bonds, we analysed the close contacts between the nitrene N atom and these C–H bonds observed in the MD simulations (Fig. 5c). Here, a close contact is defined as when the N⋯C distance is shorter than 4.0 Å and the ∠N⋯H–C angle is greater than 115°. In the unrestrained simulations with TmPgb-PIP-G7, close contacts were essentially not observed with ε-C–H and only observed in 0.1% of the MD frames with δ-C–H, indicating substantial conformational changes would be needed to achieve either 1,6- or 1,5-HAT TS geometry. In contrast, 31.5% of unrestrained MD frames with GDM-NT-401 show close contacts with ε-C–H, whereas only 3.3% have close contacts with δ-C–H. Therefore, the nitrene binding pose in GDM-NT-401 not only enables a bent alkyl chain geometry necessary for the HAT transition state, but also positions the benzylic ε-C–H closer to the reactive nitrene centre, thereby enhancing the kinetic regioselectivity for 1,6-HAT. To further mimic Fe–nitrene/C–H interactions near the HAT transition states, a distance restraint (2.9–3.5 Å, force constant 100 kcal mol−1 Å−2) was applied between the nitrene nitrogen and the centroid of the δ- and ε-carbons. In these restrained MD simulations, TmPgb-PIP-G7 populated close contacts with ε-C–H (1,6-HAT) in 32.3% of the MD frames and with δ-C–H (1,5-HAT) in 2.8% of the frames, whereas GDM-NT-401 gave a higher ratio favoring ε-C–H (42.4% compared with 2.1% for δ- C–H. See Supplementary Information Fig. S28 for more details), consistent with the observed regioselectivity.

  Taken together, these results indicate that the de novo-designed active site pocket of GDM-NT-401 enhances 1,6-HAT regioselectivity by conformational preorganization enabled by a rationally designed input motif. It enforces a bent nitrene conformation that preorganises the benzylic ε-C–H towards the nitrene centre, whereas the extended pose enforced by the evolved TmPgb-PIP-G7 active site requires substantial reorganisation to reach either HAT transition state. This contrast highlights a central advantage of mechanism-guided de novo enzyme design. Directed evolution can efficiently interrogate local sequence space once a suitable scaffold has been identified, but its success depends on the accessibility of the pre-existing landscape. De novo design addresses the upstream problem by allowing a mechanism-informed motif to guide scaffold construction rather than relying exclusively on architectures represented in natural proteins.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, we demonstrate that the combination of de novo enzyme design and directed evolution can enable new-to-nature chemistry beyond the reach of existing small-molecule and biocatalytic paradigms. Through this integrated approach, we developed a regio- and enantioselective intramolecular C–H amination reaction for the synthesis of chiral piperidines, a transformation requiring precise control over highly reactive intermediates and competing reaction pathways. To our knowledge, this reactivity has not previously been achieved in either transition-metal catalysis or biocatalysis.

  More broadly, this work highlights the synergistic relationship between de novo design and directed evolution. Whereas de novo design expands the accessible structural landscape by generating catalytically permissive protein architectures beyond those found in nature, directed evolution efficiently navigates local sequence space to optimise activity and selectivity. The real-world improvements from directed evolution can then directly benefit de novo design through motif refinement, as demonstrated by the substantially higher hit rate and better initial activity. The rapid emergence of a highly active and selective piperidine synthase after only two rounds of design and engineering illustrates the power of combining three-dimensional sequence-structure-function exploration with conventional sequence-function screening. We anticipate that this design-evolution paradigm will provide a general framework for developing new enzyme scaffolds and catalytic functions, enabling increasingly sophisticated transformations that remain inaccessible to both naturally occurring enzymes and existing synthetic catalysts.
