# Integrated computation-guided thermostabilization of the fungal sweet protein Honey Truffle Sweetener


## Abstract
  Thermostability limits the use of sweet proteins as sugar substitutes in thermally processed foods. Here, we report an integrated computation-guided workflow for thermostabilizing Honey Truffle Sweetener (HTS), a recently identified fungal sweet protein with low intrinsic stability. We determined the HTS crystal structure and used it to guide subsequent computational analyses. Rosetta and ThermoMPNN evaluated candidate substitutions from complementary physics-based and deep-learning-based perspectives, and the selected single-mutant library was experimentally characterized. The resulting melting-temperature data were then used to develop SMRC-Net, which combines mutation-centered features from the ESM2 protein language model with molecular dynamics-derived structural descriptors to support mutation prioritization. Repeated nested cross-validation supported quantitative Tm prediction within the HTS scaffold, while prospective testing showed 9/10 directional agreement in a small, computationally selected variant set. Validated substitutions were then assembled stepwise in small modules, followed by the introduction of an engineered disulfide bond. The final variant, FM, had a melting temperature of 82.1 °C, 27.5 °C higher than that of wild-type HTS. Untreated FM retained sweetness, while separate heat- and pH-treatment assays showed retained solubility and largely preserved secondary-structure profiles. Sweetness after these treatments was not evaluated. Molecular dynamics analyses indicated that FM was not uniformly rigidified but instead exhibited localized flexibility redistribution and a more consolidated conformational ensemble. This work provides a practical computation-guided framework for protein thermostabilization, with potential applicability to other compact proteins.


## Introduction
  Sweet proteins have attracted increasing interest as natural high-intensity sweeteners because they provide strong sweetness with negligible caloric contribution, making them promising alternatives to conventional sugars in food systems (Bilal et al., 2022; Farag et al., 2022). Representative sweet proteins, including thaumatin, monellin, brazzein, and neoculin, have demonstrated the potential of protein-based sweeteners for reducing sugar intake while maintaining desirable sensory properties (Shirasuka et al., 2004; Morris and Cagan, 1972; van der Wel and Loeve, 1972; Assadi-Porter et al., 2000). However, the practical application of sweet proteins remains limited by their insufficient thermal stability, because sweetness depends strongly on preservation of the native three-dimensional structure required for recognition by the human sweet taste receptor complex, and even modest unfolding, aggregation, or local conformational perturbation can compromise sweetness and reduce compatibility with thermally processed foods (Temussi, 2009; Tancredi et al., 2004; DuBois, 2016).

  A range of protein engineering strategies has been explored to improve thermal stability in structurally sensitive proteins. Classical approaches such as disulfide bond engineering, proline substitution, directed evolution, and consensus design have yielded stabilized variants in diverse systems (Qiao et al., 2020; Dombkowski et al., 2014; Herning et al., 1992; Stimple et al., 2020; Porebski and Buckle, 2016). In parallel, structure-based computational methods, including energy evaluation and molecular dynamics analysis, have provided useful guidance for identifying flexible regions and stabilizing substitutions (Zhang et al., 2025). Recent studies on neoculin and monellin have demonstrated that computation-guided design can substantially improve thermal resistance while maintaining functional properties (Ma et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024). Nevertheless, most successful examples have focused on better-characterized sweet proteins, whereas reliable thermostability engineering remains challenging for newly identified proteins that lack a substantial mutational history or sufficiently large experimental datasets.

  Honey truffle sweetener (HTS), a 121-amino-acid sweet protein from Mattirolomyces terfezioides, is a newly identified protein-based sugar substitute with attractive sweetness and sugar-reduction potential (Nadal et al., 2022; McFarland et al., 2024). In the HTS discovery patent, the protein is referred to as Myd or mycodulcein and is reported to be approximately 18,000 times sweeter than sucrose on a molar basis and 400 times sweeter on a weight basis (Nadal et al., 2022). Despite these attractive properties, WT HTS exhibits limited intrinsic thermal stability, with a melting temperature of 54.6 °C in this study. This level of stability may be insufficient for processing-relevant conditions such as pasteurization, hot filling, baking, or prolonged heating, under which heat-induced unfolding or aggregation could reduce sweetness and product compatibility (Pomon et al., 2023; Nirasawa et al., 1994). Consistently, WT HTS rapidly lost solubility during incubation at 80 °C. Therefore, the specific research gap addressed in this work is how to rationally improve the thermal robustness of HTS while retaining sweetness.

  This challenge makes structure-guided and data-efficient engineering particularly important for HTS. Although HTS has been discovered, expressed, and preliminarily evaluated for food-related safety and sweetness, no systematic structure-guided thermostabilization study has yet been reported for this protein (McFarland et al., 2024). Moreover, HTS is a cysteine-free, helix-free, β-rich protein and therefore lacks common stabilizing elements such as native disulfide bonds or α-helical packing units. Determining its high-resolution structure is therefore an essential first step toward identifying potentially stabilizable regions and prioritizing mutations when experimental mutational data are limited. At the computational level, Rosetta and ThermoMPNN offer complementary structure-based perspectives: ThermoMPNN estimates whether a substituted amino acid is compatible with the local structural environment, whereas Rosetta evaluates mutation-induced energetic changes after side-chain repacking and structural relaxation (Dieckhaus et al., 2024; Thieker et al., 2022; Kellogg et al., 2011). Using the two methods together can improve confidence in candidate selection and reduce the number of variants requiring experimental testing.

  In this study, we first determined the crystal structure of HTS and screened candidate stabilizing substitutions using the complementary approaches described above. Experimental measurements from the resulting single-mutant library were then used to develop SMRC-Net, which combines a sequence-derived Tm estimate with an MD-based correction for mutation-associated structural changes. Guided by the computational predictions and experimental results, we assembled compatible substitutions stepwise and obtained a highly thermostable HTS variant that retained sweetness. This workflow offers a practical route for thermostabilizing compact proteins by integrating structure-based screening, experiment-derived learning, and stepwise mutation assembly.


## Methods

### Plasmid construction
  The wild-type HTS gene was designed based on the HTS sequence reported by McFarland et al. (2024) and was codon-optimized for expression in Escherichia coli before chemical synthesis. The synthetic gene was inserted into the pET-28a vector between the NdeI and XhoI restriction sites, generating an N-terminal His6-tag followed by a thrombin cleavage site (Leu-Val-Pro-Arg↓Gly-Ser, LVPR↓GS) for tag removal after purification. Genes encoding all engineered single-point and multi-site variants were generated by commercial gene synthesis (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) based on the codon-optimized WT HTS sequence and cloned into the same pET-28a expression vector. All WT and variant constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing before protein expression.


### Protein expression and purification
  Sequence-verified plasmids encoding WT HTS or engineered variants were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) competent cells for protein expression. A single colony was inoculated into LB medium containing kanamycin and grown at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm until OD600 reached 0.6-0.8. Protein expression was induced by adding IPTG to a final concentration of 0.6 mM, followed by incubation at 16 °C and 160 rpm for 14 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min, resuspended in lysis buffer (500 mM NaCl, 20 mM NaH2PO4, pH 7.4), and disrupted by ultrasonication on ice. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 16,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C.

  Soluble protein was purified using Ni-Sepharose affinity chromatography. The His6-tag was removed by overnight thrombin digestion during dialysis at 4 °C, and the cleaved protein was further polished by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 75 10/300 GL column. Purity and yield were assessed by 15% SDS-PAGE.


### Crystallization and structure determination
  Initial crystallization screening was carried out using the sitting-drop vapor-diffusion method. Crystals formed by mixing 1 μL of HTS (20 mg/mL) with 1 μL reservoir solution (20% PEG 3350, 0.1 M Bis-Tris pH 6.0) and incubating at 16 °C. Diffraction-quality crystals appeared within one week and were cryoprotected and flash-cooled in liquid nitrogen.

  X-ray diffraction data were collected at 100 K on beamline BL02U1 of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility using single-wavelength synchrotron radiation. The wavelength was 0.979 Å, and diffraction images were recorded on a DECTRIS EIGER2 S 9M pixel detector at a crystal-to-detector distance of 165.0 mm. A total rotation range of 360° was covered with an oscillation angle of 0.5° per image and an exposure time of 0.100 s per image. The diffraction data were processed using xia2 with the 3dii pipeline (Winter, 2009) and scaled in space group P1211. Initial phases were obtained by molecular replacement with PHASER (McCoy et al., 2007) using an AlphaFold-predicted (Jumper et al., 2021) HTS model as the search template. Iterative model building and refinement were performed using Coot (Emsley et al., 2010) and PHENIX (Liebschner et al., 2019), respectively.


### ΔΔG-based computational screening
  ΔΔG-based computational screening was performed to identify candidate stabilizing mutations in HTS using two complementary approaches: the physics-based Rosetta framework and the deep learning-based ThermoMPNN model (Dieckhaus et al., 2024; Thieker et al., 2022; Kellogg et al., 2011). The experimentally determined high-resolution crystal structure of WT was used as the structural template for all calculations.

  For Rosetta-based calculations, computational saturation mutagenesis was carried out using PyRosetta. Single-point mutations were introduced at each of the 121 residue positions, followed by local side-chain repacking and constrained relaxation. The predicted change in folding free energy upon mutation (ΔΔG) was approximated as the difference in Rosetta energy between the mutant and WT models after local side-chain repacking and constrained relaxation, calculated as ΔΔG = Emutant - EWT. Negative ΔΔG values indicate mutations predicted to stabilize the protein relative to the WT.

  ThermoMPNN predictions were performed independently using the publicly available ThermoMPNN web server (https://colab.research.google.com/drive/1OcT4eYwzxUFNlHNPk9_5uvxGNMVg3CFA), which provides pretrained models for estimating mutation-induced stability changes from protein structures. For each residue position, substitutions to all other 19 amino acids were evaluated, and the corresponding stability scores were obtained.

  To prioritize candidates for experimental validation, Rosetta and ThermoMPNN predictions were considered jointly. The two scores were interpreted separately, with negative ΔΔG values indicating favorable predictions within each method. Candidates supported by both methods or by either method alone were considered for experimental validation. The candidate set also included substitutions that were not predicted to be stabilizing by either method. Some of these substitutions were selected manually to explore alternative amino acid replacements at positions where either method predicted multiple other substitutions to be stabilizing. No fixed score-magnitude cutoff was imposed. Computational screening focused on thermostability and did not include a predictive constraint on sweetness. Sweetness retention was evaluated experimentally during subsequent validation using the sensory procedure described in Section 2.6.


### Thermal stability assays
  Thermal stability of HTS and variants was quantified using thermal shift assays (Protein Thermal Shift Dye Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each 20 μL reaction contained protein at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, 5 μL Protein Thermal Shift Buffer, and 2.5 μL 8× dye. Fluorescence was monitored using an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR system during a temperature ramp of 25-95.5 °C at 0.5 °C/min. Melting temperatures (Tm) were determined using TSA-CRAFT (Huynh and Partch, 2015; Lee et al., 2019). Each Tm value was determined from three replicates.


### Sensory evaluation
  The sensory procedure was adapted from previously reported sweet-protein threshold assays (Assadi-Porter et al., 2000; Ma et al., 2025; Delfi et al., 2021). Sweetness perception of WT HTS and its engineered variants was evaluated by eight trained panelists aged 20-26 years, including four females and four males. All participants reported normal taste perception and no known food allergies or adverse reactions to protein-containing food samples. Before the evaluation, the panelists provided written informed consent and tasted sucrose solutions at concentrations of 0%, 2%, 4%, 6%, and 10% (w/v) to familiarize themselves with the sweetness intensity scale. The average sweetness scores assigned to the 2% and 4% sucrose solutions were set as 1.0 (faintly sweet) and 2.0 (sweet), respectively.

  Protein samples used for sensory evaluation were not subjected to heat treatment before tasting. Samples were prepared at concentrations of 80, 100, 120, 135, 150, 165, 180, and 200 μg/mL. The panelists tasted the samples in ascending order of concentration and evaluated their sweetness intensity by selecting the sucrose reference with an equivalent sweetness intensity. After each tasting, the panelists rinsed their mouths with distilled water and rested for 3 min before evaluating the next sample. For each panelist, the individual sweetness threshold was operationally defined as the lowest tested protein concentration at which the sweetness score reached or exceeded 2.0. The reported group threshold was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the individual thresholds obtained from the eight panelists.


### SMRC-Net: Sequence-to-Structure Molecular Residual Correction Network
  SMRC-Net (Sequence-to-Structure Molecular Residual Correction Network) was developed as a residual-learning-assisted framework for quantitative Tm prediction and stabilizing-mutation prioritization under limited experimental data. The framework comprises two components: a sequence-based baseline model and an MD-derived residual correction model. The baseline model used mutation-centered differential embeddings from the pretrained facebook/esm2_t33_650M_UR50D checkpoint (Lin et al., 2023), with residue representations extracted from the final transformer layer (layer 33; 1280 dimensions). ESM2 was kept frozen during feature extraction and was run in evaluation mode without gradient calculation; no fine-tuning was performed. Only amino-acid residue embeddings were used, while special sequence tokens and padding positions were excluded. Whole-sequence and local-window representations were calculated by arithmetic mean pooling. The local window extended five residues on either side of the mutation site and was truncated at the sequence boundary without artificial padding. The final sequence baseline used the mutation-site and local-window features. The residual correction model incorporated WT-referenced RMSF, SASA, residue-contact, and global perturbation descriptors derived from molecular dynamics simulations. WT reference statistics were obtained from three independent 100-ns trajectories, whereas each of the 65 mutants in the paired MD subset was simulated for 30 ns. Trajectory stability was assessed from backbone RMSD over 20–30 ns and by comparing residue-wise RMSF, SASA, and contact-degree profiles between the 20–25 ns and 25–30 ns intervals (Fig. S7; Table S17).

  Model performance was evaluated using repeated strict nested five-fold cross-validation. Both the outer and inner folds were constructed at the individual-variant level rather than by residue position, so different substitutions at the same residue position could occur in different folds. In each outer fold, held-out variants were completely isolated from all data-dependent procedures, including feature scaling, ESM2 feature selection, MD feature selection, residual construction, ensemble-weight estimation, hyperparameter selection, and model fitting. All sequence-based feature transformations and hyperparameters were optimized exclusively through inner cross-validation using the corresponding outer-training set. Residual targets for the MD-based correction model were generated from inner-loop out-of-fold predictions of the sequence baseline, keeping residual construction separate from the held-out outer-test variants. The resulting performance estimates therefore characterize generalization to held-out variants within the HTS scaffold.

  The MD module learned the difference between experimental Tm and the inner out-of-fold ESM2-baseline prediction using WT-referenced RMSF, SASA, residue-contact, and global perturbation descriptors. The final prediction was obtained as Tm(final) = Tm(ESM2) + λ × r(MD), where r(MD) is the residual predicted from the MD descriptors and λ is the training-selected residual shrinkage coefficient. Residual-model hyperparameters and λ were selected exclusively within the corresponding outer-training partition.

  After model selection, the final prospective prediction model was retrained on the complete development set and frozen before application to the independently designed Rosetta variants. These prospective variants were excluded from all outer and inner cross-validation procedures. Detailed feature construction, model settings, nested cross-validation procedures, residual-model configuration, and evaluation metrics are provided in the Supplementary Methods.


### Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy
  The secondary structures of the HTS protein and its mutants were analyzed using a J-1700 circular dichroism spectrometer (JASCO, Japan). Protein samples were prepared at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in a buffer containing 20 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.4) and 500 mM NaCl. CD spectra were recorded at room temperature in the far-UV region (190-260 nm) using a 0.1 mm path length cuvette. Measurements were performed with a 2 nm bandwidth, and the baseline was corrected using the corresponding buffer solution. Secondary structure content was subsequently calculated using the online tool BeStSel, available at https://bestsel.elte.hu.


### Statistical analysis
  Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from independent measurements as indicated in the figure legends. For comparisons of relative soluble protein levels under different heat-treatment or pH conditions, statistical significance was evaluated using one-way ANOVA. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

  To assess whether the low thermal stability of HTS could be explained by weak overall packing, we compared its residue-contact density and pocket/cavity features with those of representative β-rich sweet proteins (Table S14). Under the same analysis criteria, the overall contact density of HTS was comparable to that of thaumatin I and higher than that of brazzein. Thus, the data do not support uniformly loose packing across the HTS fold. However, CASTpFold analysis identified a higher total molecular-surface pocket/cavity volume per residue in HTS than in the reference proteins (Ye et al., 2024). Local pocket/cavity heterogeneity may therefore remain relevant even though the global contact density is not reduced.

  The principal differences instead involve the stabilizing architectures of these proteins. Thaumatin, mabinlin, and brazzein contain multiple disulfide bonds and both α-helical and β-sheet elements, while neoculin is reinforced by extensive disulfide crosslinks (Ko et al., 1994; Li et al., 2008; Caldwell et al., 1998; Shimizu-Ibuka et al., 2006). Monellin is largely β-rich but retains a short α-helix that contributes to its structural stability (Hobbs et al., 2007). HTS, by contrast, contains neither cysteine residues nor α-helical elements. These comparisons suggest that its limited thermal stability may reflect the absence of these stabilizing features together with local pocket/cavity heterogeneity, rather than a global reduction in packing density.


### Complementary ΔΔG-based screening identifies stabilizing single-point mutations
  To identify candidate stabilizing substitutions, we performed comprehensive in silico mutational screening across all 121 residue positions using two complementary ΔΔG-based approaches, the physics-based Rosetta framework and the deep learning-based ThermoMPNN model. For each position, single amino acid substitutions were evaluated relative to the WT structure, and the predicted stability landscapes revealed multiple sites that appeared tolerant to potentially stabilizing mutations, with partial overlap but also clear differences between the two methods (Fig. 2a and b). The resulting library comprised 72 single-point variants.   Experimentally, WT exhibited a Tm of 54.6 °C, confirming its limited intrinsic thermal stability relative to many previously characterized sweet proteins. Of the 72 variants, 41 (56.9%) showed ΔTm > 0 °C. When an operational threshold of ΔTm ≥ 0.5 °C was used to distinguish stabilizing variants from neutral or marginal changes, 33 variants (45.8%) met this criterion, while 27 variants (37.5%) showed ΔTm > 1.0 °C. Q37A and S55G produced the largest increases in thermostability, with ΔTm values of 3.91 and 4.23 °C, respectively (Fig. 2c; Fig. S3; Table S2).

  TSA analysis of WT and six single-point variants (L4Y, T21L, A28E, Q31L, I49V, and E117V), spanning changes in side-chain polarity, charge, and aromaticity, showed that all examined proteins exhibited low pre-transition fluorescence followed by a distinct major fluorescence transition (Fig. S6). Differences in baseline fluorescence and signal amplitude did not obscure the transition region used for Tm determination.

  Together, the Rosetta and ThermoMPNN predictions guided the experimental characterization of 72 single-point HTS variants. The experimentally validated stabilizing substitutions served as candidates for subsequent modular assembly, while a curated subset of the resulting Tm measurements was used to develop SMRC-Net.


### SMRC-Net integrates sequence and MD information for HTS mutation prioritization
  Building on the experimentally characterized single-mutant library generated through ΔΔG-based screening, we developed SMRC-Net as a residual-learning framework that combines a sequence-derived baseline estimate with mutation-specific structural information (Fig. 3a). The sequence module generated a baseline Tm estimate from mutation-centered ESM2 features, including substituted-residue and local-window mutant-minus-WT embedding differences. The second module used WT-referenced MD descriptors to model the difference between the experimental Tm and the ESM2-based prediction. The ESM2 model therefore provided the initial Tm estimate, while the MD descriptors accounted for mutation-induced changes in flexibility, solvent exposure, and residue contacts.Fig. 3SMRC-Net framework and cross-validation performance. (a) Schematic workflow of SMRC-Net, in which a mutation-centered ESM2 baseline is combined with an MD-derived residual correction module. (b) Regression metrics from repeated strict nested cross-validation on the paired MD-feature subset (N = 65), comparing the ESM2 baseline with the final residual-learning model. (c) Parity plots comparing experimental and predicted Tm values for the sequence baseline and the final residual-learning model.

  Repeated strict nested cross-validation was performed on the paired subset with complete MD descriptors (N = 65). The mutation-centered ESM2 baseline achieved an R2 of 0.442, MAE of 1.348 °C, RMSE of 1.747 °C, Pearson r of 0.671, and Spearman r of 0.540. Incorporation of the MD-derived residual correction increased R2 to 0.596, reduced MAE to 1.152 °C and RMSE to 1.486 °C, and increased Pearson r to 0.772 (Fig. 3b and c; Table S4). Thus, the final model accounted for approximately 60% of the observed Tm variance in the aggregated outer-fold predictions, while decreasing MAE and RMSE by approximately 14.5% and 15.0%, respectively, relative to the sequence baseline.

  The relative ranking of the variants improved after MD correction, with the Spearman rank correlation increasing from 0.540 to 0.698 (Table S4). The higher R2 and correlation coefficients, together with the lower prediction errors, indicate that the model captured a substantial portion of the mutation-dependent stability trend within the HTS sequence background, rather than merely distinguishing stabilizing from non-stabilizing mutations.

  The cross-validation results supported the prospective application of SMRC-Net. After the model configuration had been finalized, the sequence baseline and MD residual model were retrained on the complete development dataset and frozen. Predictions from this frozen model were considered together with ΔΔG screening, structural inspection, and experimental feasibility to nominate variants for prospective validation.

  The finalized residual-correction module relied primarily on descriptors associated with flexibility redistribution and contact-network remodeling, including ΔRMSFstd and the minimum, maximum, and mean changes in contact degree (Table S5). These descriptors provided complementary information on mutation-induced conformational dynamics that could not be sufficiently captured by the sequence-based baseline alone. Because preprocessing, feature selection, model optimization, residual generation, and correction-model training were confined to the outer-training partitions, the resulting performance estimates characterize generalization to held-out single-point variants within the HTS scaffold. These results do not establish generalization to unseen residue positions or other protein scaffolds.


### Prospective evaluation of SMRC-Net directional performance in a selected HTS variant set
  The directional performance of the frozen SMRC-Net deployment model was examined using ten Rosetta-designed single-point mutants selected through the preceding computational workflow (Fig. 4; Table S6–S8 and S16). These variants were excluded from model development and cross-validation, including all data-dependent preprocessing and model-selection steps.Fig. 4Prospective evaluation of SMRC-Net directional predictions in ten selected HTS variants. Experimental ΔTm values are shown as mean ± SD from triplicate measurements.

  For the ten prospective variants, final predicted Tm values ranged from 53.69 to 56.94 °C, corresponding to predicted ΔTm values of −1.12 to 2.13 °C relative to WT. A28D, A28E, T77V, S79A, and T115I showed predicted gains greater than 1.5 °C. These five substitutions were distributed across distinct structural regions rather than concentrated within a single sequence or structural motif.

  Experimental measurements provided a directional test of the frozen model within this selected prospective set, and the corresponding experimental Tm and ΔTm values are provided in Table S16. SMRC-Net correctly predicted the direction of the stability change for 9 of the 10 variants, corresponding to a directional accuracy of 90%. The prospective analysis was therefore used to assess directional consistency within the selected HTS design set, while quantitative regression performance was evaluated by the repeated nested cross-validation described in Section 3.3.

  T115I was the only directional mismatch: it was predicted to be stabilizing (predicted ΔTm = 1.85 °C) but showed a small experimental decrease in Tm (experimental ΔTm = −0.67 °C; Table S16).

  Component-wise inspection was consistent with this directional interpretation. Sequence-based predictions remained clustered near the WT baseline, whereas the structure-aware residual correction provided mutation-specific adjustments to the final predictions. Together, these results show that the frozen deployment model provided directional agreement within this small, computationally selected HTS variant set.


### Stepwise modular assembly of stabilizing mutations yields highly thermostable HTS variants
  Ten stabilizing substitutions identified in Sections 3.2, 3.4 were selected for modular assembly. Sensory evaluation showed that the corresponding single-point variants retained sweetness (Table S3). These substitutions were mapped onto the HTS structure and then combined to construct multi-site variants (Fig. 5a). Since the effects of multiple substitutions are difficult to predict, we did not introduce all ten substitutions at once. Instead, they were divided into four modules containing two or three substitutions and tested before further assembly. S60A and S79A were the only selected residues that were adjacent in the HTS structure, so they were paired in Mut2-1 to determine whether local interference would offset their individual stabilizing effects. The other substitutions were grouped into Mut2-2 (A28E/W45S), Mut3-1 (I49V/S55G/R106K), and Mut3-2 (N15D/S41E/A84Q). All four modules increased Tm relative to WT.   We first assembled the two three-residue modules through complementary intermediate routes. Adding A84Q to Mut3-1 gave Mut4-1 with a Tm of 62.67 °C, while adding N15D and S41E gave Mut5-1 with a Tm of 63.88 °C. Incorporating the remaining substitutions from Mut3-2 into these intermediates produced the six-site variant Mut6-1, which reached a Tm of 67.77 °C (ΔTm = 13.17 °C) (Fig. 5c; Table S9). The ΔTm of Mut6-1 was 13.17 °C, compared with a summed ΔTm of 13.29 °C for Mut3-1 and Mut3-2 (7.04 and 6.25 °C, respectively), a difference of only 0.12 °C. SDS-PAGE confirmed the expected apparent molecular size and comparable purity of the multi-site variants (Fig. S5).

  Mut6-1 was then used as the common background for two parallel assembly routes. Addition of the A28E/W45S module produced Mut8-1 with a Tm of 69.51 °C, whereas addition of the S60A/S79A module produced Mut8-2 with a Tm of 68.78 °C (Fig. 5c; Table S9). The substitutions present in Mut8-1 and Mut8-2 were then combined to generate the ten-site variant Mut10-1, containing N15D, A28E, S41E, W45S, I49V, S55G, S60A, S79A, A84Q, and R106K. Mut10-1 had a Tm of 73.03 °C, which was 18.43 °C higher than that of WT (Fig. 5c; Table S9). Tm increased at every step along both assembly routes. However, the gains contributed by the two-residue modules were smaller in the Mut6-1 background than when the same modules were tested in WT.

  In parallel, candidate disulfide bonds were screened on the basis of structural geometry using the PyRosetta DisulfidizeMover. Q37C/D46C was selected as the most favorable pair, and the resulting disulfide bond is shown in Fig. 5b. SDS-PAGE analysis of the purified disulfide variant is provided in Fig. S4. The disulfide variant had a Tm of 61.55 °C, 6.95 °C higher than WT (Fig. 5c; Table S1). Introducing Q37C/D46C into Mut10-1 produced FM, with a Tm of 82.07 °C. This represented a further increase of 9.04 °C over Mut10-1 and an overall increase of 27.47 °C over WT (Fig. 5c; Table S9). Sensory evaluation showed that the sweetness thresholds of Mut10-1 and FM remained within a range similar to that of WT (Table S3). Thus, modular assembly provided a practical way to identify compatible mutation combinations, although the magnitude of each stability gain remained dependent on the mutation background.


### The engineered FM variant shows enhanced thermal stability and broad pH tolerance while retaining sweetness
  Although melting temperature provides a useful measure of thermodynamic stability, proteins intended for food-related applications must also tolerate prolonged heat exposure and variable environmental conditions. We therefore examined the solubility and secondary-structure profiles of the engineered FM variant after prolonged heating and incubation at different pH values.

  To assess heat tolerance, WT and FM were incubated at 80 °C for different time periods, and the soluble fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE. After 1 h of heating, WT had largely precipitated, whereas FM remained predominantly soluble. Even after 4 h at 80 °C, FM retained a substantial soluble fraction (Fig. 6a and b).   To determine whether this improved solubility was accompanied by preservation of secondary structure, we performed circular dichroism spectroscopy after heat treatment. The CD spectra of FM after 1-4 h of heating showed only minor deviations from the untreated control, indicating that the secondary-structure profile was largely maintained despite prolonged thermal stress (Fig. 6c; Table S10). These results show that the engineered FM variant retained solubility and largely preserved its secondary-structure profile under conditions that rapidly destabilize the WT protein.

  We next assessed environmental stability by incubating FM under acidic, near-neutral, and alkaline conditions (pH 3, 6, and 9). SDS-PAGE analysis showed that FM remained soluble across all tested pH conditions, with only minor variation in soluble protein levels (Fig. 6d and e). Consistently, CD spectra recorded after pH treatment were highly similar, indicating that the secondary-structure profile of FM was largely preserved under the tested pH conditions (Fig. 6f; Table S10).

  Together, these results demonstrate that the engineered FM variant possesses enhanced thermal stability and broad pH tolerance, supporting its potential utility in processing-relevant environments.

  To investigate the structural basis of the enhanced thermostability of FM, we analyzed multiple molecular dynamics-derived descriptors, including backbone flexibility, solvent accessibility, residue contact organization, conformational sampling, and secondary-structure retention.   Residue-wise RMSF analysis revealed a non-uniform remodeling of backbone dynamics rather than a global suppression of fluctuation (Fig. 7a). Several labeled engineered sites, including N15D, A28E, Q37C, S41E, W45S, D46C, I49V, S55G, S79A, A84Q, and R106K, showed RMSF values that were higher than or comparable to those of WT. Slightly reduced fluctuations were confined to limited local segments around S60A and a short C-terminal-proximal region after R106K. This region-dependent pattern suggests that the engineered substitutions redistributed local flexibility across the β-rich scaffold, consistent with stabilization through selective local dynamic modulation rather than wholesale rigidification.

  In parallel, residue-wise SASA changes were small on average (ΔSASA = +0.028; Table S11), indicating that FM preserved the global surface organization of HTS. Local decreases in solvent exposure were observed around selected mutation-proximal regions, suggesting improved local burial or altered surface packing without large-scale rearrangement of the protein surface (Fig. 7b).

  Residue-contact analysis further indicated localized remodeling rather than broad reinforcement of residue contacts. The average contact-degree change in FM was small (Δcontact = +0.008; Table S11), consistent with largely preserved global contact density (Fig. 7c). Among the labeled mutation sites, the most prominent local increase was observed near S55G, whereas S60A, S79A, and R106K showed only small positive changes. N15D, A28E, Q37C, W45S, D46C, and A84Q showed contact-degree values close to WT or only minor changes, while S41E and I49V showed reduced local contact degree. This local increase in contact degree near S55G was not accompanied by a corresponding decrease in RMSF but coincided with lower local SASA, suggesting changes in local packing or contact organization without restricting backbone mobility.

  Contact-persistence analysis showed that the global interaction architecture was largely maintained between WT and FM, while selected contacts became more persistent in the engineered variant (Fig. 8a). Network-level metrics were consistent with a modestly reorganized interaction topology, including a higher clustering coefficient, shorter characteristic path length, and a small increase in average degree in FM (Fig. 8b). Mapping these changes onto the structure revealed spatially localized regions of contact remodeling near engineered mutation sites and neighboring loop-strand interfaces (Fig. 8c). These contact-persistence changes indicate altered residue-interaction patterns around these positions.

  The contact changes were concentrated around structural regions that connect flexible loops with β-strand elements. In particular, the segment around residues 36-49 and the strand-loop region spanning residues 80-110 showed altered contact persistence and local surface organization, consistent with improved coupling between flexible segments and the β-sheet framework. In addition, MD contact analysis suggested that a long-range H16-F99 contact may contribute to tertiary connectivity across distant regions of the fold. To experimentally evaluate this interaction, we constructed an H16A/F99A double mutant designed to disrupt the contact. Thermal shift analysis showed that H16A/F99A had a Tm of 49.4 °C, corresponding to a 5.2 °C decrease relative to WT HTS (Table S15), supporting the contribution of this long-range contact to HTS stability. These features provide a structural rationale for the experimentally observed thermostability gain while preserving the native HTS architecture.

  Principal component analysis further indicated differences in conformational sampling between WT and FM. Whereas WT sampled a more fragmented conformational landscape with multiple shallow minima, FM was dominated by a single continuous basin in the projected free-energy landscape (Fig. 8d). Although the spread along PC1 and PC2 was greater in FM than in WT (Table S12), the sampled conformations remained within a single connected region. DSSP analysis also showed increased secondary-structure retention in FM (0.898 versus 0.874 for WT; Table S13), particularly in β-strands and adjacent loop regions overlapping with reinforced interaction clusters. These observations are consistent with reduced conformational partitioning and stabilization of a dominant folded ensemble in the engineered variant.

  Taken together, the molecular dynamics analyses support the conclusion that enhanced thermostability in FM is associated with localized flexibility redistribution, contact-network remodeling, and stabilization of a dominant folded conformational ensemble.


## Discussion
  This study demonstrates that substantial thermostabilization of HTS can be achieved through an integrated workflow linking crystal structure determination, complementary computational screening, experimental single-mutant characterization, residual-learning-assisted prioritization, and stepwise mutation assembly. The final variant FM increased the Tm of HTS from 54.6 to 82.1 °C (ΔTm = 27.5 °C) and retained sweetness. Separate heat- and pH-treatment assays showed retained solubility and largely preserved secondary-structure profiles. These outcomes reflect the coordinated use of structural, computational, and experimental information rather than reliance on any single design method.

  The magnitude of this improvement is particularly meaningful in the structural context of HTS. Contact-density analysis showed that HTS is not characterized by uniformly loose packing, although it has a larger pocket/cavity volume per residue than the reference sweet proteins examined. Its architecture also differs from those of previously engineered sweet proteins: neoculin is reinforced by multiple disulfide bonds, whereas monellin/MNEI contains a stabilizing α-helical segment. The Tm of FM approached the 84.7 °C reported for an engineered neoculin variant, while engineered monellin/MNEI variants have reached values above 96 °C (Ma et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024). These comparisons show that substantial thermostabilization can also be achieved in the initially cysteine-free, helix-free, β-rich HTS scaffold through distributed substitutions and a single engineered disulfide bond.

  The central value of the computational workflow lies in the complementary roles of its components. The HTS crystal structure provided a common structural reference for mutation evaluation, molecular dynamics feature generation, and disulfide-bond design. Rosetta evaluated mutation-induced changes through an empirical energy function after side-chain repacking and structural relaxation, whereas ThermoMPNN assessed residue substitutions using learned compatibility with the local structural environment. Experimental characterization of the 72 selected variants supplied both stabilizing substitutions for subsequent assembly and a curated set of Tm measurements for SMRC-Net development. The SMRC-Net residual-learning step then provided quantitative Tm prediction within the HTS scaffold, reaching R2 = 0.596, MAE = 1.152 °C, RMSE = 1.486 °C, and Pearson r = 0.772 under repeated strict nested cross-validation, while the selected prospective set showed 9/10 directional agreement.

  Rosetta and ThermoMPNN guided candidate selection, although their scores showed only modest correlations with experimental ΔTm across the 72 characterized single-point variants (Pearson r = 0.206 and 0.248, respectively, using −ΔΔG; Table S18). This limited correspondence between predicted scores and measured stability changes highlights the value of incorporating experimental feedback into the engineering workflow. SMRC-Net provided a means of learning from the HTS measurements by combining a sequence-derived baseline with MD-based residual correction. Published performance estimates for PON-Tm and HoTMuSiC on the T267 benchmark are also summarized in Table S18 to place this modeling approach in context (Kuang et al., 2024; Pucci et al., 2016). The different evaluation sets and validation procedures preclude a direct ranking of these methods. Within the present workflow, computational predictions informed which substitutions to test, while experimental measurements established their stabilizing effects and provided the basis for subsequent modular assembly. Related studies on food-derived bioactive peptides have also combined computational screening with experimental validation to identify functional candidates (Chen et al., 2025).

  The combined effects of individually stabilizing substitutions remain difficult to predict because interactions among mutation sites can either weaken or enhance their contributions to stability. Accumulating multiple substitutions may also perturb the protein scaffold sufficiently to compromise function, which is an important concern for sweet proteins whose sweetness depends on preservation of the native fold and receptor-interacting surface. We therefore evaluated selected substitutions in small modules and assembled them stepwise through parallel routes. This strategy kept the number of required constructs experimentally manageable and allowed the thermal effect of each assembly step to be evaluated before constructing higher-order variants, thereby reducing the risk of carrying incompatible mutation combinations forward. More reliable methods for predicting multi-mutation interactions and their structural consequences would further improve this stage of protein engineering.

  The molecular dynamics analyses further suggest that FM stabilization is not achieved through uniform rigidification of the protein. Instead, FM retained the overall HTS fold while showing localized flexibility redistribution, modest contact-network remodeling, and stabilization of a dominant folded conformational ensemble. Experimental disruption of the H16–F99 contact further supported a contribution from long-range tertiary connectivity to the stability of this β-rich fold.

  Thermostability cannot be optimized in isolation from sweetness because stabilizing substitutions may alter surface features involved in recognition by the human sweet taste receptor. Computational methods for predicting mutation-induced changes in sweetness remain limited, partly because detailed structural information on sweet protein–receptor recognition is still scarce (Temussi, 2009; Tancredi et al., 2004; DuBois, 2016). Both Mut10-1 and FM retained sweetness in untreated samples. FM also retained solubility and largely preserved its secondary-structure profile during prolonged heating at 80 °C and after incubation at pH 3, 6, and 9. The preserved secondary-structure profile after pH treatment does not exclude changes in side-chain protonation and local salt-bridge interactions. Whether such rearrangements contribute to the pH tolerance of FM remains to be established. The observed heat resistance supports the potential of FM as a heat-tolerant protein-based sweetener. However, sweetness after heat or pH treatment was not evaluated, so its retention under food-processing conditions remains unverified. Sensory evaluation of treated samples and testing in complex food matrices are needed to establish its suitability for thermally processed foods.

  The fitted SMRC-Net predictor and the performance estimates reported here are specific to the HTS scaffold, which defines the present scope of quantitative validation. The underlying residual-learning architecture, in which a pretrained sequence-derived baseline is corrected using scaffold-referenced MD descriptors, can in principle be retrained for other proteins. This strategy may be particularly practical for compact proteins because repeated MD analysis remains computationally feasible and a modest single-mutant dataset can provide scaffold-specific calibration. Evaluation across additional protein scaffolds will be needed to establish the broader transferability of the framework, and larger or structurally more complex proteins may require different descriptors or simulation strategies.

  Overall, this work shows that a sweet protein can be engineered into a thermally robust, function-retaining variant through a coordinated computation-guided workflow built on an experimentally determined structure. The broader contribution is a staged engineering strategy that links complementary structure-based calculations, experiment-derived learning, and experimentally tractable mutation assembly. This strategy provides an efficient route for thermostabilizing compact proteins when a reliable structure and a suitable set of mutational measurements are available.
