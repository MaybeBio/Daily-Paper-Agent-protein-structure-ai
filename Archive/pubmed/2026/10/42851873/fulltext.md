# Examining Protein Residue-Level Stability Using Theory
and Experiment


## Abstract
  Protein behavior, whether evolved in nature or intentionally designed, is governed by the energetics of amino acid interactions that bridge sequence to function. As an extension of the theory of protein folding funnels, local frustration quantifies the optimality of these residue-level interactions relative to alternatives. Although this framework has existed for decades, experimental validation has remained elusive. Past deep mutational scanning datasets enable experimental assessment across nearly 8,000 positions from 178 proteins. Results provide experimental evidence for theoretical predictions regarding the relationship between local frustration and protein sequence, structure, and evolution. An evaluation of three in silico methods shows modest agreement with benchmarks (r = 0.03 to 0.26), with the predominant local frustration predictor (Protein Frustratometer) capturing under 3% of the experimental variance. A recent deep learning model, Pythia, outperformed other tools across all evaluated metrics (r = 0.42). Finally, this scale of empirical data enables an evaluation of the strengths and limitations of energetic measures themselves, inspiring a complementary Sequence Probability INverse (SPIN) framework, which characterizes optimality through a Boltzmann-weighted selection probability within an ensemble of sequences. These findings help provide experimental grounding for the theoretical principles that govern protein sequence energetics.


## Introduction
  A major advance in the energy landscape theory of protein folding resulted when the fundamental tension between thermodynamic stability and the kinetics of protein folding was resolved. ,  Borrowing spin-glass physics concepts, this framework asserted that proteins bypass the Levinthal random-search problem using an energy landscape funneled toward the native state. In other words, for proteins which fold by themselves, motion through conformational space is guided by an energetic bias toward a stable native state and not akin to random motion on a flat golf-course landscape, as Levinthal had envisioned. For a funneled energy landscape to occur, the principle of minimal frustration must be met. The principle states that evolution selects for sequences which encode two energetic features: the native fold must be located at the global energy minimum, and it must be energetically well-separated from a Gaussian background of competing arrangements, or non-native decoys (Figure  ). A uniquely low-energy native state, combined with a lack of stable alternatives, has been described as the energy gap criterion for a protein to fold robustly (Figure  A and D). Experiments and simulations have repeatedly supported this principle. −

  Energy landscape theory has been extended from global frustration to local frustration to quantify the energetic separation of a wild-type (WT) residue relative to alternatives at the same position (Figure  E–F). Just as global minimal frustration relies on the separation of the native state energy from alternative structure energies, local frustration quantifies the separation of residue-level energies from alternatives. Specifically, local frustration quantifies this energy separation using a Z-scorewhich gives the direction and number of standard deviations that separate the WT residue interaction energy from the distribution of decoy sequence energies (Figure  B–C and E–F). In other words, global frustration measures how energetically optimal a total protein fold is relative to competing arrangements, whereas local frustration measures how energetically optimal existing amino acid interactions are relative to alternatives. Depending on how these alternatives (or “decoys”) are constructed specifically, different definitions of local frustration have been proposed (Table  ). Based on the resulting Z-scores, residue local frustration is categorized as minimal, moderate, or high. Minimally frustrated sites occupy the stabilizing tail of the energy distribution as set by past literature (Z > 0.78), whereas highly frustrated positions are unusually destabilizing (Z < −1). Moderately frustrated residues are closer to the average energy of alternatives at a given site. Since its inception, proteins have been hypothesized to contain around 40% minimally frustrated residues and around 15% highly frustrated residues, with highly frustrated positions often enriched near the protein surface and at functional sites.

  Much like energy landscape theory, local frustration has become an influential framework for understanding protein function at the residue level. While thermodynamic stability itself is important in determining the relative abundance of the native fold at equilibrium, selection pressures of specific interactions are always relative to alternative sequences. This difference makes local frustration and its representation of energetic optimality a distinct and critical metric from stability itself. Local frustration has been used to map fitness landscapes for SARS-CoV-2 evolution, to describe the energetics of disease-linked mutations, and to connect energy landscape features to enzymatic catalysis. Its applications have extended to large-scale properties such as flexibility and allostery, ,  along with the energy landscape theory itself. Despite its impact, local frustration has remained computational, as experiments require vast stability measurements across many residue positions.

  Almost two decades after its introduction, advances in high-throughput stability measurements finally enable benchmarks of local frustration in natural proteins. This allows falsifiable predictions to be tested: at a basic level, experimental local frustration should mirror patterns predicted by theory, and quantitatively, existing in silico methods should recapitulate energetics at the level of individual residues. Finally, empirical benchmarks also allow for analysis of frustration definitions, clarifying where measures capture experimentally meaningful local energetics. By grounding local frustration in experimental evidence, this work clarifies protein local energetics in evolved proteins and may aid future protein design strategies.


## Results and Discussion

### Mapping Experimental Local Frustration Patterns
in Gβ1
  Recent high-throughput studies have clarified how mutations change protein stability, but these insights have not yet been applied to the framework of local frustration. ,−      The single residue definition of local frustration (Table  ) was first analyzed using deep mutational scanning of the Gβ1 domain as a model. A detailed analysis of this protein was selected, as it is one of the most characterized systems for mutational stability. ,  Frustration Z-scores were derived from WT stability and the distribution of mutational ΔG values (eq S1). Specifically, single-residue frustration was calculated using a Z-score, or the difference between the WT energy and the mean energy of the mutant ensemble, normalized by the standard deviation of the mutational energy spread (Table  ).

  Past datasets reported 55 of 56 residues in Gβ1 mutated to 17 alternatives, excluding Trp and Cys residues to avoid interference in fluorescence detection and folding, respectively. Some mutations resulted in misfolding or insolubility; these were classified as qualitatively destabilizing and highlight a limitation in current frustration definitions. Data were filtered using a threshold of 15 mutations per position and compared against a stricter criterion requiring all 17 ΔG values (Supplementary Data). Although the 15-mutation cutoff was more inclusive (46/55 positions vs. 28/55), the resulting single residue frustration patterns differed as a result of categorical groupings, so the stricter cutoff was adopted out of caution.

  Local frustration has been predicted to relate with evolutionary pressures, such as the selection of residues required for function at the expense of potentially more stable residues, giving rise to pockets of high frustration, whereas thermodynamic stability and robust folding select for minimally frustrated amino acids, ,,  As expected, given these competing pressures, the relationship between sequence conservation and frustration index was found to be modest (r = 0.30) (Figure S2B). Functionally, Gβ1 is known to bind IgG with its α-helix and third β-sheet. Results here are consistent with past theory and prediction, in that both highly frustrated residues were found in functional regions at the protein surface, and compensatory minimally frustrated residues are found nearby (Figure  G).

  A related hypothesis, in past literature, is that evolution sculpts a minimally frustrated contact network in the protein core. Again, experimental findings in the present study support past theory, where residue burial strongly reduces frustration (Figure  H–I). Most solvent-exposed positions show moderate and high frustration levels, and all buried residues are found at positions with minimal and undefined frustration (Figure  I). Further, burial percentage is statistically significant and explains 36% of the variance in local frustration (p < 0.001) (Figure S2A). Although positions with many qualitatively destabilized substitutions lack formal frustration indices, they may be viewed as similar to minimally frustrated residues. Under this interpretation, the hypothesis of a buried contact network is reinforced by further tests of contact networks and sequence conservation, which together indicate a buried, densely connected, evolutionarily conserved protein core (Figure  H). However, because Gβ1 is a small model protein with a relatively small fraction of buried residues overall, these findings should be cautiously generalized.


### Comparing Local Frustration
from Experiment and Computation
  Experimental single residue frustration data was compared against in silico predictions, starting with the Protein Frustratometer 2.0, the predominant framework for local frustration analysis. Mutational stability scanning tools not explicitly designed to compute frustration were also assessedDeepDDG, PoPMuSiC 3.0 (Prediction of Protein Mutant Stability Changes), and Pythia. Additionally, pSUFER (protein Strain, Unsatisfactoriness, and Frustration findER) was evaluated using its alternative definition of protein frustration (Table  ). To minimize potential bias, only residues with complete quantitative data were analyzed, though this restriction may increase sampling variability (Figure  A).

  Surprisingly, the Protein Frustratometer showed only a modest correlation with experimental results, explaining approximately 7% of observed variance in Gβ1. This disagreement in a simpler form of local frustration prediction raises questions regarding the accuracy of more complex mutational and configurational calculations, along with their derivatives, ,  that rely on an identical energy function. Compared to the Protein Frustratometer, PoPMuSiC more accurately predicted mean mutant energies, DeepDDG better predicted the standard deviation of mutant energies, and both underperformed relative to the Protein Frustratometer in capturing frustration itself (Figure  A). Collectively, these results indicate that local frustration is harder to predict than stability change alone, as it requires accurate modeling of both the mean mutant energy and the dispersion of mutational energies. Notably, Pythia achieved the highest overall accuracy across all components of frustration prediction with Pearson r values of 0.42, 0.73, and 0.59 for local frustration, mean decoy energy, and energy variability, respectively (Figure  A).

  Some disagreement between predictions is expected, as these programs vary in their stability calculations and structural representations. In this dataset, residue burial correlates strongly with frustration, suggesting that differences in how solvent exposure is modeled may explain differences in accuracy. DeepDDG emphasizes solvent exposure as a key predictive feature, PoPMuSiC encodes accessibility and residue volume within its statistical potentials, and the Protein Frustratometer includes a specific burial term. While the Frustratometer implements the AWSEM (Associative memory, Water-mediated, Structure and Energy Model) force field, DeepDDG, PoPMuSiC, and Pythia were developed using different training data and network architectures. Furthermore, all methods except Pythia assume a fixed backbone, although secondary structure terms are taken into account explicitly by DeepDDG and implicitly by PoPMuSiC. ,

  Pythia is distinct in representing local structure as a nearest neighbor graph, which by comparison is less physically explicit. This allowed for deep learning on large structural datasets (about 105 proteins), rather than smaller mutational datasets used in DeepDDG and PoPMuSiC (about 102 proteins). Graph representations may also have the benefit of putting less constraint on stability predictions, reminiscent of a strategy that helped to advance AlphaFold2, where structure prediction was allowed to imagine physically impossible bond geometries before refinement.

  The Protein Frustratometer 2.0 coarse graining has been reported to slightly worsen the identification of interacting residues: in all-atom Rosetta local frustration analyses using REF15, direct contact prediction improved at the expense of water mediated contact accuracy, yet overall, predicted frustration patterns were largely unchanged. This observation suggests that the AWSEM force field and its coarse graining alone does not explain the disagreement with experiment observed here, because separate all-atom force fields were found to produce similar results. ,  Consistent with this argument, this study next used the same all-atom force fieldREF15, as implemented by pSUFER softwareand found its accuracy (per its separate definition of local frustration) to be similarly limited. Together, this evidence suggests that transitioning from AWSEM coarse graining to newer all-atom local frustration calculations would likely not result in a substantial increase in prediction accuracy.

  Overall, pSUFER underestimated stabilizing mutations and identified only 6 frustrated residues (those with 5/19 stabilizing mutations, or 26%), whereas experiment located 34 frustrated positions (Figure  C). Inherent experimental variability was reported to be typically <0.1 kcal/mol, so the true number of stabilizing mutations may differ slightly. However, this margin of error cannot explain the magnitude of discrepancies between predicted and experimental stabilizing counts. Furthermore, since agreement has been reported between AWSEM and REF15-derived frustration scores, and this work finds that AWSEM and REF15 only modestly recapitulate experiment, it is predicted that all-atom calculations of Z-scores using REF15 would be similarly limited.

  To assess whether prediction accuracy applies in proteins beyond Gβ1, a recent, expanded stability dataset was considered, in which ΔG values were inferred from proteolysis. Prior work reported agreement in stability changes for point mutations, but as discussed, local frustration measurements may be less forgiving, with the potential for compounding errors. Therefore, before extending analyses, a comparison of Gβ1 frustration levels between measures was undertaken. The two datasets strongly agree (r = 0.92), substantially more than the agreement between in silico predictions and experiment (Figure  A). This consistency argues against experimental variability as the main explanation for prediction discrepancies and supports expanding tests to the larger dataset.


### Patterns in Experimental Frustration from Expanded Datasets
  Further analysis focused on a high-quality subset of evolved proteins, as categorized by their ΔG range and mutational scanning depth (Figure  B). Single-residue local frustration was calculated for positions with at least 18 of 19 possible amino acid substitutions, resulting in nearly 8,000 local frustration values across 178 natural proteins. Most positions achieved complete coverage (>70%). While a stricter requirement for proteins to have 75% of all positions quantified was considered, the frustration distributions remained nearly identical, showing a median change of only 0.04. All 178 proteins were retained to maximize the dataset and avoid discarding relevant information.

  As the prevailing in silico method for local frustration prediction, the Protein Frustratometer was examined further. The program again demonstrated weak association with experimental data, explaining 2.6% of the variance in empirical results, although these predictions were statistically significant (r = 0.16; p < 0.001) (Figure  B). Because this dataset contains only small protein domains, a higher proportion of solvent-exposed positions may influence predictions, and data could be biased by excluding non-conformers. Additionally, the limited data points used to define single-residue frustration may contribute to variability. However, the thousands of sampled positions should provide sufficient power to identify general local frustration patterns and assess overall prediction accuracy.

  Like the Protein Frustratometer, Pythia was also further tested on additional proteins as a validation step, selected on the basis of it best capturing experimental local frustration among evaluated methods. A randomly selected subset of 20 proteins (n = 22,420 predicted mutations) (listed in Supplementary Data) from the larger database was first analyzed: a markedly similar ability to recapitulate experimental Z-scores was found (r = 0.47) (Figure S1). This indicated that its performance on Gβ1 generalized to the larger dataset and that Pythia can be confidently expected to outperform the Protein Frustratometer, despite being not designed to predict local frustration itself. Still, these results also indicate moderate predictive accuracy of Z-scores in terms of correlation strength, and the expanded subset analysis here similarly involved small, model proteins.

  A large-scale analysis of empirical local frustration results also enables an evaluation of concepts themselves, or their relative strengths and weaknesses in capturing meaningful measures of energetic optimality. First, there are important differences in what in silico approaches and experiment can measure. Computational methods can decompose energy predictions for specific contacts, whereas real-world experiments often provide only a single ΔG value. Further, many mutations are qualitatively deleterious to stability but cannot produce quantitative values required for Z-score calculations. In the Gβ1 domain, over 1 in 10 mutations were qualitatively destabilizing, yet in larger and more complex proteins, which are more common in nature, this fraction would likely increase: a high-throughput study on a multi-domain enzyme found over 1 in 5 mutations induced misfolding.

  Even with complete data, interpreting Z-scores remains difficult because they are sensitive to outliers and neglect biologically relevant energy magnitudes. Local frustration indices far from zero show substantial variability in relative stability (Figure  A). For instance, similar frustration values near 2 can represent energy distances between the WT and mean decoy energy that span an order of magnitude, from about 0.25 to 2.5 kcal/mol (Figure  A). Finally, evaluating energy optimality becomes more complex when local decoy energy distributions deviate from a standard Gaussian shape. To determine the typical shape of decoy distributions, skew and kurtosis were analyzed across the same residue positions (Figure  B). Based on these two measures, 60.2% of positions are well-described by a Gaussian distribution (skewness −1 to 1; excess kurtosis −2 to 2), and another 25.4% meet a more relaxed threshold (skewness −2 to 2; excess kurtosis −2 to 7). The remaining 14.4% of residue positions show strongly non-Gaussian mutational behavior.

  Beyond distribution shape, local frustration differs from the global framework in which decoys are prioritized. Global minimal frustration focuses on the energy gap between the native state and its lowest energy alternatives (Figure  D). Viewed in equilibrium terms, a stable protein dominates the population, rendering higher-energy global decoys negligible. Only decoys near the native energy level substantially compete for the population fraction at equilibrium. In contrast, local frustration Z-scores emphasize average stability rather than these low-energy competitors. The pSUFER definition of frustration accounts for low-energy alternatives and their magnitudes via adjustable thresholds, but it has notable drawbacks as a simpler definition. Specifically, pSUFER only identifies high frustration as present or absent, and it does not describe destabilization at all, which is clearly important in vivo.


### Complementary Measure of Residue-Level Stability
Optimality:
SPIN
  While current definitions of local frustration provide valuable insights, evaluating different stability optimality frameworks motivated a complementary approach centered on low-energy competitionmuch like global minimal frustrationwith a less model-dependent interpretation. The key conceptual shift involves treating alternative sequences as competitors, rather than alternative folds. In this framework, an ensemble of sequences is analyzed where relative abundances at equilibrium reflect Boltzmann-weighted differences in stability. This allows WT abundance to be compared directly to the mutant ensemble (and may mirror either single-residue or mutational frustration) (Table  ). Aligning sequence stabilities is deliberately simple, but related versions have been used in past protein folding and evolution literature. ,  This measure is termed here as “Sequence Probability INverse,” defined as SPIN = 1/p­(WT). (This term also references the origins of frustration in spin glass physics.) If stabilities are equal among all amino acids at a single residue position, the WT is found 1 every 20 times, and SPIN equals 20. In contrast, perfect stability separation yields a SPIN value of 1, and unusually low WT stability produces large SPIN values. Interpretation of stability optimality via SPIN is thus relatively straightforward: assuming an ensemble of 20 states and stability considerations alone, values under 20 represent WT energetic favorability, whereas SPIN exceeding 20 indicates a relative lack of stability optimization. Note that other forms of SPIN could be envisioned as well by considering different ensemblessuch as double-mutant scanning in parallel with mutational local frustration.

  In contrast with Z-scores, where highly destabilizing mutant outliers are emphasized, and therefore, hindered by qualitative data, SPIN oppositely gives little attention to large destabilizations. For example, past work has related qualitative destabilization to ΔΔG values of −4 kcal/mol in Gβ1: this strongly destabilizing effect would contribute to SPIN about 0.1% as much as a ΔΔG of 0 kcal/mol at 298 K. Since highly destabilizing substitutions are more likely to cause incomplete mutational data at a position than stabilizing substitutions, which are more amenable to quantitative measurement, SPIN is constructed to be less susceptible to incomplete datasets as compared to Z-scores. In the case that ΔΔG values are incomplete, however, SPIN values under a different mutant number (M) may simply be adjusted to account for the full number of mutant alternatives (F) via: SPINadjusted = 1 + F/M × (SPINmeasured – 1). This normalization allows comparisons between complete and incomplete sites, but the measure assumes the unknown contribution as average and worsens as mutational scanning becomes more sparse. Other forms of SPIN could be easily envisioned by considering different ensembles, such as double-mutant scanning in parallel with mutational local frustration.

  By changing the temperature of analysis, the relative exponential weighting of ΔΔG values in SPIN calculations allows for the tuning of selection strength for stability (Table S1). At the high-temperature limit, stability bias diminishes in favor of random sequence selection, such that SPIN approaches 20. Conversely, lowering analysis temperature strengthens energetic penalties. Under very low temperature, if the WT residue is most stable in the evaluated ensemble, SPIN converges to 1; otherwise, SPIN diverges (as WT probability approaches zero). Between these extremes (e.g., 298 ± 25 K; Table S1), the effect of temperature is expected to change SPIN only modestly. This temperature-dependence provides a local analogue to phase diagrams in spin-glass and global frustration theory, where temperature separates random, glassy, and folded regimes by determining whether many states, non-target low-energy states, or the target state dominates, respectively. Therefore, SPIN is not simply a stability measure, but a relative measure of local stability optimality within an evolutionary context. Finally, although Boltzmann-weighted probabilities are the most physically interpretable near physiological temperature, the temperature-dependence of SPIN could provide secondary descriptors of the local stability landscape, such as the global minimum SPIN value and its associated temperature (Table S1). These secondary terms could distinguish sites with similar fixed-temperature SPIN values (but modestly different underlying distributions of individual ΔΔG values) by solving what strength of energetic discrimination the WT is most favorable.

  It is not sufficient to propose a SPIN index only in principle, so the ability of the measure to capture energetic optimality at the amino acid level was examined. Across the same 178 evolved proteins, the median SPIN is 12, consistent with an expectation that most residues in natural proteins are moderately optimized for stability (Figure  C). In further support of this claim, around 90% of residues have SPIN indices below 20, or bias toward WT stability. Near perfect stability separation of the WT in the sequence ensemble (SPIN near 1) is uncommon but present, with an apparent evolutionary difficulty of achieving SPIN values under 3 (Figure  C). At its opposite end, a heavy tail of SPIN indexes approximates a power law from around 20 to 300, indicating a broad range of energy optimization in proteins found in nature (Figure  C).

  Local frustration and the SPIN index align or diverge based on the specific mutational landscape. Agreement occurs at neutral sites where no clear energy preference exists between the WT and decoys; here, SPIN values and Z-scores are near 20 and 0, respectively (Figure  D). Agreement is also observed at positions where the WT is consistently more stable than higher-energy decoys, placing SPIN values far below 20 and Z-scores within the minimally frustrated regime. The two measures disagree when a limited number of low-energy competitors are present. In such instances, SPIN identifies a lack of optimization, while local frustration characterizes the same sites as energetically neutral or preferable (Figure  D). For example, at one position, the average decoy energy is substantially less stable than the WT, but a single mutant provides a large stabilizing effect (>2 kcal/mol). This position yields a high SPIN value of 60.5 despite being classified as minimally frustrated (Z = 1.16) (Figure  D). This disagreement is by design. SPIN emphasizes competitive low-energy features as described above, whereas local frustration measures WT separation from average energies independent of magnitude.

  Finally, the relationships between residue-level stability optimality measures and well-known features connecting protein thermodynamic stability to structure and evolution were examined. First, SPIN was also compared with percent residue burial and sequence conservation scores in the well-characterized Gβ1 system (as performed with local frustration earlier): results were inconclusive as neither measure was significantly associated (Figure S2D,E), in contrast to the results for local frustration and residue burial (Figure S2A). Examining the relationship between SPIN and local frustration showed a clear divergence between the two measures at larger SPIN values (>20) and negative frustration indices (Figure S2F, Figure S3). Second, experimental frustration Z-scores and SPIN values were compared beyond representative examples and across the entire 178 protein dataset (Figure S3). The two measures were found to be moderately related but only partially overlapping, particularly as SPIN values below 20 expand in a way that corresponding Z-scores do not, as discussed above. Overall, these findings indicate that residue-level stability optimality measures are partially connected to established structural and evolutionary variables, but they remains a separate measurement.


## Conclusions
  George E.P. Box famously remarked, “Remember that all models are wrong; the practical question is how wrong do they have to be to not be useful”. In past literature, the combination of expanding experimental datasets, careful analysis of definitions, and simulations has already proven itself usefulwhen applied to Φ-value analysis. This combined approach helped refine what Φ-values can (and cannot) reveal about folding mechanisms, the importance of measurement accuracy, and how underlying energetic variables relate to the final calculated Φ-value in practice. The technique continues to be used, but like any careful and disinterested assessment of an influential scientific concept, how Φ-values are interpreted has been improved as a result.

  As mutational scanning datasets continue to expand, particularly for more complex proteins, additional benchmarks will help clarify the three issues considered in this work: the correspondence between theoretical predictions of local frustration and the patterns observed in nature; the accuracy of in silico approaches at the residue level; and the usefulness of different energetic optimality measures toward describing empirical protein behavior, including SPIN, which may better capture stability differences with low-energy alternative residues. Additional computational tools not examined here can be evaluated, and fold-switching proteins can provide tests of predictions specific to configurational local frustration (Table  ).

  Results in the present study support overall predicted patterns of local frustration in protein structure, function, and evolution, but the computational tools examined could not generally capture experimental behavior at the residue level. Further, the use of Z-scores in describing energetic optimality was found to be limited in certain situations: in cases of qualitative destabilization; when energy magnitudes are important; and, to some extent, when mutational energy distributions deviate from a Gaussian shape. Nonetheless, an integrated approach on residue selection optimality should continue to provide a helpful framework for how nature encodes stable and foldable protein structures, and it may also advance the field of protein design toward goals ranging from human to planetary health.


## Methods
  Stability and structural data were obtained from ProtaBank (ID no. GwoS2haU3) for Gβ1 domain ΔΔG­(m-avg) values and Zenodo (ID: 7992926) for proteolysis assays, with the same offset as previously used. In this work, stability change was defined as ΔGU–N and ΔΔG = ΔGMut U–N – ΔGWT U–N. Sequence conservation was calculated using the ConSurf server with alignment and evolutionary score calculations by MAFFT-L-INS-i and Bayesian inference methods, respectively. Residue burial categories were assigned using GetArea with default atomic solvation parameters and a 1.4 Å water probe.

  Local frustration was defined as the relative distance between the energy of the WT residue and alternative decoys using a Z-score, as described previously. This relies on three terms: the stability of the native state; mean decoy stability; and the standard deviation of decoy stability (eq S1). For single-residue frustration calculations, decoys were composed of all tested point mutations which generated quantitative ΔG values for at least 17 of the 19 mutations at a single residue position, used in place of E0 ij and EU i′j′.

  The Protein Frustratometer 2.0 was implemented under default settings, including Debye–Hückel electrostatic potential. DeepDDG scanned mutants on its non-integrated neural network model setting, and PoPMuSiC (v3.0) and Pythia were run under systematic mode and standard self-supervised prediction conditions, respectively. Last, pSUFER was employed with empirical thresholds for ≥5 stabilizing mutations of any magnitude. Protein structures were examined in ChimeraX.

  Agreement between experiment and computational methods were tested by linear regression and resulting Pearson correlation coefficients were determined by comparison of the test statistic to critical t-values (MS Excel). Skew and kurtosis of per site mutant stability were calculated as the third and fourth standardized moments, respectively, with standard cutoffs of distribution shape as described in West et al. 1995. Excess kurtosis is reported relative to a normal distribution.

  SPIN index was calculated as SPIN = 1/p­(WT), where WT probability is determined in a Boltzmann-weighted ensemble at equilibrium, evaluated here at 298 K. The ensemble assessed mirrored single-residue local frustration, where the alternative sequences considered in the ensemble were the total point mutants at a single position.
