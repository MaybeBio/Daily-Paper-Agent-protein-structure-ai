# Uncoupling of the allosteric signalling network in the β1-adrenergic receptor by a negative allosteric modulator


## Abstract
  Allosteric modulation is a fundamental aspect of G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) regulation where molecules that bind to alternative sites outside the orthosteric pocket alter agonist-induced signalling responses. The advantages of allosteric modulators over traditional orthosteric agonists have positioned them as highly promising therapeutic agents. GPCRs exist in tuneable equilibria between multiple functional states, yet the ways in which allosteric modulators reshape these equilibria remain incompletely understood. Here we characterise how changes to the allosteric signalling network influence the conformational dynamics of the β1-adrenergic receptor (β1AR) through mutations at the E1303.41 position or using the negative allosteric modulator, AS408. Both act through a known transmembrane (TM)3-TM4-TM5 site, reducing signalling to similar extents, but through distinct molecular mechanisms as revealed by pharmacological assays, MD simulations, and solution nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy. E1303.41 mutations reduce agonist-stimulated signalling as a side chain-dependent rheostat, shifting receptor equilibria to populate states with weaker G protein affinities. In contrast, AS408 uncouples the intracellular region of β1AR from the influence of the orthosteric pocket and reduces G protein coupling by adoption of an alternative signalling-incompetent intracellular surface arrangement. Our investigations emphasise the relevance of mechanistic understanding in drug development and the importance of dynamics in GPCR allostery.


## Introduction
  G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are seven-transmembrane (TM) proteins that, following ligand binding, relay extracellular stimuli across the cell surface membrane to the cytosol to facilitate cellular responses1. Communication of the ligand-specific instructions across the membrane during receptor activation is mediated by highly interconnected allosteric networks. Conserved microswitches such as the P5.50-I3.40-F6.44 or N7.49-P7.50-x-x-Y7.53 motifs (superscripts refer to Ballesteros-Weinstein numbering2) facilitate residue side chain repacking within the hydrophobic core of class A GPCRs3–5. This repacking results in substantial structural rearrangements towards the intracellular surface of the GPCR3 and enables the population of receptor states that favour coupling to signalling effectors, which include heterotrimeric G proteins and β-arrestins6.

  Modulation of GPCR activity is essential to the regulation of downstream signalling pathways in many disease states, and thus over 500 approved therapeutics have been developed to target GPCRs7,8. A majority of small molecule GPCR drugs bind into an orthosteric binding pocket, which is generally located towards the extracellular surface9. In contrast, allosteric modulators act at distinct sites from canonical orthosteric pockets to increase (positive allosteric modulator, PAM) or decrease (negative allosteric modulator, NAM) signalling at receptors bound to orthosteric ligands. These drugs bind commonly but not exclusively into shallow pockets formed between the transmembrane helices10,11. Although allosteric modulators are associated with several benefits over orthosteric ligands, including generally increased selectivity since allosteric pockets have lower sequence conservation than orthosteric sites12, there are far fewer allosteric modulators available in the clinic. Drugs with these modalities only represent a small (~1%13) proportion of approved drugs. Consequently, allosteric modulators remain a substantial area of interest for GPCR drug development.

  Structural studies indicate GPCRs sample several distinct functional states connected via tuneable equilibria, including inactive, pre-active, and active states14–25 (Fig. 1a). The exchange between receptor states is directly mediated by the conserved microswitches in the allosteric activation network. This conformational plasticity is critical to receptor signalling as binding of orthosteric ligands modify GPCR equilibrium populations and the rates at which these receptor states interconvert14,16,18,19,24,26–28. Changes in the magnitude of functional responses subsequently arise as these states couple to binding partners with variable effectiveness. Some allosteric modulators have been suggested to stabilise specific receptor states within these equilibria, thereby altering the efficiency of binding partner recruitment induced by an orthosteric ligand5,11,29–34. Current GPCR structures with allosteric modulators represent around 2% of structures in the GPCRdb35, with fewer studies conducted with solution methods such as nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy30–34,36,37. Thus, although communication between allosteric sites and the internal allosteric network of microswitches is integral to the control of receptor activity, the molecular mechanisms by which allosteric modulators impact signalling networks and energy landscapes of GPCRs4,11,38–40 are less well understood than for orthosteric ligands and warrant further investigation.Fig. 1E130X3.41 mutations reduce β1AR signalling by decreasing affinity for G proteins in a rheostat fashion.a Cartoon illustration of β1AR activation showing conformational equilibria between inactive (I), pre-active (P) and active (A) states and their relative G protein affinities.   The β1-adrenergic receptor is the most prevalent βAR subtype in cardiac tissue41 and has been targeted with a range of orthosteric agonists42. The conformational equilibria of β1AR have been investigated through pharmacological assays and solution studies including NMR spectroscopy14–16,22,26–28,36,43,44, providing a useful model system to investigate modulation of receptor energy landscapes. Recently, we showed that reversal of a thermostabilising E130W3.41 mutation45 to wildtype E1303.41 shifted the equilibria of the turkey β1-adrenergic receptor (hereon referred to as β1AR) towards an active state14. The E3.41 position is located in a known TM3-TM4-TM5 allosteric site in GPCRs30,31,46–52. In our current investigation, we establish how varying the environment in this allosteric pocket through introduction of different mutations at this key position can influence the dynamics of the β1AR, and how this informs on the regulation of the allosteric signalling network through this site.

  We then endeavour to compare these data to the effects elicited by a small molecule allosteric modulator. A recent study introduced AS408 as a NAM for the human β2AR (hβ2AR)29. AS408 was shown to bind hβ2AR at the TM3-TM4-TM5 interface, with the 3.41 position forming a key part of the allosteric pocket. The same work found that, although the affinity for the NAM was highest at the hβ2AR, AS408 reduced β-arrestin recruitment at several other related receptors in addition to hβ2AR, including the human β1AR (hβ1AR). Based on sequence and structural homology, we anticipated that AS408 would act through the same allosteric site at the β1AR. Consequently, AS408 could enable pharmacological and structural investigations of small molecule allosteric modulation, complementary to mutations at E1303.41, at the β1AR.

  Here, we first confirm the location of the AS408 binding site at the β1AR and establish that AS408 acts as a NAM for G protein signalling. We then investigate the structural mechanisms by which AS408 affects β1AR signalling and its internal allosteric network by NMR spectroscopy and pharmacological functional assays, utilising mutations at E1303.41 to guide the analysis of conformational equilibria changes induced by AS408.

  Our investigations reveal a similar reduction in G protein signalling at the β1AR by both AS408 and mutations at the E1303.41 position, but by differing mechanisms. Mutations at the E1303.41 position alter the magnitude of receptor signalling by stabilising pre-active or inactive states with lower affinities for G proteins than the agonist-stabilised active state, effectively reducing the efficacy of the agonist. In contrast, AS408 acts distinctly from this mechanism by stabilising an alternative non-productive conformation differing from currently identified receptor states. Notably, we find uncoupling of the allosteric network upon AS408 binding, as the intracellular region adopts an alternative arrangement whilst the organisation of the orthosteric pocket remains predominantly determined by the bound agonist. Overall, our study elucidates the roles of conformational dynamics and plasticity in allosteric modulation of GPCRs and offers insight into the differing signalling mechanisms that can emanate from the same allosteric site.


## Results

### E130X3.41 mutations reduce activity by shifting β1AR equilibria
  Activation of the β1AR by orthosteric ligands arises from the repositioning of dynamic equilibria connecting receptor states of differing signalling activity (Fig. 1a), due to the communication of ligand information through the allosteric network of conserved microswitches. In our recent work with β1AR, we found that reverting the E130W3.41 thermostabilising mutation45 to E1303.41 (β1AR-E) (Fig. 1b) resulted in a more active receptor which occupied an active state when stimulated with a full agonist14. The 3.41 position (Supplementary Fig. 1) is adjacent to I3.40 in the PIF motif and forms part of the pocket in which the allosteric modulator AS408 binds to hβ2AR29 (Fig. 1b-c, Supplementary Fig. 2). We hypothesised that other mutations at E1303.41 would result in changes to the receptor energy landscape that might further our mechanistic insight into how an allosteric modulator binding at this site rewires the allosteric network in β1AR. Therefore, we characterised the pharmacological profiles and conformational equilibria for a set of receptor constructs with differing residues at the E1303.41 position (β1AR-X, where X is the mutation at the E1303.41 position).

  In the work in which the W3.41 thermostabilising mutation was proposed for the hβ2AR, the authors examined a range of mutations at the 3.41 position45. We chose a subset of these mutations to investigate at the β1AR, including a group of hydrophobic residues (E130I/L/M/V3.41) and a second set of aromatic residues (E130W/F/Y3.41), selected due to the presence of aromatic ring systems in AS408 (Fig. 1c).

  Ligand binding affinities for the full agonist isoprenaline to β1AR E130X3.41 variants were determined in competition assays, using receptor constructs N-terminally tagged with NanoLuc (Nluc) (Supplementary Fig. 3a) and expressed in HEK293T cells14,53,54. Low micromolar Ki binding affinities were observed for all β1AR constructs (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 3-4). Analyses of kinetic parameters revealed no significant differences in ligand dissociation or association rate constants between β1AR-E and the β1AR E130X3.41 variants (Supplementary Fig. 3b-e), and we found no significant difference in pKi between β1AR-E (pKi = 5.44) and hydrophobic E130X3.41 variants or β1AR-W (p > 0.05). β1AR-F and β1AR-Y did show significant decreases in isoprenaline equilibrium binding affinities compared with β1AR-E (pKi = 4.69, p = 0.0195; and pKi = 4.87, p = 0.0074, respectively), although still within one order of magnitude.

  β1AR-E displays primary coupling to the Gs protein subtype with secondary coupling to the Gi/o family upon agonist stimulation14,41. Therefore, we assessed the effect of E130X3.41 mutations on isoprenaline-stimulated G protein coupling using the TRUPATH BRET2 biosensor assay55 in HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 5a). β1AR-E had the greatest potency for G protein activation with respect to Gs (Fig. 1e) and representative Gi/o family members (Supplementary Fig. 5b–c). Decreases in potency were observed for hydrophobic E130X3.41 variants relative to β1AR-E, more similar to the previously characterised β1AR-W variant14, whilst β1AR-F and β1AR-Y were unable to generate full concentration-response curves.

  We determined that cell-surface expression of the E130X3.41 variants did not influence these findings, as there was no significant difference (p > 0.1) between FLAG-tagged β1AR-E and the E130X3.41 variants measured by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 6).

  We further characterised Gs protein coupling through binding affinities of the engineered mini-Gs protein56 to isoprenaline-stimulated receptors using biolayer interferometry (BLI) dose-response assays14 (Supplementary Fig. 7a–b). Receptor affinities for mini-Gs protein were reduced for all E130X3.41 variants compared to β1AR-E (Fig. 1f, Supplementary Fig. 8), mirroring the trends in potency decreases obtained in the TRUPATH assays. Mini-Gs affinities for hydrophobic E130L/I/M/V3.41 variants were intermediate between β1AR-E and β1AR-W but were over an order of magnitude (~16-72 fold) lower for β1AR-F and β1AR-Y relative to β1AR-E. These differences in mini-Gs affinities resulted from decreases in kon rate constants and increases in koff rate constants (Supplementary Fig. 7c–d, Supplementary Table 1).

  Having established that signalling through β1AR was significantly reduced by E130X3.41 mutations without substantial effects on full agonist binding, we reasoned that the decreases in potency were related to changes in communication through the allosteric network of microswitches between the ligand binding site and the intracellular G protein binding surface. Therefore, we examined the effects of E130X3.41 mutations on receptor conformational equilibria by NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 9). We first investigated the impact on the intracellular surface equilibrium, most distant from the orthosteric ligand, using single site 19F labelling coupled with 19F NMR spectroscopy.

  We recorded NMR experiments of detergent-purified β1AR-E and E130L/I/M/V3.41 variants, labelled with 19F probes at the TM7/intracellular loop (IL) 4 cysteine C3447.54 (TETC3447.54)26,27 or the introduced TM6 cysteine A282C6.27 (A282C6.27,BTFA)26,27 (Fig. 2a), in the presence of isoprenaline. Spectra were then analysed by signal deconvolution to establish the populations of receptor states14. All four hydrophobic E130X3.41 variants occupied two states matching the previously described pre-active and active states observed for isoprenaline-bound β1AR-E14 (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 10). We measured the populations of receptor states for each variant, as measured by the relative areas under the curve (AUC) for each signal. Unlike β1AR-E, which favours occupation of the active state14, the hydrophobic E130X3.41 variants predominantly occupied the pre-active state as previously observed for isoprenaline-bound β1AR-W26 (Fig. 2c).   We confirmed a corresponding response of the other regions in β1AR to the effects of the E130X3.41 mutations using 2D 1H-13C HMQC methionine NMR shift correlation experiments in combination with 13C-methyl-methionine selective isotope labelling. The 13C NMR spectra substantiated that hydrophobic E130X3.41 variants predominantly populated the pre-active state (Supplementary Fig. 11). Changes for multiple probes relative to spectra of β1AR-W, which populates the pre-active/inactive state equilibrium26,27, also corroborated the minor occupation of the active state (Supplementary Figs. 11–12).

  Together, the NMR data indicated that the hydrophobic E130X3.41 mutations shifted the relative β1AR populations of the active/pre-active state equilibrium towards the lesser active pre-active state, compared to β1AR-E (Fig. 1a). The pre-active state has a lower affinity for G proteins than the active state14,22, therefore explaining the reduced signalling observed in TRUPATH and BLI assays (Fig. 1e-f).

  We hypothesised that aromatic E130X3.41 mutations would move the agonist-bound receptor equilibrium position even further away from the active state as these variants had substantially reduced signalling capabilities. 13C NMR spectra of isoprenaline-bound β1AR-F and β1AR-Y were generally similar to β1AR-W (Fig. 2d), in agreement with the population of the pre-active/inactive state equilibrium. Previous NMR investigations of β1AR revealed correlations between ligand efficacy and chemical shift positions for multiple methionine probes14,27. These correlations were indicative of changes in the equilibrium position between the inactive and pre-active receptor states that exchanged rapidly on the NMR timescale. We observed similar chemical shift dependencies for several probes in the spectra of E130X3.41 aromatic variants, including for M15334.57, M1784.62, M2836.28, and M2966.41, that correlated with receptor signalling activity (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 13). Accordingly, we concluded that the populations of the pre-active state were smaller for β1AR-F and β1AR-Y than for β1AR-W, decreasing in the order β1AR-W > β1AR-F > β1AR-Y, whilst the inactive state population increased reciprocally. Thus, aromatic E130X3.41 mutations reduced G protein coupling by stabilising the inactive state, which results in poor signalling.

  Finally, we assessed how the E130X3.41 mutations affected receptor dynamics in triplicate 1 µs all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of isoprenaline bound β1AR starting from an activated state57. The canonical activation microswitches4, such as the TM3-TM6 hydrophobic lock, the DRY motif ionic lock, the PIF motif, and the Y5.58-Y7.53 motif, generally remained consistent with an active-like state in all systems for the duration of the simulation, although the Y5.58-Y7.53 motif being most susceptible to disruption (Supplementary Fig. 14). Interactions of the agonist were also maintained and were similar across the variants (Supplementary Fig. 15). These simulations implied that the structural transitions which resulted in the differing conformations observed by NMR occurred on a longer timescale than in our simulations, and hence we focused on the local dynamics of the TM3-TM4-TM5 allosteric pocket. Analysis of molecular interactions revealed that the variants established increased interactions with TM5 residues relative to E1303.41. Compared to E1303.41, aromatic mutants E130W3.41 and E130Y3.41 formed considerably more hydrogen bonds (7.2 ± 3.6% (standard deviation) vs 96.5 ± 1.2% and 98.4 ± 0.1%, respectively), and Van der Waals (VdW) interactions (11.5 ± 2.8% vs 98.2 ± 0.9% and 99.6 ± 0.1 %, respectively) with I2145.45 (hydrogen bonding with the carbonyl group) and VdW interactions (7.1 ± 0.6% vs 53.0 ± 2.8% and 43.3 ± 5.7%) with P2195.50. These VdW interactions were also observed for E130F3.41 and hydrophobic mutants but to a lesser degree (Fig. 2e–f, Supplementary Figs. 16, 17).

  Overall, E130X3.41 mutations reduced signalling efficacy at the full agonist-stimulated β1AR by shifting the receptor equilibria populations away from the active state in direction of the less active states, acting through the PIF motif.


### AS408 reduces β1AR signalling by binding the TM3-TM4-TM5 site
  Our MD simulations of the E130X3.41 variants revealed similar interactions in the allosteric pocket, specifically involving I2145.45 and P2195.50 with the aromatic E130X3.41 position in β1AR-W and β1AR-Y, as those reported with the small molecule allosteric modulator AS408 at the hβ2AR29. We, therefore, utilised our pharmacological and NMR methodologies to inform on the likely mechanism by which the small molecule AS408 modulates agonist-stimulated signalling at the β1AR and to compare it to the E130X3.41 variants.

  We established that AS408 reduced signalling through the G protein pathway at the β1AR in both TRUPATH (Fig. 3a) and BLI (Fig. 3b, c, Supplementary Fig. 18) assays, reminiscent of the E130X3.41 mutations. By combining these two parameters log(αβ) we can determine the overall effect of an allosteric modulator on agonist signalling. For AS408, the log(αβ) is −3.2 ± 0.5, thereby confirming AS408 as a NAM at the β1AR. The affinity of AS408 to β1AR-E (log(KB) = −6.1 ± 0.2) was similar to the hβ1AR when stimulated with noradrenaline (log(KB) = −5.6 ± 0.1)29. In the BLI assays, AS408 decreased mini-Gs binding through concentration-dependent reductions in the affinity for β1AR-E (Fig. 3b–c).   We next assessed the structural properties of AS408 binding to β1AR-E in MD all-atom simulations. The allosteric pocket is highly similar between the hβ2AR and β1AR, implying AS408 would likely bind at the same interface at β1AR-E. Therefore, we ran 1 µs simulations in triplicate starting from the activated β1AR-E structure with AS408 modelled into the TM3-TM4-TM5 allosteric pocket in the same orientation as in the hβ2AR crystal structure29. The addition of AS408 did not lead to inactivation of the receptor on the 1 µs simulation timescale as indicated by the canonical activation microswitches4, similar to the E130X3.41 variant simulations (Supplementary Fig. 14). Therefore, we focused our analyses on the AS408 binding pocket.

  Differences in AS408 binding were conceivable compared to β2AR, since the valine residues at positions 3.48, 5.45 and 5.49 in hβ2AR are larger isoleucine residues in β1AR (Supplementary Fig. 21a). Indeed, the RMSD of AS408 suggested that the initial binding pose of AS408 proved unstable (Fig. 3d), meaning that the allosteric binding pocket of β1AR did not accommodate AS408 in the same orientation as hβ2AR. The polar interaction of AS408 with the backbone of residue 5.45, which is prevalent in hβ2AR, was significantly reduced in AS408 binding to the β1AR (99.0% vs 2.6 ± 1.0%)29. Instead, AS408 predominantly occupied a pose at the same TM3-TM4-TM5 interface but shifted closer to TM4 with substantial rearrangements in the phenyl ring (Fig. 3e, Pose 1, Supplementary Fig. 21b). In this orientation AS408 established hydrogen bonds with E1303.41 alongside additional hydrophobic and VdW interactions with residues including E1303.41, I1373.48, M15334.57, I1614.45, and I1684.52 (Supplementary Fig. 22a). Consequently, AS408 had a smaller capacity to interact with P2195.50 and I2145.45 compared to the E130X3.41 variants and the equivalent AS408-hβ2AR interaction.

  Interestingly, the simulations also revealed that AS408 reversibly sampled an alternative position closer to the intracellular end of TM4 and IL2, visiting several conformations (Fig. 3e, Pose 2, and Supplementary Fig. 22b), though only in one of the three replicates (R2 in Fig. 3d). Such fluctuations in the allosteric binding pocket of β1AR between several binding poses likely resulted in the attenuated affinity for the β1AR relative to the affinity reported for hβ2AR29.

  We then experimentally substantiated that binding of AS408 at the β1AR occured at the same TM3-TM4-TM5 allosteric interface as at the hβ2AR29 by recording 13C NMR spectra of β1AR-E, examining signals for M15334.57 and an introduced I168M4.52 probe. Both residues were in close proximity to the AS408 in both poses in our MD simulations (Fig. 3f), meaning their NMR signals would respond to AS408 binding.

  The M15334.57 signal gradually shifted downfield in the 1H dimension upon increasing addition of AS408 in the NMR spectra of both the isoprenaline-bound (Fig. 3g) and the apo β1AR-E (Supplementary Fig. 23a). The gradual concentration-dependent shifts were indicative of a fast exchange regime, which was consistent with the weak µM AS408 binding affinity obtained from operational model fit of the TRUPATH assay (Supplementary Table 2). Notably, nominal concentrations up to 1000 µM were necessary to approach saturation, likely due to the poor solubility of the hydrophobic AS408 and its reduced availability following incorporation into detergent micelles (Supplementary Fig. 24). In the saturated AS408-bound receptor, the major M15334.57 signal positions were comparable between the apo and isoprenaline-bound spectra (Supplementary Fig. 23b), suggesting similar chemical environments and therefore similar AS408 orientations. A shoulder present in the isoprenaline-bound M15334.57 signal (Supplementary Fig. 23b-c) implied several AS408-bound environments were populated in exchange on the µs-ms timescale, consistent with the MD simulations which also suggested several AS408 binding poses.

  The signal of the I168M4.52 probe was generally very unresponsive towards agonist-stimulated conformational changes in the absence of AS408 (Supplementary Fig. 23d–e). In contrast, the gradual concentration-dependent changes in the I168M4.52 signal which occurred upon AS408 addition (Fig. 3h) further supported the µM affinity binding of the NAM within the proximity of the probe in the TM3-TM4-TM5 allosteric pocket. A similar upfield shift was also apparent for the apo receptor in the presence of AS408 (Supplementary Fig. 23f), indicating a similar binding orientation to the agonist-bound receptor. Finally, I168M4.52 did not respond to AS408 addition in ternary complexes of β1AR-E with isoprenaline and mini-Gs which supported the BLI-measured koff rate constant (Supplementary Fig. 18c) in suggesting that AS408 did not interact tightly with the ternary complex at this site (Supplementary Fig. 23g).

  The differences in AS408 binding orientation between the β1AR and hβ2AR, in addition to the reduced interactions with P2195.50 and I2145.45, implied that the structural mechanisms by which AS408 impacted signalling could differ between the receptors. Therefore, we next assessed the impact of AS408 on the conformational sampling of the core region of β1AR, inclusive of the orthosteric ligand binding pocket. We recorded NMR experiments for β1AR-E in the presence of increasing amounts of AS408 to obtain information on conformational changes induced by the allosteric modulator.

Thus, the AS408-bound receptor occupied a conformation in the TM7/IL4 region that was more similar in chemical environment to the pre-active state than the inactive state.

  We extended the coverage at the cytoplasm using the introduced 13C methyl-methionine probe L289M6.34, which is a reliable reporter of the local conformational environment on the intracellular side of TM614. Addition of AS408 resulted in little change to L289M6.34 for the isoprenaline-bound receptor even at high AS408 concentrations (Fig. 5c), where the signal remained in a position associated with the active-state (Supplementary Fig. 28c).   We further investigated the potential for probe dependency (a phenomenon where an allosteric modulator’s effect on a receptor differs depending on the orthosteric ligand) for AS408 at the β1AR using the β1AR-E BRET-sensor.   Overall, AS408 acted as a NAM to generate a signalling deficient receptor independently of orthosteric ligand efficacy by uncoupling ligand binding from intracellular activation.


### AS408 and E130X3.41 act by independent allosteric pathways
  The adoption of an alternative receptor conformation, driven by uncoupling of the allosteric network relating the orthosteric site to the intracellular surface of β1AR, was distinct to AS408. However, the dynamic nature of agonist-bound β1AR-E impeded a meticulous analysis of the receptor core region upon AS408 binding as the signals for the NMR probes in this region remained unfavourably broadened by conformational exchange (Fig. 4a). Since the E130X3.41 mutations repositioned the conformational equilibria connecting the same states also sampled by β1AR-E, we questioned whether we could utilise these differences in equilibria positions inherent to the E130X3.41 mutations to further ascertain the impact of AS408 binding on the dynamic core region of β1AR by NMR.

  The 3.41 position has been suggested as important in the binding of AS408 to the hβ2AR since E1223.41 mutations affected the extent to which AS408 reduced signalling by removal of a hydrogen bond between E3.41 and AS40829. The E1303.41 in β1AR also formed substantial interactions with AS408 in Pose 1 (Fig. 3e). Therefore, we first assessed the effects of AS408 on G protein coupling to β1AR E130X3.41 variants. Titration of AS408 decreased the activity and potency of E130X3.41 variants in TRUPATH assays with no substantial difference in the effects of AS408 between the variants and β1AR-E (Supplementary Fig. 32). These data implied that changing the side chain at the E1303.41 position did not have a major impact on AS408 binding to β1AR. Consequently, we leveraged the shift in receptor state equilibria positions associated with the E130X3.41 mutations to observe how the orthosteric ligand and AS408 simultaneously influenced the allosteric network in this region by NMR.

## Discussion
  The plasticity and conformational flexibility of GPCRs are critical properties which influence the effects of drugs on signalling. Modulating the energy landscapes of GPCRs38–40,65 has been rigorously applied towards a better understanding of how orthosteric ligands work14,16,18,19,24,26–28. Here we endeavoured to investigate the role of manipulating receptor dynamics in GPCR allostery through alterations in the chemical environment of an allosteric pocket, using a combination of pharmacological assays, MD simulations, and biophysical and NMR conformational analyses.

  Emulating the effects of allosteric drug binding through E130X3.41 mutations in a TM3-TM4-TM5 allosteric pocket of β1AR resulted in changes to the equilibrium populations of previously characterised receptor states, favouring the pre-active and inactive states (Figs. 1–2). Due to the different G protein coupling efficacies of each of these states in the β1AR equilibria (Fig. 1a), the E130X3.41 variants behaved as a rheostat for G protein signalling (Fig. 1e). The conformational equilibria positions were altered in an analogous manner to the impact of orthosteric partial agonists14,16,26–28, where conformations were altered through coordinated changes within the proposed framework of receptor activation31–34,36. Hence, the mutations at X3.41 regulated transduction of ligand information in a residue-type dependent manner by effectively imparting fractional efficacy effects on the full agonist to control β1AR signalling (Fig. 8a), resulting in the changes in maximal response and potency observed in functional data (Fig. 1e). Our data underpin the conformational selection of orthosteric ligand-stabilised functional states as a fundamental strategy by which agonist-dependent signalling can be modulated by allosteric therapeutics, supporting observations at the µ-opioid receptor31, the neurotensin receptor33, the cannabinoid receptor32, and the β1AR36.Fig. 8Mechanisms of allosteric modulation at the β1AR.a E130X3.41 mutations shift orthosteric agonist-stabilised conformational equilibria, affecting the extent to which ligand efficacy information is transduced through the allosteric network of microswitches in β1AR. Shifts in equilibria away from the active state (A) reduce G protein coupling efficiency in a side chain-dependent rheostat. Mutations variably stabilise receptor states, favouring stabilisation of the pre-active state (P) in receptors with hydrophobic E130X3.41 mutations (left) or stabilisation of inactive states (I) in receptors with aromatic mutations (right). b The NAM AS408 uncouples ligand efficacy information in the orthosteric pocket from the intracellular surface, resulting in a non-productive conformation (pink) with poor G protein coupling efficiency. c The influences of orthosteric agonist and allosteric ligand AS408 on receptor conformational dynamics. Below the PIF motif, AS408 impacts the TM5-TM6 interface and uncouples this region from the agonist efficacy information in the allosteric activation network. Above the PIF motif, the conformational dynamics are predominantly impacted by the orthosteric agonist with minor effects from AS408. Around the PIF motif, influences of both the orthosteric agonist and the NAM AS408 affect the conformational equilibrium.

  Our MD simulations of the TM3-TM4-TM5 allosteric pocket indicated that the highly inactivating aromatic E130X3.41 variants had increased interactions between the E130X3.41 position and neighbouring residues including I2145.45 and P2195.50 in the conserved P5.50-I3.40-F6.44 microswitch (Fig. 2e–f). We inferred that the resulting changes to the receptor conformational landscape were communicated to the rest of the allosteric activation pathway via the PIF motif, to concertedly stabilise pre-active or inactive states. This mechanism was similar to the action of the small molecule modulator AS408 at the hβ2AR29.

  In contrast, despite the overall similarity in the allosteric pocket’s structure and sequence between hβ2AR and β1AR, we found that AS408 acted via a discrete mechanism by stabilisation of alternative β1AR conformations that were distinct from those stabilised in E130X3.41 variants or by ligands used in previous studies14,26,27. Thus, we attributed the substantial decreases in G protein coupling (Fig. 3a) to the occupation of this ‘non-productive’ conformation, which had a low affinity for G proteins (Fig. 8b). Stabilisation of energy minima outside the equilibria of endogenous states has also been noted at the muscarinic M2 receptor upon binding of the PAM LY211962037, supporting this as another available mechanism for allosteric modulators.

  AS408 predominantly acted on the intracellular conformation with far reaching allosteric effects across the G protein binding surface (Figs. 5–6). Outward displacement of TM6 is an essential conformational rearrangement for efficient G protein coupling to Class A GPCRs3. The considerable structural changes in TM6 (Fig. 5) indicated AS408 disrupted these key transitions and lead to the observed reduction in β1AR signalling. Previous studies have found the intracellular side of β1AR to be more loosely coupled to the orthosteric pocket in the agonist-bound active state14,22. This inherent plasticity was exploited by AS408 to potentiate the uncoupling of the intracellular surface from the orthosteric pocket, as the non-productive conformation showed independence from the presence of ligand in the orthosteric pocket (Figs. 5–6). In contrast, the influence of orthosteric ligands from differing classes, including agonist (Figs. 4,7) and antagonist (Supplementary Fig. 25), remained apparent in the strongly coupled receptor core and orthosteric pocket. Accordingly, the effective magnitude of the allosteric changes induced by AS408 varied across the receptor (Fig. 8c) in contrast to the E130X3.41 mutations which had a coordinated effect on equilibria populations.

  Although our investigations have utilised an avian GPCR, our data demonstrate the breadth in both binding poses and mechanisms available to allosteric modulators at GPCRs. The binding poses of AS408 at β1AR revealed in MD simulations (Fig. 3e) were distinct relative to the AS408 interaction with hβ2AR29 and likely accounted for the disparate mechanisms of allosteric modulation. The substantial reductions in interactions with residues around the PIF motif, including with I2145.45 and P2195.50 (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 22), meant that AS408 could not impart a stabilising effect which could be communicated through the allosteric activation network. Interestingly, solution studies of β1AR have indicated the TM5 region in the two turns below the PIF motif is amongst the most dynamic in the active state of β1AR22, and includes residues which contact AS408 in binding pose 1 (Supplementary Fig. 36). AS408 may impart its modulator activity by directly contacting this highly dynamic region on TM5 which could explain the primary localisation of AS408-induced conformational rearrangements at the intracellular surface.

  The reorientation of AS408 in the TM3-TM4-TM5 allosteric pocket in MD simulations of β1AR resulted in altered interactions relative to AS408-bound hβ2AR, including between the phenyl ring and the 3.48 position. The TM3-TM4-TM5 allosteric pocket is generally well conserved between the β1AR, hβ1AR, and hβ2AR (Supplementary Table 4), but notably the 3.48 position has bulkier Ile and Leu residues in β1AR and hβ1AR, respectively, compared to a smaller Val residue in hβ2AR. This difference may favour the differing AS408 binding pose at the β1AR to reduce steric clashes between the AS408 phenyl ring and the larger Ile side chain (Supplementary Fig. 21a) and consequently AS408 may also reorient similarly at hβ1AR. However, differences in overall receptor dynamics between β1AR and hβ1AR44 could also contribute to differing mechanisms.

  Overall, our study emphasises not only the importance of conformational variability in allosteric modulation of GPCR signalling, but also the synergy of orthogonal experimental approaches for mechanistic studies into receptor modulation. It is currently unclear if other GPCR therapeutics also exhibit several binding poses when bound to different human receptors that may increase off-target interactions. Our multi-dimensional approach presents an attractive opportunity for more effective design of subtype-selective modulators within this emerging field13, as information regarding the conformational plasticity of drug-receptor complexes may aid in concentrating structure guided screening efforts to improve selective targeting of allosteric sites in GPCRs.

  In summary, we provide insight into the structural mechanisms of negative allosteric modulation at the β1AR, and the variable conformational dynamics by which the GPCR energy landscape can be altered. The mechanistic differences between E130X3.41 and AS408 demonstrate how varying influences in the same allosteric pocket can differentially affect conformational equilibria.


## Methods

### β1AR constructs—Biophysics
  The β1AR constructs were based on the minimally thermostabilised β1AR construct27. The β1AR-E construct differed from wild type turkey (Meleagris gallopavo) β1AR as described previously14,26,27. The N-terminus (Δ3-32), C-terminus (Δ368-483) and IL3 (Δ244-271) were truncated66. Expression yields were improved by the single residue mutation C116L3.27 67. The palmitoylation site C358A8.59 was removed to improve receptor purification. Thermostabilising mutations were introduced at R68S1.59, F327A7.37 and F338A7.4868. Additional methionine mutations M44L1.35, M48L1.39, M179L4.63, and M281A6.26 were introduced to reduce spectral crowding27. The E1303.41 position was mutated in E130X3.41 variants to Val, Met, Ile, Leu, Trp, Phe, or Tyr for use in BLI assays and NMR experiments. 1H-13C NMR experiments examining TM6 used the mutation L289M6.34, which has been previously characterised14,17. An additional methionine I168M4.52 was introduced to probe the TM3-TM4-TM5 allosteric site. See Supplementary Fig. 1a for an overview of the receptor construct.

  The 19F NMR experiments utilised modified constructs for site specific labelling with small molecule 19F probes26. The C85V2.48 and C163L4.47 mutations were made to remove off-target labelling. 19F NMR experiments examining TM7 using TETC3447.54 were labelled at the native C3447.54 residue and experiments examining TM6 with A282CBTFA,6.27 used constructs with further C344S7.54 and A282C6.27 mutations. See Supplementary Fig. 1b.

  BLI experiments used receptor constructs with an avi-tag sequence (GLNDIFEAQKIEWHE) introduced at the receptor N-terminus between M1 and G2 via mutagenesis. Agonist affinity measurements used receptor constructs with a FLAG-Nluc sequence inserted between M1 and G2. Mutations were introduced using site directed mutagenesis via PCR with Q5 polymerase (New England Biolabs). PCR products were phosphorylated with PNK (Thermo Fisher Scientific), ligated with T4 ligase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the template was removed via Dpn1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) digestion. Ligated PCR products were transformed into E. coli (DH5α, New England Biolabs) and amplified plasmids were purified by PureLink miniprep (Invitrogen). The mutation primers (Merck – Custom Oligos) used for mutagenesis in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 5.


### β1AR constructs—pharmacology
  The β1AR-E and β1AR-W constructs expressed using a pcDNA3.1 vector and used in pharmacological experiments (TRUPATH and ligand binding) were obtained as reported in Jones et al.14. Mutants (Val, Met, Leu, Ile, Phe, or Tyr) of these constructs were obtained for use in pharmacological assays by site directed mutagenesis from E130W3.41 using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 5.

  The turkey β1AR BRET biosensor was based on the β1AR-E sequence with additions as shown in Supplementary Fig. 29a. The biosensor sequence was synthesised by Twist Bioscience (San Francisco, CA) and subcloned into pcDNA3.1. The construct sequence was checked for fidelity through Sanger Sequencing.


### β1AR expression and purification
  β1AR E130X3.41 constructs were expressed and purified in lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG) as previously described14,26,27. In brief, Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) cells (Thermofisher, 12659017) were transfected with pBacPAK8 plasmids containing the relevant β1AR construct and flashBac (Oxford Expression Technologies Ltd) expression vector using CellFectin II reagent (Gibco) to generate baculoviral particles. Sequential infections of increasing volume were carried out to amplify the baculovirus. Sf9 cells in mid-log phase were diluted to 3 × 106 mL−1 and receptor was expressed by infection with viral stocks at a 1:200 dilution. 13C-methyl-methionine labelled receptor was expressed in ESF 921 ∆ methionine deficient media (Expression Systems) supplemented with penicillin (50 U mL−1), streptomycin (50 μg mL−1), and amphotericin B (50 μg mL−1). For 13C NMR, 13C-methyl-methionine was added at 0.25 g L−1 4 h post baculoviral infection and cell density was diluted to 1.5 × 106 mL−1. Unlabelled receptor was expressed at 1.5 × 106 mL−1 in Insect-XPRESS with L-glutamine (Lonza) supplemented with 5% heat inactivated FBS (Sigma-Aldrich), and the above antibiotics. Infected cells were incubated at 27 °C with shaking for 48 h and harvested via centrifugation at 4000 g. Cells were washed once with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged before storage at −20 °C until receptor purification.

  All purification steps were performed at 4 °C unless otherwise stated. Cells were thawed at RT and resuspended at 4 °C in solubilisation buffer (20 mM TrisHCl, pH 8.0, 350 mM NaCl, 3 mM imidazole, 1.5% LMNG, and 1x protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche)) for 1 h with constant stirring. Lysed cells were centrifuged at 75,000 g for 45 min. The supernatant was collected and sonicated (Fisherbrand 505) on ice at 25% amplitude for a total of 2 min, with pulse on and off times of 5 s. Clarified lysates were applied at 1 mL min−1 to a 5 mL NiNTA column (Cytiva) and washed in a buffer containing 20 mM TrisHCl, pH 8.0, 350 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, and 0.02% LMNG. Receptor was eluted from the column in elution buffer (20 mM TrisHCl, pH 8.0, 350 mM NaCl, 250 mM imidazole, and 0.02% LMNG). The elution fractions were pooled and concentrated with a 50 kDa MWCO Amicon concentrator. Receptor was applied to a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated in gel filtration buffer (20 mM TrisHCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl and 0.02% LMNG) and the resulting peak fractions were pooled and concentrated, generating receptor samples of >95% purity.


### 19F labelling
  Receptors were purified by NiNTA affinity chromatography as above, and then buffer exchanged 10-fold into labelling buffer (20 mM TrisHCl, pH 8.0, 350 mM NaCl, and 0.02% LMNG). A282C6.27 was labelled with 3-bromo-1,1,1-trifluoroacetone (BTFA) by adjusting the receptor concentration to 20 μM and incubating the sample at 4 °C overnight in the presence of 200 μM BTFA (Sigma) and 50 μM glutathione disulfide (GSSG)14. Labelled samples were then buffer exchanged 10-fold into labelling buffer. C3447.54 was labelled with 2,2,2-trifluoroethanethiol (TET)14,26. The receptor was buffer exchanged into labelling buffer and concentrated to 10 μM before being incubated at 4 °C with 100 μM Aldrithiol-4™ (Sigma) and 50 μM GSSG for 10 min. The receptor was buffer exchanged 10-fold into labelling buffer, and the receptor was concentrated to 10 μM and incubated at 4 °C with 100 μM TET and 50 μM GSSG for 15 min. The labelled sample was buffer exchanged once more into labelling buffer to remove excess TET label. 19F labelled proteins were concentrated to 1 mL and applied to a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column running with gel filtration buffer (20 mM TrisHCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.02% LMNG). The resulting peak fractions containing receptor were pooled and concentrated for NMR experiments.


### Mini-G expression and purification
  The mini-Gs protein56 used here was mini-Gs 393 with an additional C96A mutation made to reduce disulfide mediated dimerisation14. Mini-Gs was expressed and purified as previously described14 in BL21-RIL cells (New England Biolabs). Starter cultures were grown overnight in LB supplemented with ampicillin (50 μg mL−1) and used to inoculate 500 mL of LB supplemented with ampicillin and 10% glycerol in 2 L baffled flasks. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm until an OD600 of 0.6. Expression was induced with 1 mM IPTG, and the culture was incubated at 25 °C overnight with shaking. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 g for 15 min. Cell pellets were resuspended in PBS and centrifuged before short term storage at −20 °C. All purification steps were performed at 4 °C unless otherwise stated. Cell pellets were thawed and resuspended into lysis buffer (40 mM TrisHCl, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, 5 mM MgCl2, 50 μM GDP, 1 mM PMSF, 100 μM DDT, 50 μg mL−1 lysozyme, and 1 × Roche protease inhibitor cocktail). The cells were lysed with 3 passes through an EmulsiFlex-C5 (Avestin) operating at ~500 bar. Lysates were clarified at 75,000 g for 30 min and the supernatant was sonicated (Fisherbrand 505) on ice at 40% amplitude for a total of 2 min, pulsing with on and off times of 5 s. The lysate was passed over a 5 mL NiNTA column at 3 mL min−1. The column was washed in 40 mM TrisHCl, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, 5 mM MgCl2, and 50 μM GDP, and then washed in 20 mM TrisHCl, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 40 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, 1 mM MgCl2, and 50 μM GDP. The mini-Gs was eluted in 20 mM TrisHCl, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, 1 mM MgCl2, and 50 μM GDP. Peak fractions were pooled and supplemented with 5 mM TCEP and His tagged tobacco etch virus protease (TEV, made in-house) at a 1:30 (mg TEV:mg mini-Gs) dilution. The sample was placed in a 3.5 kDa cutoff dialysis membrane (Spectrum Labs) and dialysed against 1 L of dialysis buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 μM GDP) for 20 h, and then dialysed for 4 h in 1 L of fresh dialysis buffer. The sample was supplemented with 20 mM imidazole and passed over a gravity flow column packed with 5 mL of NiNTA resin (QIAGEN). The column was washed with 5 mL of dialysis buffer plus 20 mM imidazole, and the flow-through was collected. The mini-G protein was concentrated to 2.5 mL using a 10 kDa MWCO concentrator and purified by size exclusion chromatography on a HiPrep 16/60 sephacryl S200 HR column (Cytiva) running in 20 mM TrisHCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, and 1 μM GDP. Peak fractions containing mini-Gs were pooled and concentrated to ~1.7 mM.


### NMR experiments
  NMR samples containing 13C-methyl-methionine labelled or 19F labelled β1AR at 20–60 μM in 20 mM TrisHCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl and 0.02% LMNG were supplemented with 5% D2O and prepared in 5 mm Shigemi tubes. Receptor was prepared in the apo form, with 1 mM isoprenaline (Sigma), or 1 mM propranolol (Sigma) to saturate in the ligand-bound receptor population and ensure changes to NMR spectra reflected conformational exchange of the ligand-bound states. Sodium ascorbate (2 mM) was added to decrease ligand oxidation rates. Unless otherwise stated, apo spectra were recorded at 298 K due to the low stability of β1AR-E in the absence of orthosteric ligand. Mini-Gs was added at two molar equivalents with 15 mM MgCl2. The negative allosteric modulator AS408 (Biosynth Ltd) was dissolved in deuterated DMSO to 100 mM, see figures for concentrations used. Samples were incubated with AS408 for 20 min prior to NMR experiments.

  1H-13C 2D HMQC NMR experiments were performed on a Bruker Avance III 800 spectrometer (1H 800.13 MHz) running TopSpin 3.1 and equipped with a 5 mm TXI HCN/z cryoprobe. NMR spectra were recorded at 308 K or 298 K using the XL-ALSOFAST sequence69 with experiments typically run with 64 scans (~2 h), using 100 increment pairs in the indirect dimension (t1H 50 ms, t13C 25 ms) and with a spectral width of 10,000 Hz (offset: 3750 Hz) in the direct dimension and 4000 Hz (offset: 3420.27 Hz) in the indirect dimension. Experiments recorded for β1AR-E I168M4.52 were recorded using the above pulse programme at 308 K or 298 K using a Bruker Avance III 600 spectrometer (1H 600.13 MHz) running TopSpin 3.1 and equipped with a 5 mm QCI HFCN/z cryoprobe. Experiments were typically run with 64 scans (~1.5 h) using 76 increment pairs in the indirect dimension (t1H 50 ms, t13C 25 ms) and with a spectral width of 10,000 Hz (offset: 2818 Hz) in the direct dimension and 3012 Hz (offset: 2565.34 Hz) in the indirect dimension.

  Multiple experiments were co-added to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. Data were processed in Azara v2.8 (W. Boucher) and analysed using CCPN Analysis V270.

  19F 1D NMR spectra were recorded at 298 K using a Bruker Avance III 600 spectrometer running TopSpin 3.1 and equipped with a 5 mm QCI HFCN/z cryoprobe (19F 564.64 MHz). 19F chemical shifts were referenced against an internal standard of 2 μM trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) at −75.22 ppm. Pulse acquire experiments were run recording 2560 complex points (50 ms) with a spectral width of 50,000 Hz (offset: −50,821.80 Hz) and consisting of 5000 to 25,000 scans with a recycling time of 1 s, which lasted between 1 and 6 h. Spectra were recorded at 298 K to increase the chemical shift difference between pre-active and active states. Multiple experiments were co-added to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. All 1D FIDs were apodized with 20 Hz line broadening and zero-filled to 64k points prior to FFT using Topspin 4.4.1. All signals were deconvoluted as Lorentzian lines for the measurement of linewidths at half height and the comparison of R2 values. Overlapping signals were deconvoluted using in-house written software14,26. Fitted curves produced residuals that deviated from the baseline no more than the expected noise level.


### In cell ligand affinity measurements
  HEK293T cells (ATCC, CRL-3216) were transiently transfected with the relevant FLAG-Nluc-β1AR construct. Cells were grown overnight, harvested and seeded at 10,000 cells per well onto white 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One). Media was removed after 24 h before addition of 90 μl PBS containing 0.49 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 0.9 mM CaCl2·2H2O, 0.1% BSA and 1:20,000 Nano-Glo® Substrate (Promega). The plate was incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Cells were stimulated with varying concentrations of (S)-propranolol-red (CA200689, Hello Bio), from 0.1 nM to 300 nM, in the absence or presence of 10 μM unlabelled propranolol, and plates were read immediately using a PHERAstar microplate reader (BMG Labtech, software version 1.60 R4, firmware version 1.33) with a NanoBRET filter module. BRET ratios (Em. λ > 610 nm/Em. λ 460 nm) corrected to vehicle-treated cells were used for subsequent analysis. Specific binding of each concentration of (S)-propranolol-red was determined by subtracting the vehicle-corrected, ΔBRET values for non-specific binding (with 10 µM propranolol) from total binding. Kinetic data were fit using the ‘Association kinetics – Two or more conc. of hot’ model in GraphPad Prism 10.5 to determine kon and koff values. Equilibrium specific binding data, using the BRET ratio at ten minutes, were fitted to the one-site specific binding model in GraphPad Prism 10.5 to calculate the KD for (S)-propranolol-red at each FLAG-Nluc-β1AR E130X3.41 mutant. Differences between (S)-propranolol-red binding parameters were assessed using ordinary one-way ANOVA, using Dunnett’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons and presented as described in Curtis et al.71.

  The pKi for isoprenaline at each FLAG-Nluc-β1AR E130X3.41 construct was determined by transfecting cells and seeding as described above. After 10 min incubation of cells in 90 μl PBS containing 0.49 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 0.9 mM CaCl2·2H2O, 0.1% BSA and 1:20,000 Nano-Glo® Substrate, cells were stimulated with varying concentrations of isoprenaline, from 10 nM to 1 mM, in the presence of 50 nM (S)-propranolol-red. Emission was measured using the NanoBRET module of a PHERAstar microplate reader, and the BRET ratio (Em. λ > 610 nm/Em. λ 460 nm) corrected to cells treated with 100 μM isoprenaline. Kinetic ΔBRET values were fit using the ‘Kinetics of competitive binding’model in GraphPad Prism 10.5, to determine kon and koff values, using the mean values for CA200689. Equilibrium affinity (pKi) values were determined using the ‘One site – fit Ki’ model and are represented as mean of three separate experiments ± SEM. Differences between isoprenaline affinity parameters were assessed using ordinary one-way ANOVA, using Dunnett’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons and presented as described in Curtis et al.71.

  For assays involving AS408, cells were pre-incubated with 0, 5, 10, 25, 50 or 100 µM AS408 alongside NanoGlo® for 15 minutes before the addition of 60 nM (S)-propranolol-red and isoprenaline. The kinetic parameters kon and koff were calculated for isoprenaline in the presence of each AS408 concentration based on the corresponding values for (S)-propranolol-red. Note, minor compound interference was observed at high concentrations of AS408 (50-100 µM).


### TRUPATH assays
  HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with the relevant β1AR E130X3.41 construct, each Gα-Rluc8, and appropriate Gβ and Gγ55, at a ratio of 1:1:1:1. After 24 h, cells were harvested and seeded onto white 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One) at a density of 50,000 cells per well. Media was removed after 24 h, and cells washed with HBSS before addition of 90 μl HBSS containing 20 mM HEPES, 0.1% BSA, adjusted to pH 7.4, and 11.1 μM coelenterazine 400a (Nanolight Technology, USA). The plate was then incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Cells were stimulated with varying concentrations of isoprenaline, from 10 pM to 10 μM, and emission measured at 400 nm and 515 nm for 20 min, every 60 s. Emission measurements were made using BMG Labtech PHERAstar (software version 1.60 R4, firmware version 1.33). The BRET ratio (Em. λ 515 nm/Em. λ 460 nm) was corrected to vehicle-treated cells and the area under the resulting curve used to produce the concentration-response curves shown. Concentration-response curves were fitted to the 3-parameter logistic equation to determine Emax and pEC50 values for isoprenaline at each β1AR E130X3.41 mutant.

  For assays involving AS408, cells were pre-incubated with this compound alongside coelentrazine 400a for 15 minutes before the addition of isoprenaline. To account for changes in basal activity of β1AR, the baselines subtracted from BRET ratios were obtained from cells transfected with pcDNA3.1 containing no receptor alongside TRUPATH constructs that were subsequently treated identically to those expressing β1AR.


### Conformational Biosensor assay
  HEK293A (a gift from Professor David Yule, Thermofisher, R70507) cells stably expressing the β1AR-E BRET biosensor were seeded at 30,000 cells per well in white 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One) and grown for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in humidified conditions. Assays for orthosteric ligand activity at the β1AR-E BRET biosensor were carried out in PBS containing 0.1% BSA, 0.5 mM MgCl2 and 0.9 mM CaCl2. Cells were incubated with AS408 and NanoGlo® substrate (1:20,000) for 15 minutes at room temperature and in the dark prior to the addition of orthosteric ligands. Immediately after orthosteric ligand addition, Nano luciferase luminescence (Em. λ 410-480nm) and citrine fluorescence (Em. λ 530-540nm) were measured every 150 seconds on a CLARIOstar Plus® microplate reader (BMG Labtech, UK, SMART control version 6.20, Mars version 4.20). Data were analysed in GraphPad Prism 10.5 by calculating the BRET ratio (fluorescence/luminescence) for each well and timepoint, then finding the area between each curve and the baseline (AUC), as demonstrated by treatment with vehicle (Supplementary Fig. 29b). The resulting plot of AUC over logarithmic isoprenaline concentration was fitted to the 3-parameter logistic equations and represented as the mean of n ≥ 3 independent biological replicates ± SEM. In assays including AS408, minor compound interference was observed at high concentrations of AS408 (50-100 µM).

  For confocal microscopy, HEK293A cells transiently expressing the β1AR-E BRET biosensor were plated onto a poly-D-lysine-coated coverslip and mounted onto an “Attofluor” holder (Molecular Probes, Leiden, The Netherlands). The cellular location of the labelled receptors was monitored by live cell confocal microscopy performed on a Zeiss LSM 800 meta system (Carl Zeiss, Oberkchen, Germany). Zeiss Zen Blue 2 software (2.1) was used for data acquisition and analysis. Images were taken with an oil-immersion 63x lens using the factory settings for YFP.

### Flow cytometry
  HEK293T cells were transfected with FLAG-Nluc-β1AR E130X3.41 constructs for 48 hours prior to measurement of surface expression. 500,000 cells (with viability > 85%) were harvested and washed twice with PBS supplemented with 1% BSA and 0.03% sodium azide (FACS buffer). ### Biolayer interferometry (BLI) measurements
  Binding affinity assays were conducted as previously described14. Briefly, β1AR E130X3.41 proteins were immobilised to streptavidin (SA) coated bio-sensor tips (Octet) through an N-terminal avi-tag on the receptor. Purified avi-tagged receptors were diluted to ~40 μM. The avi-tag was labelled with biotin by 20 h incubation at 4 °C with 10 mM ATP, 10 mM Mg acetate, 50 μM biotin and 1-2 μM GST-BirA (prepared in house). Receptors were repurified and buffer exchanged via SEC using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column running in 20 mM TrisHCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.02% LMNG. BLI binding assays were performed in BLI buffer (20 mM TrisHCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 15 mM MgCl2, 0.02% LMNG, and 0.1% BSA) and assay mixtures were prepared in black 96-well plates using sample volumes of 200 μL. Experiments were run on an 8-channel Octet® R8 instrument (Sartorius) using Data Acquisition V11.0.0.64 (Pall ForteBio LLC), at 25 °C shaking at 400 rpm. SA coated biosensors were pre-hydrated in BLI buffer for a minimum of 10 min before experiments. Baseline signals were measured in BLI buffer for 120 s before the receptor (300 nM) was loaded onto the SA tips with an association time of 600 s. Non-specific binding was removed by dissociation into BLI buffer for 600 s. Receptor-loaded tips were transferred to BLI buffer containing orthosteric ligand (500 μM isoprenaline or propranolol, and 1 mM Na ascorbate) for 300 s. Association steps into BLI buffer plus agonist and varying binding partner concentrations were 600 s. Dissociation steps back in BLI buffer plus agonist were 600–900 s.

  Dose-response experiments for mini-Gs binding affinities to E130X3.41 mutants were recorded using a mini-Gs dilution series from 31 nM to 500 nM, using individual biosensors for each concentration and referencing to a ligand only biosensor (0 nM mini-Gs) with immobilised receptor (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Single point measurements were conducted for β1AR-Y, run in triplicate using n = 3 separate tips and a reference channel with loading steps using un-biotinylated avi-tagged receptor were used to subtract nonspecific binding.

  Experiments with AS408 used agonist and NAM association steps of 1200 s to ensure ligand association reached equilibrium. All experiments were conducted with 1 mM sodium ascorbate, including apo experiments. Concentration dependence data were obtained with increasing AS408 concentrations (range of 0–100 µM AS408, see experiments for specific concentrations of AS408 used) utilising dose-response experiments with a concentration series of 31.25 nM to 500 nM mini-Gs. Experiments used a constant corrected DMSO concentration of 0.1%, including 0 µM AS408. Experiments were referenced against a ligand and NAM only biosensor (0 nM mini-Gs) with immobilised receptor.

  BLI data was analysed on Data Analysis V11.0.0.4 (Pall ForteBio LLC) using reference channel subtraction and Savitzsky–Golay filtering. For dose-response experiments, responses were used to calculate KD, kon, and koff values using a 1:1 global fitting with Rmax unlinked by sensors. Single point measurements were analysed assuming monophasic 1:1 association and dissociation curves with local partial fitting. Concentration dependence plots for AS408 were produced using GraphPad Prism 10.5. IC50 values were calculated from non-linear regression curves using a Variable slope (3-parameter) fit and with Hill constants set to −1. See Jones et al.14, for additional information on BLI assays and control experiments.


### MD simulations
  The isoprenaline and Gs protein-bound β1AR ternary complex was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 7JJO)57. The Gs protein and nanobody Nb35 were removed, and Modeller73 was used to replace missing residues and introduce mutations according to the β1AR-E construct (Supplementary Fig. 1a), excluding any N-terminal tags or the C-terminal octa-His tag. The structure was mutated at E1303.41 via the rotamers tool in ChimeraX74 to generate the E130X3.41 variants and aligned to the orientation of the reference 7JJO structure from the Orientations of proteins in the membrane (OPM) database75. The receptor structures were processed using CHARMM-GUI76. The N- and C-termini were capped with acetyl (ACE) and amidated (CT2) patches, respectively. Hydrogen atoms were added, and amino acids were kept in their standard protonation state at pH of 7.0 with the exception of D872.50, E1303.41, and D1383.49 being protonated in agreement with previous studies stating their importance in the active state77. The disulphide bonds (C114-C199, C192-C198) were maintained. Isoprenaline was maintained in the orthosteric pocket. In simulations with AS408, the initial AS408 pose was obtained by aligning the crystal structure of the ligand with hβ2AR (PDB ID: 6OBA)29 to the curated β1AR-E structure. Ligand parameterisation and force field topology were obtained via CGenFF and ParamChem through CHARMM-GUI78. The protein model was embedded in an in-vivo mimetic membrane79 (Supplementary Table 6). The embedded protein was inserted into a ~ 95 Å (x and y) rectangular simulation box with a height of ~140 Å (z), which was then filled with ~26,000 TIP3P80 water molecules. NaCl ions were added to neutralise the charge and achieve a final salt concentration of 150 mM. Simulation boxes contained ~110,000 atoms overall.

  Each system was energy-minimised with the steepest descent algorithm. The system was equilibrated in six steps, gradually reducing restraints on the protein side chains, backbone, and lipid molecules, following a protocol similar to the default procedure used by the CHARMM-GUI Membrane Builder (Supplementary Table 7) for a total equilibration time of 50 ns. The first two equilibration steps were performed in the NVT ensemble with a time step of 1 fs while the third step was performed in the NPT ensemble with a 1 fs time step. The remaining steps and production run were continued in the NPT ensemble with a 2 fs time step. Bonds involving hydrogen were constrained with the LINCS algorithm81. A Verlet cutoff scheme was used with a neighbour list update frequency of 20 steps and a list cutoff of 1.2 nm. Van der Waals interactions were treated with a cutoff scheme using a force-switch modifier with a switch distance of 1.0 nm and a cutoff of 1.2 nm. Electrostatic interactions were calculated using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method with a real-space cutoff of 1.2 nm. The temperature was maintained at 310.15 K using the v-rescale thermostat with a coupling time constant of 1.0 ps, applied independently to the solute, membrane, and solvent groups. In the NPT ensemble, pressure was maintained at 1.0 bar using the C-rescale barostat with semi-isotropic pressure coupling, a time constant of 5.0 ps, and a compressibility of 4.5 × 10⁻⁵ bar⁻¹. All systems were simulated using GROMACS version 2024.682 and the CHARMM36/CGenFF force field combination78,83. Production runs were conducted for 1000 ns. Three independent MD simulation replicates were run, together generating 3 µs data for each system (27 µs of data in total). Analysis of MD trajectories was performed with MDAnalysis v2.10.084, ProLIF v2.1.085 was used to extract molecular interactions, mdciao v1.0.086 was used to generate the flareplots, and molecular visualisation was performed using ChimeraX v1.10.174.
