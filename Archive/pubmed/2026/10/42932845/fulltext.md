# EquiDesign: a versatile computational framework for de novo protein sequence design


## Abstract
  Designing amino acid sequences that uniquely fold into target three-dimensional conformations defines the protein inverse folding problem, a formidable bottleneck in de novo protein design. Current computational paradigms, including deep learning-based approaches, often struggle to reconcile high sequence accuracy with structural stability, computational efficiency, and generalizability across diverse protein families. Here, we present EquiDesign, a deep learning framework that surmounts these constraints via a novel multistep Equiformer-enhanced architecture. By disentangling geometric features into invariant scalars and equivariant vectors, EquiDesign preserves the intrinsic directional topology of the protein backbone during message passing. This is achieved through spatial K-nearest neighbor graph construction and geometric attention mechanisms that hierarchically fuse multiscale contextual information, yielding a physically grounded representation of protein conformations. Extensive benchmarking demonstrates that EquiDesign achieves superior sequence recovery on standard and challenging test sets compared with state-of-the-art methods. Critically, the generated sequences exhibit exceptional structural fidelity, folding stability, and sequence diversity, as validated by comprehensive controls against training-set memorization, compositional bias, and physical energy landscapes, alongside targeted diversity sampling. These results establish EquiDesign as a robust, scalable, and generalizable inverse folding solution, driven by SE(3)-equivariant encoding and multiscale geometric attention, thus propelling de novo protein design toward unprecedented structural precision.


## Introduction
  Proteins are the molecular engines of life, executing a vast array of catalytic, regulatory, and structural functions dictated by their precisely folded three-dimensional (3D) conformations [1–3]. The de novo design of protein sequences that spontaneously fold into target native-like structures (commonly referred to as the protein inverse folding problem) remains a grand challenge in structural biology and synthetic biology, with transformative implications for therapeutics, industrial enzymes, and biosensors [4, 5]. feature extraction module is adapted

  Historically, conventional computational approaches bifurcated into physics-based and statistics-based strategies. Physics-based methods, rooted in molecular mechanics, typically formulate inverse folding as a combinatorial energy minimization problem, relying on physical force fields and conformational sampling to identify stabilizing sequences for a fixed backbone [6, 7]. Despite their physical interpretability, these methods are plagued by inaccuracies in empirical energy functions and the exponential complexity of the discrete sequence space, severely limiting their scalability and robustness [8]. Conversely, statistical methods infer sequence-structure correlations from multiple sequence alignments or structural homologs [9]. While intuitive, these homology-driven approaches inherently struggle with novel folds and suffer from a critical limitation: the “data scarcity” paradox. As highlighted by recent studies, the exponential growth of sequence space vastly outstrips available annotated data, making it difficult for purely data-driven models to generalize to underrepresented or de novo scaffolds [10, 11].

  To address these limitations, early machine learning approaches introduced a paradigm shift by leveraging pattern recognition to bypass explicit physics simulations. Methods such as support vector machines and hidden Markov models attempted to classify designability based on coarse-grained features like hydrophobic/polar patterns [12]. Neural network-based frameworks, such as SPIN, further advanced this by integrating local structural fragments with energy terms to predict compatible sequences [13]. These pioneering efforts laid the conceptual groundwork for modern deep learning, demonstrating that high-level sequence-structure relationships could be effectively captured without exhaustive sampling.

  The advent of deep learning has since catalyzed a revolution in protein design, fundamentally changing how we model the relationship between sequence, structure, and function [14]. The field has rapidly evolved from static encoders to dynamic, generative architectures, with a particular emphasis on geometric deep learning​ to preserve 3D physical constraints. The introduction of models like ProteinMPNN marked a significant milestone by demonstrating that sequence recovery could be dramatically improved by operating directly on atomic coordinates and incorporating bidirectional message passing [15]. This was followed by a proliferation of graph-based neural networks (GNNs), such as PiFold [16] and GeoSeqBuilder [17], which represent proteins as residue graphs to natively capture long-range spatial dependencies. Furthermore, the integration of AlphaFold 2 into design pipelines, exemplified by frameworks such as AlphaDesign, has enabled controllable, high-fidelity generation by closing the loop between design and structure validation [18].

  Despite these advances, the quest for truly generalizable and robust design frameworks continues. Recent breakthroughs are pushing the boundaries of what is possible. For instance, the development of relaxed sequence optimization has challenged the traditional discrete nature of amino acids by formulating design as an optimization problem in a continuous sequence space, allowing for ultraefficient exploration and scalable generation of complex topologies [19]. Simultaneously, to combat data scarcity, particularly for functional protein generation, hierarchical models leveraging large language models, such as ProteinRG, have emerged [20]. These models utilize regenerative learning and pretrained sequence embeddings to generate high-quality, functional sequences even from small datasets. On the geometric front, novel architectures like ProtSeqGen are refining the precision of side-chain packing and local backbone compatibility through sophisticated multistage geometric graph networks, achieving superior sequence recovery rates across diverse structural subclasses [21].

  Here, we introduce EquiDesign (Fig. 1), a framework for de novo protein sequence design employing Equiformer [22] as its core engine to model long-range spatial interactions. Input structures are encoded into local geometric graphs, extracting six complementary intra- and inter-residue matrices; Equiformer then performs spatially aware refinement by jointly optimizing invariant scalars and equivariant vectors. Trained on CATH 4.4 [23], EquiDesign outperformed state-of-the-art (SOTA) baselines on canonical (TS50/TS500) [16, 24] and challenging cohorts (IDRome-120 and TS45 [21]), exhibiting superior generalization. Notably, it generated thermodynamically stable, high-fidelity native-like folds across nine diverse proteins, establishing a robust platform for atomic-precision design of predefined scaffolds.


## Materials and methods

### Dataset collection and preprocessing
  Generalization across protein folds was assessed using the CATH 4.4 dataset [23], containing single-chain structures with a residue length between 40 and 500, enforcing a 40% sequence identity to eliminate homology redundancy. This yielded 29 453 training, 1732 validation, and 3466 test structures [25]. Model performance was further evaluated on the backbone-only benchmarks: TS50 and TS500, comprising 50 and 500 proteins with experimental structures [13, 26].

  To probe challenging targets, we tested specialized datasets enriched for intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) [27, 28] and free modeling (FM) targets [29, 30]. Curated from 1508 annotated candidates (<200 residues), IDRoom-120 [21] comprises 120 samples evenly distributed across four chain length intervals (<50, 51–100, 101–150, and 151–200), with exactly 30 samples in each category. Second, we employed TS45 (CASP15), a stringent benchmark of FM targets lacking detectable homology to known structures [29, 31]. Dataset statistics are summarized in Table 1.

### EquiDesign model training and calibration
  Optimized using Adam with weight decay and label smoothing, EquiDesign employs a Noam scheduler with linear warm-up to ensure stable convergence [37]. Training utilizes automatic mixed precision and gradient clipping, with a fixed seed guaranteeing reproducibility. Manual hyperparameter tuning (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2) precedes evaluation, where final results are quantified as the mean and standard deviation across 10 independent training trials.

### Comparisons with existing methods
  For a holistic assessment, EquiDesign is benchmarked against 16 representative baselines, categorized into five architectural families. The first two categories include MLP-based techniques (SPIN [13], SPIN2 [38], and Wang’s model [39]) and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based approaches (SPROF [40], ProDCoNN [41], DenseCPD [42], and ScFold [24]). Given that GNN approaches represent the current mainstream, we further divide them into two subgroups: message-passing GNNs (ProteinMPNN [15], PiFold [16], GeoSeqBuilder [17], ProtSeqGen [21]), global-aware GNNs (GraphTrans [43], GVP [44], GCA [16], AlphaDesign [18]). Notably, SurFold is categorized separately, as it uniquely integrates both structural and surface features [45]. Considering the broader coverage of structures, all models are trained on CATH 4.4 and evaluated on four independent test sets: TS50, TS500, IDRome-120, and TS45 (Table 1). Additionally, the evaluation of folding stability is implemented on both CATH 4.4 and CATH 4.2 [46] for unbiased benchmarking.

  We systematically evaluated the EquiDesign framework through three ablation perspectives. ### Probabilistic sampling for diverse sequence generation
  To assess the model's ability to explore diverse yet physically plausible sequence space beyond deterministic prediction, we modified the final MLP layer to output a probability distribution over 20 amino acids via softmax, rather than a single argmax prediction. During inference, we applied top-k sampling (k=5) to generate four distinct sequences per backbone, yielding 36 designed sequences in total across the nine test cases. For reproducibility, we used a base seed of 260 816 with four consecutive seeds per protein, incremented by 100 between targets.

  The generated sequences were evaluated using four complementary metrics: (i) sequence identity to the native sequence, measuring deviation from memorization, (ii) pairwise Hamming distance among sampled sequences, quantifying intrabackbone diversity, (iii) KL divergence of amino acid distributions relative to the native set, assessing physicochemical plausibility, and (iv) foldability rate (self-consistency root mean square deviation, (scRMSD) < 2.0 Å), validated by both ESMFold and AlphaFold 3 (AF3), ensuring structural validity.


### Simulation environment
  EquiDesign was implemented in PyTorch 2.4.1 and Python 3.10.10, running on a system equipped with a 12th Gen Intel® Core™ i9-12900K processor and 128 GB RAM. All simulations leveraged CUDA 12.1.105 for acceleration on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX A4000 GPU (32 GB VRAM). Three-dimensional structures were predicted using AF3 via the AlphaFold Server (https://alphafoldserver.com).


## Results

### Ablation studies on model architecture
  To validate the design of our model architecture, we systematically dissected the contributions of structural configurations, feature representation components, and Equiformer hyperparameters through comprehensive ablation studies.

  First, we assessed structural choices by evaluating various combinations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mathrm{C}\mathrm{\beta}$\end{document} geometry and vn. As shown in Table 2, incorporating Cβ geometry was pivotal, with the model achieving peak performance specifically when utilizing two vn.

  Second, we quantified the impact of removing specific feature embeddings (Table 3). The removal of inter-residue features (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${M}_1$\end{document}) caused the most severe degradation, underscoring their essential role in capturing long-range dependencies. In contrast, excluding intra-residue features (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${M}_2$\end{document}) or vector embeddings (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${M}_3$\end{document}) led to milder declines (recovery rates of 42.64% and 53.76%, respectively), suggesting that scalar invariant features alone provide a sufficiently robust backbone for sequence generation, while geometric details offer supplementary benefits.

  Finally, we evaluated varying Equiformer depths (Table 3). Deeper architectures improved stability, but the gain from two to four layers was marginal (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${M}_5$\end{document}−\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${M}_7$\end{document}). The full model with three-layer blocks achieved an optimal trade-off between capacity and efficiency, yielding the highest recovery (56.59%), and the lowest loss (1.37).

  Collectively, these results validate the proposed architectural design.


### Evaluating EquiDesign against SOTA methods on CATH 4.4
  On the CATH 4.4 test set, we compare EquiDesign against SOTA methods categorized by architectural features (Table 4). Under the standard backbone-only input condition, EquiDesign achieves a sequence recovery rate of 56.59%, with the lowest perplexity (3.95) and loss (1.37), outperforming all competitors across every metric. It surpasses conventional baselines (MLP, CNN) and message-passing GNNs (ProteinMPNN, PiFold, GeoSeqBuilder) by a substantial margin, including a 9.06% improvement over ProteinMPNN.

  In contrast, SurFold is uniquely designed to integrate backbone, side-chain, and surface features for sequence prediction [45]. While SurFold (Full model) achieves the best overall performance on CATH 4.4, this comparison reveals a critical discrepancy in input granularity. When restricted to backbone-only input (the standard protocol for inverse folding evaluation), SurFold’s performance drops markedly.

  Thus, EquiDesign not only surpasses traditional GNN methods but also demonstrates greater robustness than SurFold under the standard single-backbone input setting, validating its capacity for high-quality sequence design without reliance on auxiliary structural features.


### Generalization evaluation on canonical benchmarks: TS50 and TS500
  To assess the generalization ability of EquiDesign, we evaluated the pretrained model on two independent test sets, TS50 and TS500. After removing all overlapping proteins with the training set, the evaluation comprised 26 and 243 previously unseen samples for TS50 and TS500, respectively. As summarized in Tables 5 and 6, EquiDesign achieved recovery rates of 59.47% on TS50 and 61.08% on TS500, outperforming most SOTA methods. Notably, it substantially exceeded strong baselines such as ProteinMPNN (56.23% on TS50, 59.56% on TS500) and maintained the top rank across both benchmarks.

  Furthermore, we performed a rigorous statistical comparison between EquiDesign and four representative methods (ProteinMPNN, PiFold, GeoSeqBuilder, and ProtSeqGen) (Supplementary Table S3), using paired tests across TS50 and TS500. The results confirm that EquiDesign significantly outperforms the other four methods across all metrics, while its advantage over ProtSeqGen in loss does not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). This analysis underscores the robustness of EquiDesign’s performance despite the close competition.


### Perresidue accuracy and prediction confidence
  We next assessed the residue-level prediction fidelity of EquiDesign on the TS500 dataset by benchmarking against native protein sequences. EquiDesign demonstrates a balanced amino acid propensity, effectively accommodating the full spectrum of hydrophobic, aromatic, polar, and charged residues. This chemical diversity is reflected in the secondary structure-dependent accuracy, which aligns with established physicochemical principles (Fig. 2A).

  To quantify the contribution of local structural context, we parsed the recovery accuracy by residue type within alpha-helices, beta-sheets, and coils. As expected from their intrinsic structural biases, residues such as Thr (T), Val (V), Trp (W), and Tyr (Y) exhibit heightened recovery within beta-sheets, whereas Lys (K), Met (M), and Pro (P) are predicted with greater fidelity in alpha-helices and coils, respectively (Fig. 2B). Detailed per-residue accuracy metrics are provided in Supplementary Tables S6 and S7.


### Generalization evaluation on challenging benchmarks: IDRome-120 and TS45
  To assess the capability of our model on challenging tasks, we applied the pretrained EquiDesign to specialized datasets featuring IDRs [27, 28] and FM targets [29].

  First, we introduced the IDRome-120 dataset [21], comprising 120 proteins of diverse sequence lengths (<200 residues). As shown in Fig. 3A, EquiDesign delivered significantly superior performance in generating sequences for disordered regions. While the predictive performance of all evaluated methods declined relative to the standard TS50 and TS500 benchmarks, EquiDesign exhibited the smallest degradation, maintaining a substantial lead over competitors (e.g. ~3.6% higher recovery than ProtSeqGen).

  Second, we evaluated the model on TS45, a highly challenging benchmark for de novo protein design consisting of FM targets with no homology to known structures [31]. The results on TS45 closely mirrored those on IDRome-120, with EquiDesign again achieving the top recovery rate (54.71%) (Fig. 3B). This consistency underscores the comparable difficulty of these two challenging benchmarks and highlights the robustness of EquiDesign under diverse and difficult design conditions (Supplementary Table S8).

  To rigorously evaluate the capability of EquiDesign in de novo sequence generation, we curated a benchmark suite of nine representative proteins spanning diverse structural classes (Table 7), including all-α, all-β, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha /\beta$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha +\beta$\end{document} folds, and complex integral membrane proteins, covering a gradient of topological complexity from simple motifs to intricate TIM-barrels.

To validate structural fidelity, we employed AF3 to predict the 3D structures of the generated sequences. As shown in Fig. 4, the predicted conformations exhibit remarkable structural similarity to native structures, with high TM-scores and low RMSD values (average RMSD of 0.903 Å), confirming that EquiDesign robustly produces sequences that accurately fold into target native-like architectures.

  We further benchmarked EquiDesign against four SOTA models on this nonredundant dataset (Supplementary Figs S1–S4). As summarized in Table 8, EquiDesign consistently outperformed all comparators, achieving superior TM-scores and lower RMSD values. We also assessed the folding stability: designs for 1f89.A, 1luc.A, 1n27.A, 1i07.A, 1a4i.A, and 3u5t. A ranked first in stability among all evaluated models. In addition, statistical analyses showed that EquiDesign-generated sequences had significantly higher folding stability than two baseline methods, particularly ProteinMPNN and PiFold (Supplementary Table S9).

  Moreover, Rosetta energy minimization confirmed that EquiDesign-designed sequences frequently achieve lower total energy than native sequences across the benchmark set, driven by reduced van der Waals repulsion (Fa_rep) and generally favorable attraction (Fa_atr), demonstrating thermodynamic stability beyond structural compatibility (Supplementary Table S10).


#### Diversity and novelty of generated sequences
  To further assess whether EquiDesign explores viable alternative sequences beyond native recapitulation, we performed top-k sampling (k=5) to generate four sequences per backbone and evaluated them using four metrics (Supplementary Table S11). The sequence identity to native ranged from 35.2% to 65.2%, confirming the model’s ability to propose novel yet structurally compatible residues. Pairwise diversity (Hamming distance) averaged 15.3, indicating substantial sequence variation under the same backbone constraint. Critically, 50%–100% of the generated sequences were predicted to be foldable (scRMSD <2.0 Å) by both ESMFold and AF3. These results demonstrate that EquiDesign produces multiple distinct, physically plausible sequences for a given backbone, supporting its utility as a genuine design tool beyond residue classification.

  Collectively, these results establish EquiDesign as a robust framework for generating sequences that faithfully fold into diverse native-like scaffolds, achieving exceptional precision and stability as validated by AF3.


## Discussion
  In summary, we developed EquiDesign, evolving beyond our prior ProtSeqGen. The initial geometric feature extraction (including Cβ treatment), and vn components are adapted from ProtSeqGen. The key methodological novelty lies in replacing the conventional Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN) layers with an Equiformer-driven architecture for structure-guided sequence design. This upgrade enables explicit SE(3)-equivariant reasoning: whereas MPNNs implicitly propagate information via abstract node updates, EquiDesign disentangles features into invariant scalars and equivariant vectors, preserving directional backbone geometry during message passing. By leveraging spatial KNN graphs and geometric attention to fuse multiscale contextual cues, this framework achieves a more physically grounded representation of protein conformations, culminating in higher-fidelity sequence decoding.

  Comprehensive ablation studies substantiate EquiDesign’s architectural efficacy. First, inter-residue features emerge as the most critical component, as their removal causes the most severe performance degradation, highlighting the importance of modeling long-range spatial dependencies is the fundamental challenge that conventional sequential GNNs fail to address. Second, integrating Cβ geometry with vn proves instrumental for further improvement, establishing a global information hub that transcends local sequential biases and elevates performance to the next level. Finally, hyperparameter optimization confirms that our configuration, specifically three-layer Equiformer blocks, achieves an optimal trade-off between model expressivity and computational efficiency. These findings validate EquiDesign as a physically principled advancement over prior graph-based paradigms.

  EquiDesign converged stably on the CATH 4.4 benchmark (Supplementary Fig. S5), reflecting robust optimization with minimal overfitting. Notably, the pretrained model preserved high fidelity on held-out TS50 and TS500 sets; crucially, it simultaneously elevated predictive accuracy while reducing runtime and memory overhead relative to current SOTA counterparts (Supplementary Table S12). Moreover, EquiDesign exhibited remarkable generalization to high-difficulty regimes (IDRome-120 and TS45), particularly for IRDs and template-free targets, substantially surpassing representative baselines (Supplementary Tables S13 and S14). Collectively, these results position EquiDesign as a powerful framework capable of extending beyond well-folded proteins into structurally ambiguous landscapes.

  Unlike conventional physics-based approaches, EquiDesign operates without task-specific parameter tuning, thereby democratizing access to high-fidelity protein design. Given diverse de novo backbones, the model generates sequences that consistently fold into target 3D structures with high accuracy, as evidenced by superior sequence recovery, TM-scores, and low RMSD values. Most critically, EquiDesign yields sequences with markedly enhanced stability, as validated by both redesign on nine diverse scaffolds (Table 8) and leading average stability scores across CATH 4.2/4.4 benchmarks (Supplementary Table S15). This capability to generate physically realistic, robust proteins significantly expands its practical utility.

  To rigorously validate EquiDesign’s design principles beyond statistical recovery rates, we subjected the nine representative proteins to complementary structural and evolutionary analyses. First, we performed AlphaFold 3 (AF3) structure prediction on three sequence types (native, EquiDesign-predicted, and composition-matched scrambled sequences) and evaluated backbone fidelity via RMSD and confidence scores (pLDDT). EquiDesign-predicted sequences consistently recovered native-like structures with low RMSD (<2.0 Å) and high pLDDT values, whereas scrambled sequences universally failed to fold correctly, confirming that our pipeline generates physically plausible sequences rather than relying on amino acid composition alone (Supplementary Table S16). Second, to address potential memorization artifacts inherent to deep-learning-based structure predictors, we conducted homology searches (MMseqs2 [47]) and structural neighbor searches (Foldseek [48]) against the AF3 training set proxy (PDB entries released before September 30, 2021). While most targets exhibited high sequence identity (> 90%) with training structures, the maximum TM-scores for several proteins (e.g. 1i07.B at 0.926) were notably below 1.0, indicating that AF3 does not perfectly memorize specific backbone conformations (Supplementary Table S17). Critically, EquiDesign-generated sequences recapitulated target folds with high precision despite low sequence identity to training data. This confirms that our method captures genuine biophysical folding principles, not statistical correlations, and is robust against compositional biases and training set overfitting.

  EquiDesign achieves superior inverse folding performance through its unified SE(3)-equivariant framework that synergistically integrates disentangled scalar–vector representations for simultaneous capture of invariant and directional features, and multiscale geometric attention for long-range topological reasoning, collectively enabling a more expressive and physically grounded latent representation than prior handcrafted or GVP-based approaches.

  The current inverse folding paradigm has two key limitations: it depends on a prespecified backbone structure, and sequence recovery does not equate to functional activity. EquiDesign, like most structure-conditioned models, assumes the input backbone is optimal for the target function. However, this assumption may not hold in de novo design scenarios. Going forward, EquiDesign can be naturally integrated with generative backbone models (e.g. diffusion-based structure samplers) to form a complete design pipeline. Moreover, incorporating differentiable functional assays or experimental feedback loops could further close the gap between structural plausibility and biological activity.

  Despite these strengths, EquiDesign presents several areas for further improvement. First, while effective for designing a diverse set of protein scaffolds (all-α, all-β, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha /\beta$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha +\beta$\end{document} folds, membrane proteins, and TIM barrels), its transferability to other protein classes remains to be fully established. Second, the current study is limited by the lack of high-throughput structural validation of the generated protein designs. Lastly, training strategies tailored to distinct protein classes remain a crucial and open area for further investigation. To enhance the accessibility of EquiDesign, a user-friendly web server is currently under development and is planned for future release.


## Conclusion
  We developed EquiDesign, an Equiformer-augmented framework that combines spatial KNN graphs with SE(3)-equivariant reasoning to integrate multiscale geometric features for structure-guided protein inverse folding. Benchmarking across CATH 4.4, TS50, TS500, IDRome-120, and TS45 demonstrates that EquiDesign achieves superior sequence recovery and robust generalization across diverse folds. While computational metrics are compelling, future work must prioritize rigorous experimental validation of the designed proteomes. Ultimately, EquiDesign establishes a powerful paradigm for de novo protein design, paving the way for accelerated development of next-generation therapeutics and biomaterials.
