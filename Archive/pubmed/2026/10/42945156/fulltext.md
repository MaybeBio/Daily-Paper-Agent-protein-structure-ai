# Structural basis for inhibition of the lipid flippase MurJ by phage lysis protein SglPP7 suggests convergent evolution with other Sgl peptides


## Abstract
  Some bacteriophages encode lysis proteins that inhibit essential bacterial processes, and elucidating their mechanisms is valuable for developing antibacterial strategies against drug-resistant pathogens. We determined the cryo-EM structure of the complex between the essential Escherichia coli lipid II flippase MurJ and the phage lysis protein SglPP7. Structural, mutational, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulation analyses revealed that SglPP7 binds along the intramembrane cleft of MurJ and inserts its C-terminal tail into the internal cavity, stabilizing MurJ in an outward-facing conformation. Structural comparison with LysM (SglM) and SglCJ3 showed that these sequence-divergent Sgl proteins stabilize MurJ in the outward-facing conformation through distinct binding modes, consistent with the possibility of convergent evolution toward MurJ inhibition.


## Introduction
  Multidrug-resistant bacteria pose a major global public health challenge, and new antibacterial strategies are urgently needed. In gram-negative bacteria, peptidoglycan is important for maintaining cell shape and resistance to osmotic stress, and its biosynthesis pathway is a well-established target for antibiotics. Single-gene lysis (Sgl) proteins, encoded by single-stranded RNA and DNA phages and also known as “protein antibiotics,” induce host cell lysis by inhibiting essential cellular functions [1,2]. Recent metatranscriptomic analyses have greatly expanded the number of ssRNA phage genomes available in the NCBI database [3–5]. Although Sgl proteins had been identified only in representative ssRNA phages prior to this expansion, subsequent comprehensive analyses revealed diverse Sgl proteins, representing a rich source of potential “protein antibiotics.”

  Phage-derived Sgl proteins primarily target essential factors of the peptidoglycan biosynthetic pathway. For example, protein E from the microvirus ΦX174 and protein A2 from the Levivirus Qβ have been shown to inhibit MraY and MurA, respectively [6–11]. LysM, a 37-residue Sgl of phage M, induces cell lysis by targeting MurJ, the essential membrane protein that flips peptidoglycan precursor lipid II from the cytoplasm to the periplasm [12,13]. Furthermore, recent genetic screening suggested that SglPP7, a 55-residue Sgl of Pseudomonas aeruginosa phage PP7, also targets MurJ [14].

  Structural studies have shown that MurJ, which consists of 14 transmembrane helices divided into an N-lobe (TM1–6), a C-lobe (TM7–12), and TM13–14, transports lipid II via alternating inward- and outward-facing conformations, similar to those of MATE transporters [15–18] (Fig 1A). Furthermore, cryo-EM analysis of the MurJ/LysM complex revealed that LysM wedges into the outward-facing conformation of MurJ, preventing the conformational changes as well as lipid II transport, which results in cell lysis [19]. In contrast to LysM, SglPP7 is encoded at a distinct genomic position and shows no amino acid sequence similarity (Fig 1B and 1C). These findings raise the possibility that evolutionarily distinct phages independently acquired the ability to target MurJ.

  Although another group reported the structure of a stable mutant MurJ/SglPP7 complex during the preparation of this manuscript, we independently determined the cryo-EM structure of a MurJ/SglPP7 complex reconstituted into lipid nanodiscs using full-length SglPP7. Based on mutational analysis and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, we propose a model in which SglPP7 locks MurJ in an outward-facing conformation through interactions distinct from those of LysM. These findings are consistent with the possibility that these sequence-divergent Sgl proteins independently acquired distinct strategies to inhibit MurJ.


## Results and discussion

### Overall structure of the MurJ/SglPP7 complex
  We evaluated the toxicity of SglPP7 in Escherichia coli cells. The induction of untagged or N-terminal Met-His10-tagged SglPP7 caused cell lysis, whereas the C-terminal His10-tagged SglPP7 did not exhibit lytic activity (Fig 1D, upper). In contrast, LysM, which shows little sequence similarity to SglPP7 (Fig 1C), retained lytic activity when fused with either an N- or C-terminal tag (Fig 1D, lower). These results indicate that although both LysM and SglPP7 are known to inhibit MurJ [14], SglPP7 may block MurJ through a mechanism distinct from that of LysM. Therefore, the MurJ/SglPP7 complex was purified using N-terminally Met-His10-tagged SglPP71–55(WT) and Met-Ala-E. coli MurJ2–511, reconstituted into nanodiscs with E. coli lipids, and its cryo-EM structure was determined at an overall resolution of 3.2 Å (Figs 1E–1G and S1; S1 Table). Recently, the structure of an N-terminally truncated, Met-Gly-His6-22× GS-tagged SglPP725–55(S31A, S39A, S47A)/BRIL-MurJ4–500/Fab-nanobody complex was reported at a resolution of 3.70 Å [20]. Our structure was determined at a higher resolution using full-length SglPP7 reconstituted with MurJ in lipid nanodiscs (S2A–S2C Fig). Comparison of the two structures revealed a Cα root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 0.747 Å, indicating that the overall structures are nearly identical (S2D Fig). In both structures, SglPP7 is positioned along the intramembrane cleft of MurJ (Fig 1F). The modeled region of SglPP7 in each structure forms a single transmembrane helix followed by a short C-terminal tail that inserts deeply into a positively charged internal cavity on the C-lobe side of MurJ, formed by TM7, TM8, TM10, and TM12. We designate this feature as the “embedded tail” (Figs 1F, 1G, and S1F). In our structure, the N-terminal region of SglPP7 is modeled more extensively, beginning at residue 26 (S2C Fig). The structures suggest that SglPP7 stabilizes MurJ in the outward-facing conformation, arresting its transport cycle.

  Notably, AlphaFold3 [21] failed to predict the experimentally observed positioning of SglPP7 on MurJ and showed low confidence in this region, highlighting the importance of the experimentally determined MurJ/SglPP7 complex structure (S3 Fig). Furthermore, all AlphaFold3-predicted MurJ models shown in S3 Fig. adopt an inward-facing conformation, in contrast to the outward-facing conformation observed in the experimental structures. This tendency may reflect the predominance of inward-facing MurJ structures among the structural data currently available in the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Thus, AlphaFold3 may preferentially predict the inward-facing state rather than the outward-facing conformation stabilized by SglPP7.


### Importance of N- and C-terminal regions of SglPP7
  The cryo-EM map around the N-terminal region of SglPP7 (residues 1–25) was very poor and insufficient for reliable atomic modeling (S1D and S2C Figs). Therefore, residues 1–25 were not included in the atomic model, and the SglPP7 model starts at residue 26. To examine its functional importance, we generated a series of N-terminal truncation mutants of GFP-fused SglPP7 and evaluated their toxicity (S4 Fig). All truncation mutants retained lytic activity except for the GFP–SglPP722–55, indicating that the minimal functional unit of SglPP7 resides in the region beginning around residue 22.

  The C-terminal embedded tail (residues 50–55) inserts deeply into the positively charged interior of MurJ and likely plays an important role in this interaction. This structural feature is consistent with the loss of activity observed for the C-terminally His10-tagged SglPP7 (Fig 1D), indicating that the addition of a C-terminal tag sterically interferes with its insertion. Moreover, we addressed the importance of the embedded tail of SglPP7 by mutational analysis. Successive C-terminal truncations showed that retention of the sequence through residue 53 was required for lytic activity (Figs 2C, left; S5A). Next, we examined the lytic activity of several mutations at W53, which inserts deeply into the C-lobe and appears to play a key role in this interaction (Figs 2A–2C, middle; S5B). The W53A mutant retained full activity, W53G, W53C, and W53S showed slightly reduced activity, and W53K and W53R lost lytic activity. These results suggest that W53 is important for stabilizing the interaction with MurJ, although this effect may be partially compensated by neighboring residues, including residues 54–55. Additionally, we examined the same W53 variants in the truncated SglPP7 (1–53) mutant, which retains minimal activity, and all the variants showed weakened lytic activity (Figs 2C, right; S5C). These results suggest that the C-terminal region is an important determinant of SglPP7 function, with W53 serving as a key residue within this region.


### A lipid-like feature in the putative lipid II-binding groove of MurJ
  In the cryo-EM map of our MurJ/SglPP7 complex, an unassigned lipid-like map feature is observed in the hydrophobic groove of MurJ, located between TM1, TM8, and TM14 (Figs 1F and 1G, and S6). A similar unmodeled lipid-like feature was also observed in the outward-facing crystal structure of Thermosipho africanus MurJ determined using lipidic cubic phase crystallization [16]. This groove was proposed to accommodate the hydrophobic undecaprenyl chain of lipid II.

  The lipid-like map feature is located near F22, R24, and R270 in our structure (S6A and S6B Fig). R24 and R270 are conserved arginine residues located in the central cavity of MurJ and are essential for MurJ function [22–24]. D25 is structurally adjacent to R24 and R270 in the MurJ structures shown in S6C Fig. and has also been shown to be important for MurJ function [16]. Comparison of previously determined MurJ structures indicates that the relative positions of these residues change during the transport cycle (S6C Fig). In the inward-open structure of T. africanus MurJ, R255, which corresponds to R270 in E. coli MurJ, is oriented toward the central cavity. In the inward-occluded structure, R24 and R255 approach to within 2.9 Å, and this electrostatically unfavorable arrangement has been proposed to be stabilized through interactions with D25 and the negatively charged diphosphate moiety of lipid II [16]. In the outward-facing conformation, R255 is positioned away from R24 (S6C Fig). Thus, R24, D25, and R255/R270 undergo substantial rearrangement during the MurJ transport cycle, and the lipid-like map observed in our structure is located near these important residues, although it does not directly interact with them.

  Furthermore, in vivo crosslinking analysis demonstrated that F22 on TM1 crosslinked with lipid II [25]. In addition, the crosslinking of lipid II was not significantly reduced in MurJ variants with substitutions at R24 or R270. These findings suggest that these residues may contribute to steps beyond the initial binding of lipid II, including conformational transitions associated with substrate recognition and transport.

  In the MurJ/SglPP7 complex determined in this study, a lipid-like map was observed in this putative lipid II-binding region while MurJ was stabilized in an outward-facing conformation. However, the map does not contain features corresponding to the hydrophilic headgroup of lipid II, precluding identification of its molecular species. Therefore, these observations suggest only that a lipid-like molecule may be accommodated in the putative lipid II-binding region even in the SglPP7-bound state.


### Stabilization of MurJ in an outward-facing conformation by SglPP7
  To evaluate how SglPP7 binding affects the structural dynamics of MurJ, we performed MD simulations in a membrane environment, both in the presence and absence of SglPP7 (Fig 3). In each of the three independent simulations for both conditions, the RMSD of the Cα atoms of MurJ increased over time. The RMSD values of the MurJ/SglPP7 complex were consistently lower than those of MurJ alone (Fig 3A). Comparison of the structures at 120 ns with the initial structures showed that MurJ maintained an outward-facing conformation both in the presence and absence of SglPP7 (Fig 3B). However, MurJ alone underwent larger structural fluctuations from the initial structures, whereas these fluctuations were suppressed in the MurJ/SglPP7 complex (Fig 3B).

  During the simulations, the embedded tail of SglPP7 remained positioned within the internal cavity. To evaluate structural fluctuations around the embedded tail, we analyzed Cα–Cα distances of two residue pairs in transmembrane helices lining the C-lobe cavity of MurJ. Y266 on TM8 and L247 on TM7 flank the embedded tail in the MurJ/SglPP7 complex, whereas G360 on TM10 and G415 on TM12 are located deeper in the cavity and away from the embedded tail. In all three independent simulations, MurJ alone showed a tendency for the Cα–Cα distance between Y266 (TM8) and L247 (TM7) to decrease relative to the initial structure. In contrast, this distance was maintained in the MurJ/SglPP7 complex (Fig 3C and 3D). The distance between G360 and G415 remained largely unchanged under both conditions. Thus, these results support the idea that the C-terminal embedded tail of SglPP7, a structural feature distinct from LysM, stabilizes MurJ in an outward-facing conformation.


### Structural comparison of the MurJ/SglPP7 complex with previously reported complexes
  We compared the MurJ/SglPP7 complex with the MurJ/LysM complex. In both complexes, MurJ adopts an outward-facing conformation, with a Cα RMSD of 0.816 Å (Fig 4A). We further compared our MurJ/SglPP7 complex with recently reported MurJ/LysM, MurJ/SglPP7, and MurJ/SglCJ3 structures [20]: the Cα RMSDs for the MurJ regions were 0.796, 0.747, and 0.704 Å, respectively. In all of these complexes, MurJ adopts a closely similar outward-facing conformation (S7 Fig). LysM consists of two helices (Helix 1 and Helix 2). Helix 1 is located peripherally near TM2 and TM7 (Fig 4A, right). In contrast, the SglPP7 TM helix is positioned closer to TM7 and adopts a different orientation relative to TM7 (Fig 4A). Moreover, the SglPP7 helix contacts a broader region along TM7 compared to LysM (Fig 4A, left). Comparison of the SglPP7 and LysM TM helices reveals distinct side-chain compositions at the MurJ interface, with non-overlapping residue sets, indicating different interaction modes (Fig 4B). In alanine-scanning mutagenesis, only F22A, L30A, and S39A of SglPP7 showed modest reductions in lytic activity (S9 Fig). In contrast, a recent study evaluated point mutations using a Δ2–24 minimal functional variant of SglPP7 as the background and reported that some substitutions, such as Y36 substitution, caused a marked loss of function [20]. Together, these results suggest that the SglPP7 TM helix does not rely on a few specific residue interactions but is instead supported by distributed, weak interactions across a broad contact surface. We evaluated the sensitivity of LysM-resistant MurJ mutants carrying substitutions at the LysM-binding interface to SglPP7. Under SglPP7 induction, only the Q244P mutant was able to grow, whereas all other mutants remained sensitive to SglPP7 (Fig 4C). These results also support that SglPP7 and LysM inhibit MurJ through distinct binding interfaces.

  We next examined the structural similarities and differences among SglPP7, LysM, and SglCJ3 in detail (S7 and S8 Figs). Despite their highly divergent amino acid sequences (S7C Fig), the transmembrane helices of all three Sgl proteins are located at the interface between the N- and C-lobes of MurJ, particularly near TM2 and TM7. The transmembrane helix of SglCJ3 adopts a position and orientation similar to those of SglPP7 (S7B Fig), but distinct from that of LysM. Furthermore, the side chains of Y36 of SglPP7, L14 of LysM, and Y76 of SglCJ3 insert into a similar pocket on MurJ (Figs 4B and S8A). Y36 of SglPP7 and Y76 of SglCJ3 are located in similar positions on their helices, whereas L14 of LysM, which is shifted by approximately one helical turn relative to these positions, inserts into a similar pocket. Consistent with this structural observation, substitution of Y76 markedly reduced the lytic activity of SglCJ3, supporting the functional importance of this residue in MurJ inhibition [20]. These structural and biochemical findings indicate that, despite differences in their sequences and transmembrane-helix arrangements, the three Sgl proteins target a similar structural region of MurJ.

  In contrast, the C-terminal regions of the three Sgl proteins adopt markedly distinct arrangements on MurJ and interact with different regions of MurJ (S8B and S8C Fig). In SglPP7, the C-terminal embedded tail occupies the internal cavity of the C-lobe of MurJ and extends deeply into it (Figs 2 and S8C). In contrast, the C-terminal Helix 2 of LysM is positioned along the membrane surface and interacts with the external surface of MurJ (Figs 4A and S7). In SglCJ3, the transmembrane helix broadly occupies the membrane-embedded cleft formed by TM2 and TM7/TM8, whereas only part of the C-terminal region extends into the cavity. Thus, the three Sgl proteins interact with different regions of MurJ through their C-terminal regions and stabilize MurJ in the outward-facing conformation through distinct binding modes characterized as cavity-occupying, surface-binding, and cleft-binding for SglPP7, LysM, and SglCJ3, respectively.

  Similarly, phages λ, T7, T5, and P1 encode the small FtsZ inhibitors Kil, Gp0.4, Hdi, and Icd, respectively [26–29]. Despite having no apparent amino acid sequence similarity, these proteins all target the essential cell-division protein FtsZ. For these FtsZ inhibitors, structures of their complexes with FtsZ are not currently available, and it remains unclear whether they inhibit FtsZ through common or distinct mechanisms. In contrast, SglPP7, LysM, and SglCJ3 provide a structurally characterized example of how sequence-divergent phage proteins inhibit the same essential bacterial protein through distinct binding modes.


### SglPP7 working model
  Based on the MurJ/SglPP7 complex structure determined in this study, we propose a model for how SglPP7 inhibits the MurJ transport cycle (Fig 4D). During lipid II transport, MurJ undergoes conformational transitions from the inward-facing conformation to the outward-facing conformation and then returns to the inward-facing conformation via the squeezed conformation. SglPP7 forms a broad contact surface near TM7 of MurJ and inserts its C-terminal embedded tail deeply into the internal cavity of the C-lobe, locking MurJ in the outward-facing conformation (Fig 4E). In addition, a lipid-like map was observed in the putative lipid II-binding region of the MurJ/SglPP7 complex. These observations suggest that SglPP7 prevents outward-facing MurJ from undergoing subsequent conformational transitions. As a result, the lipid II transport cycle is blocked.

  In this study, we elucidated how SglPP7 inhibits the MurJ transport cycle by forming a broad interaction interface near TM7 and inserting its C-terminal tail deeply into the internal cavity of the C-lobe, thereby stabilizing MurJ in the outward-facing conformation. Structural comparison of SglPP7, LysM, and SglCJ3 further showed that, despite their highly divergent amino acid sequences and overall binding modes to MurJ, these Sgl proteins target a similar structural region of MurJ and stabilize the outward-facing conformation. Nevertheless, their binding modes are markedly distinct: cavity-occupying for SglPP7, surface-binding for LysM, and cleft-binding for SglCJ3. Thus, these phage-derived Sgl proteins with no apparent amino acid sequence similarity and encoded at different genomic locations appear to have independently acquired distinct molecular strategies to inhibit the MurJ transport cycle. Their shared ability to stabilize MurJ in the outward-facing conformation is consistent with the possibility of convergent evolution toward MurJ inhibition.


## Methods

### Bacterial strains and plasmids
  The E. coli strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in S2 Table. Details of the plasmids used are described in the Plasmid Construction section.


### Plasmid construction
  A DNA fragment containing the sglPP7 gene, which was codon-optimized for E. coli, was synthesized de novo (Integrated DNA Technologies, IDT). The sglPP7 fragment was cloned into XbaI-digested pBAD33 [30] using In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (Takara Bio). The resulting plasmid was designated pKG382. For pKH32 (pBAD18 [30] encoding his10-sglPP7), the his10-sglPP7 fragment was PCR-amplified from pKG382. The PCR product was cloned into inverse PCR-linearized pBAD18 using In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (Takara Bio). Mutant plasmids were constructed using site-directed mutagenesis.


### Media and bacterial cultures
  E. coli cells were grown at 37 °C in LB medium (Nacalai Tesque). Ampicillin (Amp) (100 µg/mL), chloramphenicol (Cm) (20 µg/mL), and spectinomycin (Spc) (50 µg/mL) were added as appropriate for plasmid-bearing cell growth and transformant selection. Bacterial growth was monitored with a Mini photo 518R (660 nm; TAITEC Co., Japan).


### Lysis assay
  E. coli BL21 ΔrecA (DE3) cells carrying a plasmid encoding one of the SglPP7 derivatives were grown overnight in LB medium supplemented with the appropriate antibiotics. The cultures were diluted 1:10,000 in phosphate-buffered saline and spotted onto LB agar plates supplemented with 0.2% (w/v) L-arabinose and the necessary antibiotics. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 22 h and subsequently imaged.


### Sample preparation of the MurJ/SglPP7 complex
  E. coli BL21 ΔrecA (DE3) cells harboring the plasmids pKG537, pKH32, and pRM1210, encoding Met-Ala-EcMurJ2-511 (from E. coli JCM 20135)-3× FLAG, Met-His10-SglPP7, and BsAmj, respectively, were cultured in LB medium (Lennox, Nacalai) supplemented with 100 µg/mL Amp, 20 µg/mL Cm, and 50 µg/mL Spc at 37 °C. Protein expression was induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and 0.2% (w/v) L-arabinose. Protein purification was performed as described previously [19]. The membrane fractions were isolated by ultracentrifugation and solubilized in buffer containing 2% n-dodecyl β-maltoside (DDM), 0.02% lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG), and 0.002% cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS). The protein was subsequently purified using Ni-NTA agarose resin (QIAGEN), followed by a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva). The MurJ/SglPP7 complex was finally purified in buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 0.005% LMNG, 0.0005% CHS, and 0.1 mM PMSF) and concentrated to ~20 mg/mL using an Amicon Ultra 30 K NMWL (MERCK). Nanodisc reconstitution was performed as described previously [19]. The purified MurJ/SglPP7 complex was mixed with MSP1D1 [31] and E. coli phospholipids (Avanti) at a molar ratio of 1.4:1:120 in buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 0.005% LMNG, 0.0005% CHS, and 0.1 mM PMSF). The mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. Then, the detergent was removed by Bio-Beads SM2 (Bio-Rad). The resulting nanodiscs were further purified using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column preequilibrated with buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 0.1 mM PMSF). The MurJ/SglPP7-reconstituted nanodisc was concentrated using an Amicon Ultra 50 K NMWL.


### Cryo-EM data collection and processing
  Quantifoil holey carbon grids (Cu R1.2/R1.3, 300 mesh) were glow-discharged at 7 Pa and 10 mA for 10 s using a JEC-3000FC sputter coater (JEOL) before sample application. A 3 μL aliquot of 2.8 mg/mL MurJ/SglPP7 complex-reconstituted nanodiscs was applied onto the grid, blotted for 2 s at 100% humidity, 8 °C, and blot force 10, and plunged into liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

  The cryo-EM dataset was collected on a CRYO ARM 300 transmission electron microscope (JEOL) operated at an accelerating 300 kV, equipped with a cold-field emission gun, an in-column Omega-type energy filter, and a Gatan K3 direct electron detector (Gatan) at SPring-8. Images were collected at a nominal magnification of 60,000×, corresponding to a calibrated pixel size of 0.752 Å/pixel, 50 frames per image with a total exposure dose of 50 e−/Å2 using a SerialEM [32].

  Data processing was performed using cryoSPARC v4.6.2–v5.0.6 software [33]. The cryo-EM data-processing workflow for the MurJ/SglPP7 complex is shown in S1D Fig. A total of 17,650 movies were aligned using patch motion correction, and the contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated using the Patch CTF estimation. The particles were automatically picked using the Blob picker in cryoSPARC from 200 of the 17,650 micrographs. Particles were extracted with downsampling via Fourier cropping to a pixel size of 1.504 Å and subjected to 2D classification, generating 2D class averages. Using these 2D class averages as templates, particles were repicked using the Template Picker from 17,650 micrographs, yielding 7,554,685 particles. The repicked particles were extracted with downsampling via Fourier cropping to the pixel size of 1.504 Å and subjected to five rounds of 2D classification (K = 350, 350, 300, 250, 250). After 2D classification, 1,777,257 high-quality particles were subjected to ab initio 3D reconstruction (K = 4), generating initial 3D models. After two rounds of 2D classification, the 3,749,310 particles were then subjected to four rounds of heterogeneous refinement (K = 4, 4, 4, 4) using the initial models as references, removing junk particles. The best 3D class, containing 453,217 particles, was re-extracted to 0.752 Å/pixel and refined using Non-Uniform (NU) refinement. After NU refinement, these particles were subjected to reference-based motion correction and then to NU refinement, and local refinement using the mask covering the entire complex, including the nanodisc. These processes yielded a 3D cryo-EM map with an estimated overall resolution of 3.2 Å. A sharpened map was generated using the Sharpening Tool in cryoSPARC with a B-factor of −100 Å2. Local resolution was estimated using the Local Resolution Estimation job in cryoSPARC.


### Model building and refinement
  The SglPP7 structure was predicted using AlphaFold3 [21]. The predicted SglPP7 model and an EcMurJ outward model from the MurJ/LysM complex structure (PDB ID: 9UKV) were docked into the cryo-EM map using UCSF ChimeraX. The model was manually modified in Coot [34], and further refinement was conducted using phenix.real_space_refine in PHENIX [35]. Data processing and refinement statistics are provided in S1 Fig and S1 Table. The final molecular model and cryo-EM map were visualized using PyMOL (https://pymol.org/) or UCSF ChimeraX [36].


### MD simulations of MurJ and the MurJ/SglPP7 complex in membrane
  MD simulations were performed for two systems, the MurJ/SglPP7 complex and SglPP7-free MurJ using YASARA dynamics software [37] (https://www.yasara.org/). The simulations were set up using the membrane MD macro (md_runmembrane.mcr). Each system was embedded in a mixed palmitoyloleoylphosphatidylethanolamine/palmitoyloleoylphosphatidylglycerol (POPE/POPG) lipid bilayer and solvated in a 0.9% NaCl solution. The hydrogen-bonding network was optimized before simulation to improve structural stability [38]. After steepest-descent and simulated-annealing energy minimization to remove steric clashes, production simulations were carried out for 120 ns using the AMBER14 force field [39]. Van der Waals interactions were calculated with an 8 Å cutoff, whereas long-range electrostatic interactions were treated without a cutoff using the particle mesh Ewald algorithm [40,41]. The equations of motion were integrated using a multiple time-step scheme with time steps of 1.25 fs for bonded interactions and 2.5 fs for non-bonded interactions under NPT conditions at 298 K and 1 atm, as implemented in YASARA [42]. To reduce bias, three independent simulations were performed using distinct POPE/POPG membrane environments. For the MurJ/SglPP7 complex, Run 1 contained 169 POPE and 61 POPG lipids, Run 2 contained 175 POPE and 58 POPG lipids, and Run 3 contained 169 POPE and 64 POPG lipids. For SglPP7-free MurJ, Run 1 contained 177 POPE and 60 POPG lipids, Run 2 contained 173 POPE and 58 POPG lipids, and Run 3 contained 174 POPE and 64 POPG lipids. The Cα RMSD of MurJ in each simulation was examined to verify the stability and flexibility of the complexes.
