# CRAFT: an antigen-specific TCR generative model for conditional de novo design with physicochemical constraints


## Abstract
  T-cell receptors (TCRs) are essential to adaptive immunity and mediate the recognition and elimination of malignant or infected cells through antigen-specific interactions. Despite the clinical progress of TCR-based immunotherapies, computational de novo design of antigen-specific TCRs remains difficult. The main difficulties include the complexity of peptide–major histocompatibility complex (pMHC)-dependent recognition, strict physicochemical requirements at the binding interface, and the small amount of paired training data available for model development. Current generative approaches depend largely on 1D sequence statistics and do not capture pMHC presentation context, interfacial physicochemical properties, and native receptor priors within a unified framework. We present conditional reconstruction of antigen fragments for TCRs (CRAFT), a decoupled generative model for pMHC-conditioned de novo design of antigen-specific TCR CDR3β  sequences under physicochemical constraints. With large-scale pretraining, CRAFT learns base representations that help reduce the limitations imposed by data scarcity. We further incorporate antigen-derived contextual information to guide antigen-specific TCR CDR3β  sequence design. The architecture comprises an antigen encoder (PrimeBERT) that captures the pMHC context and an autoregressive decoder (PrimeTCR) that preserves native receptor priors. The encoder and decoder are coupled through cross-attention and augmented with Atchley factor embeddings, allowing CRAFT to incorporate physicochemical constraints relevant to antigen-conditioned receptor design during sequence decoding. Across multiple evaluations, CRAFT demonstrates competitive sequence naturalness, together with favorable performance in specific binding, and predicted structural plausibility. Overall, CRAFT provides a computational framework for developing personalized tumor immunotherapies. It also holds potential to support clinical translation by enabling the efficient, targeted discovery of candidate TCRs.


## Introduction
  The continued development of T-cell engineering is reshaping cancer immunotherapy and advancing precision medicine [1–5]. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has demonstrated substantial efficacy in hematologic malignancies [6, 7]. However, CAR-T cell recognition relies on stable surface antigen expression. This dependency limits its application in highly heterogeneous solid tumors, where antigen loss and immune escape frequently occur [8, 9]. In contrast, engineered T-cell receptors (TCRs) circumvent this surface-target restriction by recognizing intracellularly derived peptides presented by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, including neoantigens arising from tumor-associated mutations [10, 11]. This recognition mechanism expands the addressable antigen space and supports mutation-directed individualized immunotherapy [12–14].

  Native TCR diversity stems primarily from V(D)J gene rearrangement of highly variable α  and β  chains [15–17]. This process creates a massive and sparse receptor sequence space. Within this space, the complementarity-determining region 3 of the β  chain (CDR3β ) directly engages the peptide–MHC (pMHC) complex and dictates the precision of antigen recognition. Accordingly, the CDR3β  loop has emerged as a primary target for antigen-conditioned de novo candidate generation in computational TCR design [18, 19]. Exploring this enormous sequence space via traditional experimental screening is prohibitively resource-intensive. To address these bottlenecks, a central challenge in computational modeling is to efficiently capture the sequence constraints linked to pMHC recognition and incorporate immunogenic information into target-specific CDR3β  design while preserving native-like receptor features [20].

  Deep learning has substantially transformed the traditional trial-and-error paradigm of protein engineering [21–23]. Protein language models, such as ESM (Evolutionary Scale Model) [24] and ProGen [25], are trained via unsupervised learning on large-scale protein sequence corpora to learn long-range evolutionary constraints, local compositional rules, and higher-order sequence patterns [23]. They provide effective generation priors for de novo sequence design. Recent advances in computational protein modeling have enabled protein design to go beyond sequence-level optimization [26]. Methods such as RoseTTAFold All-Atom [27], ProteinMPNN [28], and RFdiffusion3 [29] can integrate structural information into the design process and also support the construction of complex functional topologies [30]. Related progress has also informed automated antibody design [31–36].

  However, these advances in protein and antibody design are not directly transferable to TCR design. Unlike surface-binding antibodies [37], TCRs recognize pMHC interfaces that involve geometric complementarity, conformational variability, and inherently weak binding affinities [38–42]. These features make computational TCR generation substantially more challenging [43]. The difficulty is further compounded by a highly sparse sequence space, stringent interfacial physicochemical constraints, and limited paired training data [44]. Recent studies in computational immunology have therefore developed specialized frameworks for immunogenic TCR generation [19, 44, 45]. For example, GRATCR [46] adopts a Seq2Seq encoder–decoder architecture for pMHC-guided generation, whereas ER-TRANSFORMER [47] introduces bidirectional cross-attention to improve interface-level modeling. In addition, TCR-VALID [45] employs a variational autoencoder to sample candidates from a continuous latent space. Parallel to these deep learning architectures, many current approaches rely on sequence-similarity retrieval or 1D language-model frequency patterns [48–50]. Although these statistical strategies can recover local compositional regularities from native repertoires [51], they do not reliably preserve key interfacial features when designing TCRs for specific or previously unseen antigens [52].

  Despite these conceptual advances, few current generative architectures jointly and explicitly represent the pMHC background and physicochemical constraints that shape TCR recognition [53–55]. Computationally, training a conditional generator directly on limited and heterogeneous paired data often overfits the model to local training distributions. This optimization approach erodes the native generative behavior and sequence diversity of the decoder. Therefore, the field requires a unified framework capable of preserving natural generative priors while accommodating explicit biological constraints through a stable optimization pathway.

  In this study, we aim to generate TCR CDR3β  sequences that retain native-like characteristics under antigenic conditions while remaining compatible with the relevant pMHC presentation context [56]. To achieve this, we propose CRAFT. CRAFT employs a decoupled design strategy. It separately models antigen-side conditioning and receptor-side autoregressive generation, ultimately coupling them through an explicit conditioning mechanism. Specifically, an antigen-side encoding module represents epitope sequences and their presentation background [57]. Simultaneously, a receptor-side generative module learns the autoregressive distribution of CDR3β  sequences [58]. CRAFT incorporates antigen-derived information into the decoding process via a cross-attention conditioning mechanism. This mechanism allows conditional representations to interact directly with receptor-side decoding states at each generation step. Instead of relying on a monolithic architecture, CRAFT preserves the distinct representational strengths of both modalities and dynamically integrates them during sequence decoding.

  CRAFT advances conditional TCR generation beyond 1D statistical fitting by incorporating both the antigen-presentation context and residue-level physicochemical constraints. Rather than relying on computationally expensive 3D structural supervision, this framework uses pMHC-conditioned representations and explicit physicochemical descriptors to guide sequence decoding toward more plausible candidates. By introducing antigen-side contextual information early in decoding [59], CRAFT promotes physicochemical compatibility while preserving the native generative behavior of the receptor. Introducing antigen-derived contextual cues at the start of sampling defines an allowable design range that maintains physicochemical coherence without altering the receptor’s intrinsic generative behavior. Furthermore, the cross-attention mechanism offers an interpretable lens for examining position-level interaction patterns under antigenic conditioning. The following sections evaluate this framework across sequence naturalness, conditional generation fidelity, and predicted structural plausibility.


## Results

### Overview of the CRAFT framework
  An overview of the CRAFT workflow and model architecture is shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S1. CRAFT is designed for the de novo generation of TCR CDR3β  sequences in a defined pMHC context, with the aim of producing candidates that retain naturalness, specificity, and predicted structural plausibility under antigen-conditioned and physicochemical design objectives (Fig. 1a and b). Unlike sequence-only generative models [60], CRAFT explicitly incorporates the pMHC presentation background and residue-level physicochemical constraints derived from Atchley factors. This design allows conditional cues and sequence properties to be handled within a unified framework and biases early sampling toward more plausible candidates. At the model level, CRAFT adopts an asymmetric transformer encoder–decoder architecture, in which PrimeBERT encodes epitope and presentation-context information, whereas PrimeTCR performs conditional CDR3β  generation (Fig. 1c and d). Within this architecture, the Epitope Blocks illustrated in the schematic correspond to the pretrained BERT encoder layers of PrimeBERT. In parallel, the TCR Blocks represent the GPT2-based autoregressive decoder layers that constitute PrimeTCR (Supplementary Fig. S1). PrimeBERT and PrimeTCR are first pretrained to learn general representations of epitopes and natural receptor sequences, respectively, and are then adapted to the antigen-conditioned CDR3β  generation task through transfer learning-based fine-tuning on paired antigen–TCR data (Supplementary Table S1).

  To systematically validate this design, the following sections evaluate CRAFT from four complementary perspectives: repertoire-level statistical conformity, immunogenic sequence generation, structural plausibility, and residue-level interpretability. These results suggest that CRAFT may provide an extensible computational foundation for advancing antigen-specific TCR engineering in precision immunotherapy.


### CRAFT generates statistically natural and combinatorially consistent receptor libraries

Nevertheless, CRAFT largely avoided the long-tail truncation and aberrantly short sequences reported for earlier recurrent neural network architectures [61]; comparisons with other generative models are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. ### CRAFT balances sequence fidelity, conditional separability, and diversity in receptor library generation
  Building on the close statistical agreement between CRAFT-generated sequences and the global profile of native TCR repertoires, we evaluated the model regarding sequence fidelity, conditional separation, and repertoire diversity. When evaluated using character-level BLEU (Char-BLEU) [66] and native sequence recovery (NSR) rate metrics, CRAFT, TcrDesign, and the large-scale pretrained model TCR-T5 formed the top-performing group among all evaluated methods (Fig. 2d–e). Specifically, in the Char-BLEU assessment, CRAFT exhibited a score distribution centered above 0.50, with the bulk of generated sequences achieving scores broadly comparable with those of TCR-T5 and TcrDesign. In contrast, TCR-epiDiff produced markedly lower scores, with its distribution concentrated in the lower range of the evaluation spectrum. Under the more stringent criterion of NSR rate, CRAFT reached 0.5915, closely matching both TCR-T5 (0.5989) and TcrDesign (0.5976), while substantially outperforming diffusion-based TCR-epiDiff (0.4158) and GRATCR (0.5474). Taken together, these findings indicated that CRAFT maintained robust sequence-level fidelity in preserving local sequence patterns and recovering native receptors, consistently matching the performance of highly parameterized state-of-the-art baselines.

  When sequence collections generated under different epitope settings were compared, CRAFT outputs remained closely tied to the conditioning input. Jaccard similarity analysis (Supplementary Table S2) yielded a mean pairwise score of 0.00065 across epitope-specific sets, indicating only limited sharing between antigen contexts. Repertoires produced under distinct epitope conditions therefore stayed clearly separated, with little convergence from one condition to another. At the same time, this conditional separation was not accompanied by a noticeable loss of candidate diversity. The fraction of nonredundant CRAFT sequences was 0.940, approaching the 0.960 observed in the natural reference repertoire, while within-epitope duplication remained low throughout. Together, these measurements indicate that CRAFT preserves condition-dependent separation without narrowing the diversity of the generated repertoire and continues to sample a broad expanse of sequence space.

  A position-wise Shannon entropy profile provided a complementary view of the generated sequence distribution (Fig. 2f). Entropy differences near the two anchor segments of the CDR3β  loop remained close to zero, whereas the central variable segment showed a downward shift (Δ entropy ≈ -3.5), producing a V-shaped profile. This localized entropy reduction is consistent with stronger epitope-conditioned sequence preferences in the central CDR3β  region, but it may also reflect reduced local exploration and should therefore not be interpreted as intrinsically favorable. When considered together with the recovery rates, Jaccard indices, and uniqueness metrics, the results suggest that CRAFT introduced nonuniform conditional constraints while retaining substantial candidate-level diversity, rather than undergoing uniform repertoire-wide contraction. These sequence-level observations provided the basis for the subsequent structural analyses.

  The 3D structural prediction metrics provided an orthogonal line of evidence supporting these sequence-level observations (Supplementary Table S4). In assessing local conformational quality, CRAFT achieved the highest predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) score among all evaluated methods, reaching 0.9169. Furthermore, CRAFT yielded the lowest backbone root mean square deviation (RMSD) at 1.366 Å, indicating minimal geometric deviation from the natural structural references. In terms of the predicted Template Modeling (pTM) score, which reflects global topological accuracy, CRAFT reached 0.719, remaining close to TcrDesign, the top-scoring method at 0.721. Taken together, these readouts were consistent with structurally plausible and internally coherent CRAFT-generated candidates at the prediction level. A classification-based surrogate model was also used as an added assessment (Supplementary Table S4). Absolute scores stayed low across all methods, but CRAFT maintained a comparatively even pattern of performance (see Materials and Methods).


### Ablation analysis of matched pMHC conditioning and core CRAFT components
  To determine whether the performance of CRAFT arose from matched pMHC conditioning rather than from the unconditional receptor prior or the generation procedure alone, we compared the full model with controlled variants under the same data split, decoding settings, and post-processing protocol. These variants included a PrimeTCR-only model, a mismatched pMHC control, models without Atchley-factor inputs or physicochemical regularization, and simplified conditioning alternatives. Across common, rare, and novel epitope subsets, full CRAFT showed the most favorable TCRBinder-predicted binding-probability distributions (Fig. 4a–c). In particular, removing the antigen-side condition or replacing the cognate pMHC with a mismatched context reduced the predicted binding scores, indicating that the observed performance was not explained by the autoregressive receptor prior alone. The corresponding analyses of sequence-level metrics and structural rationality metrics showed concordant trends (Supplementary Figs S5–S6; Supplementary Tables S5 and S6). These results provide computational evidence that matched pMHC information contributes to target-specific CDR3β  generation, while experimental validation will be required to establish the functional properties of individual candidates.

  We next performed component-specific ablations to resolve the contributions of the physicochemical representation, physicochemical regularization, decoupled pretraining, and conditional-decoding interface. To determine whether the fixed Atchley representation provided information beyond a generic residue embedding, we compared the full CRAFT model with three variants: one without Atchley inputs, one using learned 5D residue features, and one using fixed random 5D residue features. Full CRAFT retained more favorable binding-probability and BLOSUM62 distributions across the three epitope-frequency regimes (Supplementary Figs S7–S9; Supplementary Tables S7 and S8). We further evaluated the physicochemical regularization term by setting λ =0 and sweeping λ  from 0 to 2.0; the validation analysis identified λ =0.5 as providing the most balanced performance across sequence recovery, predicted binding, similarity, and structural readouts (Supplementary Figs S10–S11). In parallel, CRAFT initialized with independently pretrained PrimeBERT and PrimeTCR outperformed variants without pretraining or with encoder-only or decoder-only pretraining, particularly in the rare and novel epitope subsets (Supplementary Figs S12–S14 and Supplementary Tables S9 and S10). Finally, replacing cross-attention with prefix or pooled-CLS conditioning, or removing residual fusion, reduced the sequence-level and predicted-binding performance relative to full CRAFT (Supplementary Figs S15–S17 and Supplementary Tables S11 and S12). Together, these analyses indicate that the complete model benefits from the coordinated use of matched pMHC conditioning, dual-branch pretraining, physicochemical regularization, and token-level cross-attention with residual fusion.

  To assess how antigen conditioning shaped the regions of sequence space reached by CRAFT, we embedded >20 000 TCR CDR3β  sequences generated with and without antigen conditioning into a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) space trained on authentic antigen–TCR pairings (Fig. 5a). The generated set occupied the main high-density neighborhoods associated with dominant natural clonotypes and extended into nearby low-occupancy areas, which kept the spatial pattern continuous around the major natural clusters. Representative sequences were then drawn from multiple cluster-defined regions and evaluated by ESM3-based [67] structural prediction. Predicted folding confidence remained high across these candidates, with TM-scores (Fig. 5a) ranging from 0.82 to 0.89 and pTM scores from 0.83 to 0.89. Evidence from similarity distributions, structure-related metrics, surrogate-model evaluation, and latent-space placement showed that CRAFT sustained immunogenic sequence generation across common, rare, and novel epitope settings. At the same time, the model retained sequence fidelity and predicted structural plausibility while enlarging the accessible candidate space.


### Structural characterization and interface distribution analysis of CRAFT-generated TCR candidates for clinically relevant cancer epitopes
  To examine the structural behavior of CRAFT-generated candidates in clinically relevant cancer settings, we selected two representative driver mutation-derived neoantigens (Supplementary Table S13) for further analysis: the TP53 R175H-derived peptide HMTEVVRHC [68] and KRAS G12-associated peptides, including VVGAVGVGK and related variants [69, 70]. The corresponding sequence logos are shown in Fig. 3e, with additional sequence views provided in Supplementary Fig. S18. For each target, CRAFT was used to generate a raw set of up to 1000 CDR3β  candidates. Complex structures for the corresponding post-filtered valid sequences were then predicted using ESM3 [67] or AlphaFold3 [71], followed by downstream structural analyses. Across the resulting candidate sets, predicted structural confidence remained consistently high, yielding a cohort-average pLDDT above 0.90 and an average pTM above 0.80. Subsequently, representative high-confidence complexes were aligned against experimentally resolved native structures (Fig. 5b–c; Supplementary Figs S19 and S20). For TP53 R175H (PDB IDs: 6VQO and 7RM4) and KRAS G12 (PDB IDs: 9IKY and 7OW6), complex predictions containing the CRAFT-generated CDR3β  candidates showed strong agreement with the native references, with TM-scores exceeding 0.93 and backbone RMSD values below 1.30 Å after global alignment using TM-align [72]. Structural superposition further revealed that, despite marked variation in amino acid composition among the generated CDR3β  sequences, their overall orientation above the pMHC surface and the anchoring positions of the backbone scaffold remained closely aligned with the native conformations. These case studies indicate close predicted structural agreement among selected CDR3β -conditioned complexes in cancer-related neoantigen contexts.

  We then analyzed multiple cancer-associated epitopes to compare interface-level structural distributions across different antigen backgrounds. For each target, solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) was derived from the predicted static structures of the valid post-filtered candidates (Fig. 6a). In this set of epitopes, SASA, as a readout of interface exposure and geometric compactness, followed a broadly similar distribution. For several targets, the generated ensembles showed lower median SASA values than those of the static reference structures of the native complexes (Fig. 6a, star markers), which corresponded to more compact interface geometries. Epitopes with relatively rigid interfaces, including KQWLVWLFL, also had a narrower interquartile SASA range, indicating a higher level of geometric consistency within the generated structures. We also assessed Δ SASA, a measure of buried SASA, and observed epitope-dependent differences in interface burial (Fig. 6b). Candidate sets for ALHGGWTTK and VVGAVGVGK had broad Δ SASA distributions, whereas HMTEVVRHC remained tightly constrained and stayed near the native reference. These SASA comparisons showed that CRAFT-generated interfaces were not confined to one fixed geometric state and instead preserved epitope-specific differences in compactness and conformational flexibility. This pattern was also supported by the structural alignments of representative neoantigens and by the interface-level differentiation observed across clinically relevant targets.


### Attention in CRAFT aligns with putative TCR contact regions
  We also examined whether the attention learned by CRAFT was concentrated in residues likely to mediate interaction. In the LLFGYPVYV-associated complex, the global attention heatmap (Fig. 6c) showed that the strongest signal was confined to a limited region connecting the antigenic peptide with the TCR β -chain. Across the β -chain framework region, attention remained weak and diffuse, whereas the CDR3β  loop formed a distinct band of higher attention weights. This distribution indicates that model attention during sequence generation was preferentially localized to the variable CDR3β  region rather than dispersed across the full β -chain scaffold.

  The residue interaction matrix centered on the CDR3β  loop (Fig. 6d) contained several localized high-attention regions, in which defined antigen-side positions corresponded to specific receptor residues. In the predicted 3D structure (Fig. 6e), these regions partially overlapped with clustered putative interface contacts, linking the 1D attention pattern to structurally relevant interaction regions in this representative example.

  To determine whether this correspondence extended beyond the LLFGYPVYV example, we further analyzed 30 TCR–pMHC complexes with available structures. Residues were classified as high-attention positions using normalized attention thresholds of 0.50, 0.55, and 0.60 and were compared with structurally annotated TCR–pMHC contact residues. At these thresholds, the mean numbers of high-attention residues per complex were 13, 10, and 8, respectively, of which an average of 6, 4, and 2 residues overlapped with the contact regions. The corresponding contact proportions among high-attention residues were 46.2%, 40.3%, and 25.0%, respectively. These results indicate that high-attention residues were partially enriched at the TCR–pMHC interface, particularly at the moderate thresholds of 0.50 and 0.55. However, the reduced correspondence at the more stringent threshold suggests that attention is not an exclusive proxy for direct physical contacts and may additionally reflect sequence context, conformational support, or longer-range dependencies. Overall, the attention analysis provides supportive, rather than causal, evidence that CRAFT captures structurally relevant patterns during antigen-conditioned sequence generation.


## Discussion
  In this study, we presented CRAFT, a computational framework for the antigen-conditioned de novo generation of specific TCR CDR3β  candidates. By integrating self-supervised pretraining on large-scale unlabeled immune repertoires with targeted fine-tuning on paired antigen–TCR data, CRAFT showed favorable performance across multiple benchmark settings. Specifically, our evaluations showed consistent advantages in biochemical conformity, specificity, sequence-level fidelity under rare-epitope settings, and predicted structural plausibility in neoantigen-related settings. Importantly, CRAFT should not be interpreted as uniformly outperforming TcrDesign or TCR-T5 across all measures of sequence naturalness. Rather, it achieves comparable native-like sequence fidelity while maintaining competitive predicted local structural quality within a pMHC-conditioned and physicochemically guided generation framework. Its main advantage therefore lies in balancing conditional specificity, candidate diversity, and structural plausibility, rather than maximizing every individual evaluation metric. This work suggests that automated TCR generation can move beyond 1D sequence resemblance toward structurally informed, antigen-conditioned candidate generation.

  The balanced performance of CRAFT across these evaluation dimensions is consistent with its overall design strategy, although the current evidence is derived primarily from sequence-level metrics and predicted structural analyses. Specifically, the functional separation between PrimeBERT for epitope-context representation and PrimeTCR for receptor generation allowed antigen-side cues to be integrated with the generative priors learned from natural TCR repertoires. This decoupled architecture accounts for the stable sequence behavior observed even in rare-epitope settings. Furthermore, the cross-attention-based conditioning scheme is consistent with the condition-dependent separation and the exploration of nearby sequence neighborhoods observed in the generated candidates. Moreover, the explicit physicochemical constraints introduced via Atchley factors may contribute to the high local conformational quality and the predicted specific interface-related geometric patterns observed in the generated complexes. Rather than claiming complete proof of the underlying biological mechanisms, we interpret these architectural choices as a plausible explanation for the model’s observed computational performance.

  CRAFT showed favorable results in sequence-level fidelity, sequence specificity, generalization capacity, and predicted structural plausibility, but additional evaluation dimensions still need to be explored. Importantly, the structural, interface-related, and attention-based analyses in this study are computational rather than direct validation of pMHC–TCR binding or T-cell function. Experimental binding and functional assays and, where feasible, structural characterization will therefore be required to validate these predictions. At present, the framework is centered on TCR CDR3β  sequence generation. A next step will be to extend it to full heterodimer design by combining hierarchical conditional decoding with structure-based co-generation so that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha /\beta $\end{document} inter-chain coordination can be modeled within the same design process. We also plan to connect this computational framework with comprehensive in vitro and ex vivo validation in a closed iterative pipeline. Such a scheme will allow functional assessment of prioritized candidates and will provide a basis for subsequent preclinical evaluation.

  CRAFT serves as a computational framework for antigen-specific de novo generation of TCR CDR3β  candidates. In sequence-based and prediction-based analyses, the model performed well in sequence fidelity, specificity, conditional separation, and predicted structural plausibility, which supported candidate generation, prioritization, and structure-guided follow-up assessment. The value of this study lies in bringing sequence generation, conditional control, and structure-based evaluation into a single workflow that links raw candidate production with downstream filtering. With the continued growth of large-scale experimental validation data, future work can use this iterative modeling strategy to investigate wider translational uses and to probe deeper immunological mechanisms [73].


## Materials and methods

### Datasets and preprocessing

#### Pretraining datasets
  Self-supervised pretraining was based on two independent unlabeled sequence collections. One collection was used for antigen epitope encoding, and the other was used for modeling TCR CDR3β  sequences. Epitope records were gathered from the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) [74], yielding >3 million unique sequences for PrimeBERT pretraining. Natural TCR CDR3β  sequences were obtained from TCRdb 2.0 [75], which provided >4.5 million entries for autoregressive pretraining of PrimeTCR. These datasets were used to initialize the encoder and decoder modules. In the preprocessing step, only complete epitope records containing standard amino acid characters were kept, and repeated entries were removed to generate the final antigen corpus. No global deduplication was performed for the TCRdb 2.0 CDR3β  dataset, which allowed the original repertoire frequency distribution to remain represented in the training data.


#### TCR–pMHC pairing dataset
  A supervised TCR–pMHC paired dataset was compiled from several public databases, including McPAS-TCR [76], VDJdb [77], TRAIT [78], MIRA [79], IEDB [74], and STCRDab [80]. These resources provided epitope peptides together with matched TCR sequences and were used to form supervised inputs for positive-pair conditional generation. Records from STCRDab were kept in the integrated dataset because this resource contains experimentally supported antigen–TCR binding entries and could serve as a structural reference. All retrieved entries were first converted to a common sequence format and then organized into paired samples. After this harmonization step, each sample contained an epitope peptide, a paired TCR CDR3β  sequence, and the corresponding observed antigen–TCR association.

  The integrated dataset was then cleaned and standardized. Malformed, incomplete, and unparsable records were excluded. Noncanonical or ambiguous residue symbols that could still be parsed, including “-,” “#,” “*,” and “X,” were converted to a single unknown token ([UNK]). All epitope and CDR3β  sequences were truncated or padded to a maximum length of 30 amino acids so that input tensors had consistent dimensions. In CDR3β  sequences, the conserved terminal residues were kept unchanged, namely the N-terminal cysteine (C) and the C-terminal phenylalanine (F) or tryptophan (W), in order to preserve the boundary features that characterize naturally recombined loops. After dataset merging, deduplication, and standardization, the final collection comprised >130 000 supervised training pairs and 24 000 independent validation pairs.

  The training and validation sets were segregated at the epitope level, ensuring that all peptides present in the validation set were excluded from model training. To facilitate subsequent generalization benchmarking, the independent validation set was further partitioned into three mutually exclusive subsets according to the number of known paired CDR3β  sequences available for each epitope: common, rare, and novel. Specifically, the common subset comprised unseen epitopes with relatively extensive paired-sequence support (>20 pairs), the rare subset contained unseen epitopes with intermediate support (10–20 pairs), and the novel subset denoted unseen low-resource epitopes with limited paired-sequence support (<10 pairs), including selected neoantigen-related contexts where applicable. Crucially, while these subsets represent different levels of biological sequence abundance, all of them remained completely unobserved during the training phase. This partition strategy enabled an epitope-level zero-shot evaluation of model generalization across unseen antigenic contexts with different levels of paired-sequence support, ranging from relatively well-supported to low-resource settings.


#### pMHC context representation
  In this study, we incorporated the MHC presentation background alongside the epitope to define a pMHC context for epitope–receptor pairing. This formulation allowed the conditional input to represent antigen identity together with its associated presentation context during CDR3β  generation. By integrating allele-specific sequences across diverse human leukocyte antigen subtypes, the model accommodated highly heterogeneous presentation contexts and enabled conditional epitope-receptor learning within a multi-allelic landscape. This configuration allowed the network to effectively capture the mappings between epitopes and CDR3β  sequences across varied antigenic backgrounds.

  At the sequence-representation level, the target CDR3β  sequence was modeled as a conditional autoregressive process. Given a conditional input c, the model generated the receptor sequence from left to right, where c consisted of the epitope sequence and the corresponding pMHC context. Antigen identity and presentation context were combined into one conditional input and used for PrimeBERT encoding as well as the following conditional decoding step. The target sequence was expressed as standard amino acid residues and generated autoregressively from the sequence prefix available at each decoding step.

  Protein sequence representation was implemented using a deterministic residue-fragment tokenizer rather than a learned subword method such as BPE or WordPiece. Sequences were segmented from left to right using a longest-match strategy that preferentially selected tripeptide, dipeptide, and single-residue tokens. Each vocabulary contained 8426 tokens covering canonical residue fragments and special tokens. Peptide-side inputs and CDR3β  sequences shared the same fragment inventory and segmentation rule but used separate token-to-index mappings and embedding spaces. For pMHC representation, the MHC pseudo-sequence was encoded residue by residue and concatenated before the fragment-tokenized epitope sequence. In parallel, amino acid residues were mapped to continuous physicochemical vectors using 5D Atchley factors. The resulting discrete pMHC representation and physicochemical features jointly defined the antigen-side conditional input for CDR3β  generation. Details of vocabulary construction, special tokens, input serialization, and uncommon-residue handling are provided in Supplementary Note 2.


### Model architecture of CRAFT

#### Technical implementation of the CRAFT architecture
  CRAFT adopts an encoder–decoder architecture for antigen-conditioned generation of TCR CDR3β  sequences. Its design includes two modules with separate roles. One module encodes epitope sequences and their presentation context on the antigen side. The other generates TCR CDR3β  sequences in an autoregressive manner on the receptor side. Within this information flow, antigen-side conditions are initially transformed into contextual embeddings by the encoder. These embeddings are subsequently injected into the decoder via a conditioning mechanism, merging with receptor-side autoregressive states during generation. Structurally, this pipeline advances directly from antigen representation through conditioned integration into receptor generation. At the methodological level, CRAFT embeds antigen-derived cues directly into the sequence generation phase, thereby reducing reliance on extensive post hoc filtering after unconditional language modeling. The following subsections detail the implementations of PrimeBERT, PrimeTCR, and the cross-attention-based conditioned decoding.

### Training strategy

#### Pretraining strategy
  CRAFT underwent a rigorous two-stage training procedure comprising initial self-supervised pretraining followed by conditional fine-tuning. During the first stage, distinct unlabeled antigen-side and receptor-side corpora explicitly initialized the network’s encoder and decoder, respectively. During the second stage, paired antigen–TCR data strictly drove the supervised conditional optimization (Supplementary Table S1). This sequential design ensured pretraining established fundamental unimodal initializations across both network branches, whereas conditional fine-tuning provided antigen-dependent supervision driving targeted end-to-end parameter updating.

  During initial pretraining, PrimeBERT and PrimeTCR underwent strictly independent optimization. PrimeBERT employed a masked language modeling (MLM) objective. #### Inference and filtering of generated sequences
  At inference time, target CDR3β  sequences were generated with autoregressive beam search using a beam width of 50. Under this setting, multiple candidate paths were retained at each decoding step. Log-probability ranking was then used to order candidate sequences for downstream selection. The number of raw outputs was specified in advance for each type of analysis. For structural case evaluation and cross-epitope interface statistics, 1000 candidate sequences were generated for each queried antigen or epitope. For latent-space analysis, >20 000 sequences were generated and projected onto a UMAP space constructed from antigen–TCR binding pairs in the training set. All raw outputs were generated before post-processing, and no dynamic replenishment of valid sequences was introduced during decoding.

  Post-processing was carried out in a fixed sequence. Within each antigen, the raw sequence pool was first screened for invalid characters and duplicate outputs. Sequences containing noncanonical, corrupted, or unresolved characters were removed, and exact-match duplicates generated for the same antigen were collapsed into a single entry. The remaining sequences were then filtered by endpoint and length requirements, so that only outputs with the expected anchoring pattern of an N-terminal cysteine and a C-terminal phenylalanine or tryptophan were kept. Beam rank was preserved after filtering. For structure-based analyses with a raw generation budget of n sequences (n denotes the user-specified number of raw sequences to be generated and can be set to any positive integer, such as 1 or 1000), all downstream calculations were performed on the resulting valid set. If more than n valid sequences remained, the top n by beam rank were retained. If fewer than 100 passed filtering, all valid sequences were used. The number of sequences entering structural analysis was therefore determined by the fixed raw generation budget together with the same filtering scheme, and no extra resampling was used to force a constant final size. The retained counts for each antigen or epitope are provided separately in the Supplementary Materials to maintain correspondence with the downstream structural and interface analyses.

  The finalized sequence sets were then carried forward to downstream analyses. For practical prioritization toward experimental validation, valid candidates were assessed using a staged multi-criterion workflow rather than a single unvalidated composite score. Sequence novelty relative to the training corpus and structural confidence were first used to identify credible candidates. Among candidates satisfying these criteria, predicted binding probability was used as the primary ranking criterion, whereas similarity to natural TCR sequences served as a plausibility check rather than a quantity to be maximized. For the highest-ranked candidates, structural descriptors such as SASA and Δ SASA, together with sequence-level metrics such as NSR, were used as complementary secondary criteria to assess interface-related structural properties and sequence quality. This prioritization workflow was intended to nominate candidates for subsequent experimental validation and was distinct from the decoding rank used to construct the analysis-specific sequence sets.

  For structure-oriented analysis, the filtered valid outputs directly supported subsequent TCR–pMHC structural prediction and representative structure comparison. For latent-space analysis, the expanded sequence pool was first mapped onto the UMAP space constructed from antigen–TCR pair representations in the training set. Representative sequences were then selected from distinct clustering regions and used for downstream evaluation of structural confidence. The same front-end inference procedure and basic post-processing rules were applied across downstream tasks, whereas candidate prioritization, structural prediction, and latent-space analysis were performed according to the requirements of each downstream analysis.


#### Evaluation overview
  The comprehensive model evaluation proceeded across the sequence, structure, and representation levels. Sequence-level analyses assessed naturalness, reference-sequence fidelity, training-set novelty, and conditional separation of the generated outputs. Similarity to natural or reference sequences was used to characterize sequence fidelity and biological plausibility, whereas novelty was evaluated independently by comparing each generated CDR3β  sequence with its nearest neighbor in the training set. Specifically, novelty at a sequence-identity threshold τ  was defined as the proportion of generated sequences whose maximum identity to any training-set CDR3β  sequence was below τ , with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\tau \in \{0.40, 0.50, 0.60, 0.70\}$\end{document}. The corresponding novelty rates were 0.622, 0.713, 0.805, and 0.884, respectively. These results indicate that a substantial proportion of the generated sequences remained distinct from the training data across multiple sequence-identity thresholds, providing evidence against simple reproduction of training examples and supporting the sequence-level generalization ability of CRAFT. Structure-level evaluations examined predicted structural plausibility, interface-related features, and the behavior of representative complexes. Representation-level analyses included both latent-space projections and attention-pattern inspections. Detailed definitions of all evaluation metrics are provided in Supplementary Note 5.
