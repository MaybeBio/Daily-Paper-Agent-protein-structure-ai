# Distal amino-acid substitutions contribute to HIV protease inhibitor resistance as directly as proximal amino-acid substitutions


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-64620-7.


## Introduction
  As of July 2025, roughly forty million people are living with HIV infection worldwide, and nearly thirty-one million are on anti-retroviral therapy (ART) (https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hiv-aids and https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hiv-drug-resistance). Most of ART targets HIV viral proteins such as reverse transcriptase (RT), protease (PR), and integrase (IN) and prevent viral replication. New anti-HIV targets and interventions continue to be explored continually1,2. Current research focuses on not only improving the safety and resistance profile of newer anti-HIV inhibitors, but also in targeting viral reservoirs and in developing antivirals that are effective at longer intervals of dosing3.

  Darunavir (DRV), an HIV-protease inhibitor (PI), has exceedingly high potency against wild-type and drug-resistant HIV4,5 (Fig. 1). DRV was approved by the US-FDA for clinical use in 2007, first for use by patients failing other anti-HIV therapies because of drug resistance, and later in drug-naïve patients6. DRV is also used as part of combination therapy regimens with RT and IN inhibitors. DRV has a high genetic barrier to HIV-1’s development of resistance. HIV carrying single mutations such as L10F, L24I, V32I, L33F, M46I, I54M, V82A, I84V, with two mutations such as V32I and I84V, or V32I and I54M, and with three mutations as in V32I, L33F, and I84V is still sensitive to DRV7–9. Though DRV has a high genetic barrier to HIV-1’s development of resistance, multiple mutations in the protease, especially in treatment-experienced patients, can lead to HIV developing significant resistance10. Thus, attempts have been made to identify new protease inhibitors with improved potency, resistance, and safety profile. GRL142 is one such PI that contained a 6–5-5 ring-fused crown-like tetrahydropyranofuran (Crn-THF) as the P2-ligand, a cyclopropyl-amino-benzothiazole (Cp-Abt) as the P2’-ligand, and a bis-fluoro-benzyl group as the P1-ligand8 (Fig. 1). GRL142 had subnanomolar activity against HIVwt and many drug-resistant HIV variants8. Compared to many other PIs, GRL142 had a higher genetic barrier against the development of resistance by HIV-1, had excellent cytotoxicity profile and high selectivity index8. Drug resistant mutations that compromise the activity of GRL142 under a clinical setting have not been reported so far to the best of our knowledge.   A drug-resistant (DR) amino-acid (AA) substitution is typically categorized as a major substitution if it arises early during a treatment and severely compromises the efficacy of the drug. The minor substitutions tend to accrue after major substitutions10. Minor substitutions may add incremental resistance and/or improve the replication fitness of the virus. For the nine protease inhibitors approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration for clinical use, HIV-1 developed resistance by a combination of major and minor mutations in the protease10. Mutations V82A and I84V caused resistance against atazanavir (ATZ), lopinavir (LPV) and indinavir (IDV); mutation of Leu90 to a methionine developed resistance against nelfinavir (NFV) and saquinavir (SQV)10. V82A and I84V are classified as major resistant mutations for ATZ, LPV and IDV10. L90M is classified as a major mutation for NFV and SQV10. Though L10F was reported as a minor drug-resistant mutation for ATZ, LPV, and NFV, it has not been reported as a resistant mutation for patients receiving DRV10. T74P was reported as a major resistant mutation for tipranavir (TPV), but as a minor mutation for DRV, but not reported as a resistant mutation for other PIs10. L89V was reported as a minor mutation for both DRV and TPV but not reported for other PIs10.

  Almost all the major AA substitutions for HIV-1 protease are active site residues that have a change in their polar and/or non-polar binding interactions with the inhibitor. Mostly, the amino acid residues classified as minor substitutions are distal from the active site. Hence, they do not have direct polar or non-polar interactions with the inhibitor and how they structurally impact binding and cause resistance is not clear. At least one study suggested that both major as well as the so called minor substitutions contributed to resistance, and in some cases the minor substitutions, as a group, may contribute more to resistance than major substitutions11. Over the years, there is an increasing realization that the current classification of major and minor substitutions is not clear cut and might be oversimplified10,11.

  HIV-1 was not able to select resistant variants against DRV when resistant selection experiments were carried out using either HIVwt or with single HIV-1 isolates12. However, resistance selection experiments carried out with a mixture of eight HIV-1 variants that had multiple amino acid mutations that made them resistant to other PIs, but not initially to DRV, ultimately resulted in a variant that was extremely resistant to DRV after 51 weeks12. This HIV-1 variant was called HIVDRVRp51. The protease region of HIVDRVRp51, henceforth referred as PRp51, had fourteen amino acid substitutions. These substitutions were Leu10Ile, Ile15Val, Lys20Arg, Leu24Ile, Val32Ile, Leu33Phe, Met36Ile, Met46Leu, Ile54Met, Leu63Pro, Lys70Gln, Val82Ile, Ile84Val and Leu89Met. Of these substitutions, three (Val32Ile, Val82Ile and Ile84Val) were proximal to the DRV binding site, the other eleven may be considered distal from the DRV binding site.

  In the current study, we used enzyme inhibition assays, antiviral assays, x-ray crystallography and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations for 1500 nano seconds to examine the structural basis of the resistance of HIVDRVRp51 and PRp51 against DRV and GRL142. Our data strongly suggested that the combination of major and minor substitutions resulted in larger structural perturbations and larger expansion of the active site volume of DRV-PRp51 over DRV-PRwt compared to the structural perturbations and active site volume expansion of GRL142-PRp51 over GRL142-PRwt. The data also helped explain the different resistance profiles of PRp51 against DRV and GRL142.


## Results
  PRp51 and HIVp51 are highly resistant to darunavir. HIVDRVRp51 (abbreviated henceforth as HIVp51) had fourteen amino acid substitutions in its protease (PRp51)12 region. We first determined the inhibition of PRp51 by DRV and by GRL142. As shown in Table 1, the Ki values of DRV and GRL142 against PRp51 were 18.7 nM and 0.604 nM, respectively. The activities of DRV and GRL142 against the wild-type protease enzyme (PRwt) had been determined previously (Table 1)13. Comparison of the Ki values indicated that DRV and GRL142 had 1,432- and 43-fold reduction in activity against PRp51 over PRwt. We also determined the antiviral activities of these inhibitors against the wild-type HIV (HIVwt) and HIVp51. Compared to its antiviral EC50 of 3.2 nM against HIVwt, DRV had an EC50 value of 1,130 nM against HIVp51, a significant reduction in potency by 353-fold. The antiviral EC50 values of GRL142 against HIVwt and HIVp51 were 0.016 nM and 1.4 nM, respectively, indicating a reduction in GRL142’s potency was only 88-fold. In summary, DRV had significant reductions in enzymatic and antiviral activities against PRp51 and HIVp51, respectively.   The molecular surfaces of residues undergoing substitution from DRV-PRwt to DRV-PRp51 form clusters of interactions. Both in in vitro and in clinical settings, DRV is known to have a high genetic barrier against HIV-1’s development of resistance9,12,15. In an attempt to generate HIV-1’s resistance against DRV, we had carried out resistance selection experiments using a mixture of 8 clinical HIV-1 variants that already had 9 to 14 PI-resistance-associated amino acid substitutions in protease12. Viral passage of this mixture of HIV-1 variants over 51 passages (one passage is seven to ten days) produced new variants of HIV-1 with significant resistance against DRV. HIVp51 had fourteen amino acid substitutions in the protease region (PRp51)12. Out of these fourteen amino acids, only two residues Val82 and Ile84 had tight van der Waals (vdW) surface interactions, and Val32 had moderate vdW interactions with DRV as observed in the crystal structure of the DRV-PRwt complex14,16. The other eleven residues that underwent substitutions did not have a direct binding interaction with DRV. We generated the molecular surfaces of all fourteen residues that underwent substitution. It was intriguing that several of these residues spanning different structural domains of the protease dimer had close molecular surface interactions with one another (Fig. 3). For example, residues Ile15, Lys20, Leu33 and Met36 in DRV-PRwt formed a cluster of vdW interactions (Fig. 3a, supplementary Fig. 4). Though Val15, Arg20, Phe33 and Ile36 in DRV-PRp51 also formed a cluster of residue surface interactions (Fig. 3b), there were differences in the surface contact areas. While Leu63 and Lys70 of DRV-PRwt had close surface interactions, the surfaces of substituted Pro63 and Gln70 in DRV-PRp51 did not interact (Fig. 3a and b). There was a noticeable difference in the interactions between Val82 and Leu10 of DRV-PRwt (which did not interact), compared to the much closer proximity of Ile82 to Ile10 in the DRV-PRp51 (Fig. 3a and b, supplementary Fig. 4). One could infer that structural rearrangements in the crystal structure of DRV-PRp51 made residues Ile10 and Ile82 move closer. Other noteworthy changes in residue surface interactions between DRV-PRwt and DRV-PRp51 were observed for the V32I-L33F-V82I-I84V, I15V-L63P-K70Q, and V32I-I54M substitutions. (supplementary Fig. 4). In summary, the residues undergoing substitution not only formed several clusters of surface interactions, but also the nature of these interactions differed between DRV-PRwt and DRV-PRp51. Mostly hydrophobic residues were involved in the substitution, with isoleucines being present eight times either as a wild-type or as a mutant residue. Eleven substitutions have either an Ile or Val present, and no residue other than Ile, Leu or Val is present in six substitutions (L10I, I15V, L24I, V32I, V82I, I84V). Foulkes-Murzycki et al. and Ishima et al. also observed the predominant involvement of hydrophobic residues in drug-resistant HIV protease17,18. The core hydrophobic residues mutate to other hydrophobic residues. The hydrophobic contacts are important for protease structure and function and are called collectively “hydrophobic sliding”. Most of the significant hydrogen bond interactions are maintained within the core. There are many isoleucines in the hydrophobic core, and the methyl groups of Ile have more possible conformations. Many of the distal residues undergoing substitution are hydrophobic in nature. They identified nineteen residues, many of them Leu and Ile that form the hydrophobic core of HIV protease17,18. Mutation of these residues changes the vdW interactions with negligible energetic penalties.

## Discussion
  Most PI-resistance-associated amino-acid substitutions deemed as major substitutions are proximal to the active site of the protease and cause changes in interactions with the PI. Examples of such major substitutions that are proximal include D30N for nelfinavir, V32I for atazanavir and lopinavir, V82A for atazanavir, lopinavir, and indinavir, I84V for many PIs including lopinavir, and tipranavir10. Though rare, a few distal substitutions such as L90M (for nelfinavir and saquinavir) are categorized as a major drug-resistant substitution10.

  The role of distal mutations in HIV protease resistance is not clear but has been examined by using various experimental and computational techniques. Using a combination of enzymatic assays, crystallography, and MD simulations, Henes et al. studied the effect of distal mutations in HIV-1 protease in causing drug resistance20. They postulated that the effect of distal mutations somehow propagates throughout protease and impacts the active site.

  Prediction of binding affinity of small molecules to their protein targets is an area of active research 21–23. HIV-1 protease flaps can stay in open, semi-open, curled or closed conformation. The conformation of the flaps must have a discernable effect on the geometry of the active site. For inhibitor-bound proteases including many drug-resistance-associated proteases, the flaps remain in a closed conformation. Moreover, the root mean square deviation of the overlays of the crystal structures of many drug-resistance-associated proteases are rather small. The change in interactions with amino-acid-substitutions is small in many instances – and doesn’t satisfactorily explain the observed changes in activity. Afterall, for a given protease-inhibitor combination, the change in geometry, volume and binding interactions with substitution(s) are significant contributors to the changes in binding affinity.

  We studied the mechanism of resistance of HIVDRVRp51 (HIVp51) against DRV and GRL142. HIVp51 has three proximal and eleven distal substitutions in the protease (PRp51). X-ray crystal structures of DRV with PRwt and PRp51 showed that the amino-acids undergoing substitution form clusters of vdW interactions and the surface contacts between residues are different between PRwt and PRp51 complexes (Fig. 3). With MD simulations, we were able to demonstrate that the distance between residues in the protease dimer that defines the active site is longer than the equilibrium crystal structure distance in a larger population of structures. We showed that the so-called minor amino-acid substitutions, along with the major amino-acid substitutions increase the inter-residue distances defining the protease binding cavity and contribute to an increase in the active site volume. This must be contributing to the loss of binding affinity of DRV with PRp51 and the resistance of HIVDRVRp51 against DRV. Our MD simulation shows that the non-active site and active site substitutions work in concert to carry out significant structural changes in DRV-PRp51 over DRV-PRwt complex. In comparison, the structural changes are less between GRL142-PRwt and GRL142-PRp51 complexes.

  Certain protease inhibitors such as DRV possess structural characteristics that make resistance relatively difficult to develop, and multiple protease mutations are generally required before clinically meaningful loss of susceptibility occurs. In the real-world setting, it is critical to delay the development of resistance-associated amino acid substitutions by reducing the generation of viral diversity itself, rather than simply suppressing viral replication. The insight gained from the analysis of our MD simulation can potentially be useful in deciding if switching to a DRV-containing therapy regimen is suitable for HIV-infected patients undergoing treatment with other clinically approved interventions. MD simulations of protease with drug-resistance-associated mutations can be carried out to determine if a particular set of mutations results in expansion of the active site and may cause resistance against DRV. In the present study, our MD simulations were able to differentiate the difference in resistance profile between DRV and GRL142. Thus, MD can potentially be used to predict the activity of newer inhibitors against drug resistant variants.

  The distal substitutions are traditionally called minor substitutions and are deemed not to influence inhibitor binding. Our work demonstrates that the distal substitutions play an important part in the expansion of the active site, and presents a simple and intuitive explanation. The expansion of the active site resulted in loss of van der Waals interactions of DRV with active site residues. The results suggest that the minor substitutions have a more direct impact on the loss of binding of DRV and in causing HIV’s drug resistance against DRV. The study suggests reexamination of the structural and functional role of distal/minor substitutions seen in drug-resistance-associated HIV protease. It might be worthwhile exploring this in other antiviral drug targets of HIV, and in the resistance mechanism in other therapeutic areas.


## Materials and methods
  Cells, viruses, and antiviral agents. MT-4 cells (RRID: CVCL_2632), authenticated using the STR profiling (ATCC, Manassas, VA), were grown in RPMI1640 culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Gemini Bio-Products) plus antibiotic–antimycotic (Anti-Anti; Gibco). HIVDRVRp51, a highly protease inhibitors (PIs)-/nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs)-resistant laboratory generated HIV-1 variant, had previously been obtained in vitro12. GRL142 was designed and synthesized as previously described24. Darunavir (DRV) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

  Drug susceptibility assay. The susceptibilities of laboratory strains of a wild-type HIV-1NL4-3 (HIVwt), and a highly PIs/NRTIs-resistant HIV-1 variant (HIVDRVRp51) to DRV and GRL142 were determined as previously described8,24 with minor modifications. For p24 assay, MT-4 cells (5 × 105/ml) were exposed to fifty 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) of each HIV-1 preparation in the absence or presence of various concentrations of each agent and were cultured at 37 °C. On day 7 of the culture, the supernatants were harvested and the amounts of p24 Gag protein were determined by using a fully automated chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay system (Lumipulse G1200; Fujirebio). The drug concentrations that suppressed the production of p24 Gag protein by 50% (50% effective concentrations; EC50) were determined by comparing the average levels of p24 production in drug-free control cell cultures. All assays were performed in triplicate.

  Protein expression and purification. PRDRVRp51 was purified as previously reported8. Briefly, Rosetta (DE3) pLysS strain (Novagen) was transformed with PRDRVRp51 expression vector25. The culture was grown overnight with Luria broth plus kanamycin and chloramphenicol (LBKm+/Cp+) at 37 ºC. The overnight culture was added to ZYM-10052 (1.0% N-Z amine, 0.5% yeast extract, 25 mM disodium hydrogen phosphate, 25 mM potassium dihydrogen phosphate, 50 mM ammonium chloride, 5 mM sodium sulfate, 1.0% glycerol, 0.05% glucose, 0.2% α-lactose, 2 mM magnesium sulphate) plus kanamycin and chloramphenicol (ZYM-10052 km+/Cp+). One litter of ZYM-10052 km+/Cp+ was used for PRDRVRp51. The ZYM-10052 km+/Cp+ culture was further continued at over 150 rpm at 37 ºC for 20 ~ 22 h26. Then the culture was spun down and pellets were resuspended in buffer A (20 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM DTT) and lysed with sonication. The cell lysates were centrifuged, and an inclusion body fraction was collected. The inclusion body fraction was washed five times with buffer A containing 2 M urea and then with buffer A without urea. The twice-washed pellet was solubilized and PRDRVRp51 was unfolded with 100 mM formic acid (pH 2.8)27. The unfolded PRDRVRp51 was purified using the fast protein liquid chromatography system (ÄKTA pure 25; GE Healthcare) and separated using the reverse phase chromatography column (RESOURS RPC 1 mL; GE Healthcare) using the gradient of buffer B (1.0% formic acid, 2.0% acetonitrile) and buffer C (1% formic acid, 70% acetonitrile). The collected fractions containing PRDRVRp51 was subjected to desalting (HiTrap Desalting; GE Healthcare) and the eluted solution was equilibrated using 100 mM formic acid and stored at − 80 °C until use.

  Enzyme inhibition assay. The inhibitory activities of DRV and GRL142 were determined as previously described, with modifications28,29. The change of the fluorescence intensity associated with the protease -elicited cleavage of the fluorogenic substrate Arg-Glu(EDANS)-Ser-Gln-Asn-Tyr-Pro-Ile-Val-Gln-Lys(DABCYL)-Arg (HIV protease substrate 1; Sigma-Aldrich) was measured. All preparation procedures were done on ice, and the enzymatic reaction was processed at 37 °C. Enzymatic assays were performed in 0.1 M sodium acetate, 1.0 M sodium chloride, 1.0 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1.0 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and 1 mg/ml bovine serum albumin (BSA) pH 4.7 buffer containing various concentrations of a test compound with 30 µM substrate and 25 nM of protease. Fluorescence was measured using a Cytation 5 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT) with an excitation wavelength of 340 nm and emission wavelength of 490 nm, and the relative fluorescent units (RFU) were obtained. Maximum velocity (Vmax) was calculated on Gen5 (BioTek), and the potency of HIV-1 protease activity inhibition by each compound was determined based on its reduced effectiveness on Vmax. Enzymatic assays for determination of the catalytic rate constant (kcat) and the Michaelis constant (Km) were also conducted under the same conditions. To determine the number of fluorophores out of RFU values, a standard curve was generated with tenfold serial dilutions of EDANS (AnaSpec, Fremont, CA), and the RFU values for each sample were converted to the numbers of EDANS molecules. IC50 values and Morrison Ki and Km values were calculated on Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA).


## Crystallization of Darunavir-PRp51 and GRL142-PRp51 complexes
  The unfolded PRDRVRp51 was refolded with the addition of a neutralizing buffer A (100 mM ammonium acetate pH 6.0, 0.005% Tween-20), making the final pH 5.0 to 5.2. The PRDRVRp51-containing solution was run through Amicon Ultra-15 10 K centrifugal filter units (Millipore), giving a solution containing PR (2 ~ 5 mg/ml) in 10 mM ammonium acetate pH 5.0 and 0.005% Tween-20. Occasionally, twice greater concentrations of DRV and GRL142 were used for crystallization. After centrifugation, the supernatants were collected and subjected to crystallization using the hanging-drop vapor diffusion method. Nextal Tubes ProComplex Suite (QIAGEN) was used for the first screening to determine the optimum crystallization condition. PRDRVRp51-DRV complexes were formed in 0.15 M (NH4)2SO4, 0.1 M HEPES pH 7.0, 20% (w/v) PEG 4000. PRDRVRp51-GRL142 complexes were formed in 0.15 M (NH4)2SO4, 0.1 M HEPES pH 6.8, 15% (w/v) PEG 4000. Crystals of PRDRVRp51 complexed with DRV or GRL142 were retrieved, immersed into cryoprotective solution containing reservoir solution plus 30% glycerol, and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

  X-ray diffraction data collection. The X-ray diffraction data of the PRDRVRp51 -DRV crystal were collected under cryogenic temperatures at SPring-8 BL44XU using X-rays of 0.9 Å wavelength and a CCD detector MX225-HE (Rayonix). A total of 180 diffraction images were collected using a rotation step of 1.0 degree with a camera distance of 161 mm. The diffraction data of the PRDRVRp51—GRL142 crystal were collected under cryogenic temperatures at SPring-8 BL41XU using X-rays of 1.0 Å and a pixel array detector PILATUS3 6 M detector (DECTRIS). A total of 360 images were collected using a rotation step of 1.0 degree with a camera distance of 210 mm.

  Structure solutions and refinement. Solvent content and Matthew’s coefficient values were determined using CCP4 suite of programs30,31. GRL142 was fit using ARP/wARP – Ligands through CCP432,33. The initial coordinates for GRL142 were taken from PDB ID 5TYS. Solvent molecules were fit using ARP/wARP-solvent through CCP4. The models were then refined using the simulated annealing protocol in phenix.refine through Phenix suite of programs34. Refinement libraries for GRL142 were optimized using the eLBOW-AM1 method and were manually checked through REEL as described previously8,35. For the darunavir–PRp51 complex, the structure 6OGL was used as the search model for molecular replacement using Molrep36. Darunavir (PDB ligand code: 017) was manually fitted into the structure. The model was refined using Refmac within the CCP4i2 interface. The 9CTX structure (PRp51 + darunavir) contains one molecule per asymmetric unit, whereas the PRp51 + GRL142 complex has two molecules in the asymmetric unit. MolProbity analysis showed no Ramachandran and rotamer outliers, with 98% of residues in favored regions for 9CTX; similarly, 6MKL exhibited no Ramachandran and rotamer outliers, with 99% of residues in favored regions. A summary of structure solution refinement statistics is given in supplementary Table 1.

  Protein structure accession IDs. The final refined coordinates of PRp51-DRV and PRp51-GRL142 along with their corresponding X-ray data were deposited in RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) with accession IDs 9CTX and 6MKL, respectively.

  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. The four crystal structures with PDB IDs 9CTX (PRp51-DRV), 6MKL (PRp51-GRL142), 4HLA (PRwt-DRV), and 5TYS (PRwt-GRL142) were used for determining the polar, nonpolar interactions and in carrying out MD simulations. Using the ‘protein preparation’ tool in Maestro, hydrogens were added, protonation states were assigned, and restrained minimization was carried out. OPLS3 force field was utilized in the simulations. A cutoff distance of 3.0 Å between a polar hydrogen and an oxygen or nitrogen atom, a minimum donor angle of 60° between D-H-A, and a minimum acceptor angle of 90° between H-A-B were used to define the presence of hydrogen bonds (“D,” “A,” and “B” stand for “donor,” “acceptor,” and “atom connected to acceptor,” respectively). Molecular surfaces for the inhibitors and selected PR residues from the active site were generated using a water sphere with a radius of 1.4 Å as a probe. Software tools from Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, as implemented in the Maestro version 10.7.015 (Schrödinger, LLC, New York), were used for model building, visualization, analysis and in generating the structural figures.

  MD simulation was carried out on fully minimized and equilibrated structures using DESMOND37 as implemented within Maestro and briefly described as follows. For PRp51-DRV, the molecular system for carrying out molecular dynamics was built using the ‘System Builder’ option using the SPC solvent model (water as solvent) in an orthorhombic box, neutralized by adding three chloride ions, and adding 0.15 M NaCl. Similar protocols were utilized for preparing the PRwt-DRV, PRwt-GRL142, and PRp51-GRL142 complexes. The box volumes were minimized, and the systems were equilibrated. MD simulations were carried out for 1500 nano seconds, with timesteps of one femtosecond, and a electrostatic cutoff at 9 Å. The simulation trajectories were saved. The trajectories were analyzed at intervals of 0.5 ns to determine structural changes and fluctuations. The simulations were carried out using NVIDIA GPUs on the NIH Biowulf cluster.

  The binding site volumes were calculated using the python script ‘trajectory_binding_site_volumes.py’ from Schrodinger, LLC. The script uses the SiteMap module to compute the binding site volume. Volumes for the PRwt and PRp51 complexes from the crystal structures were computed. From the MD simulation trajectories, active site volumes at 0.5 ns intervals were computed.
