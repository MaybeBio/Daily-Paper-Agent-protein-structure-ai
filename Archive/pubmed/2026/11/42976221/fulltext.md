# Folding without glutamine: Proteome-scale removal of a canonical amino acid


## Abstract
  All known life uses a canonical alphabet of 20 amino acids, yet which residues are structurally essential remains untested at proteome scale. Isoleucine was recently eliminated from ribosomal proteins in Escherichia coli, establishing that amino acid alphabet reduction is feasible. Here we ask whether an entire amino acid can be eliminated from a proteome, and develop a computational framework to identify the best target. Using two independent approaches — ProteinMPNN structure-based probabilities and multiple sequence alignment conservation — we define “keep-score” as the fraction of positions where the native residue is optimal. Both methods converge on an unexpected candidate: glutamine has the lowest keep-score, with proline and glycine the highest — counter to substitution-matrix intuition. Glutamine’s low keep-score is not explained by a single obvious substitute; glutamate is preferred in only 14% of replacements. Removing this target proteome-wide, we replaced every glutamine across 4,148 proteins with ProteinMPNN-optimal alternatives and modeled the resulting sequences with AlphaFold2. The recoded proteome maintained native-like structures: 85.5% of models exceeded a TMscore of 0.8, indistinguishable from the 85.9% baseline for rebuilt native sequences. Orthogonal validation — ESMFold predictions, Rosetta energy calculations, and conformational ensemble comparisons — revealed no evidence of systematic misfolding attributable to glutamine removal. As a consistency check, a glutamine-free TEM-1 β-lactamase designed with ProteinMPNN conferred ampicillin resistance in E. coli, indicating fold preservation can extend to biological function. Evolutionary evidence further supports these findings: 24% of E. coli proteins have glutamine-free orthologs in prokaryotic genomes, and glutamine ranks lowest in keep-score in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. These results show that an entire amino acid can be removed from a proteome with folds broadly preserved, establishing keep-score as a general framework and glutamine as the primary candidate for recoding. Crucially, this reflects a computational analysis of structural replaceability, not a demonstration of biological dispensability.


## Introduction
  All known life encodes proteins using the same 20 standard amino acids, a universality that has persisted across billions of years of evolution [1]. Whether this conservation reflects strict biochemical necessity or evolutionary contingency has remained an open question [2]. Some organisms approach extreme amino acid depletion — AT-rich insect endosymbionts such as Carsonella exhibit severe compositional biases driven by genomic GC content [3,4] — but no free-living organism had been shown to completely eliminate any canonical amino acid from its proteome. Recently, Liu et al. [5] eliminated all isoleucine residues from the ribosomal proteins of Escherichia coli using generative AI-guided design, a first step toward a 19-amino-acid organism. Their Ec19 strain, carrying 21 Ile-free ribosomal subunits at a native genomic locus, remained viable and genomically stable for over 450 generations, establishing that even an essential, universally conserved machine can be rebuilt without one canonical amino acid. The feasibility of drastic alphabet reduction had earlier been demonstrated at the single-protein level: Shibue et al. [6] systematically eliminated amino acids from an ancestral nucleoside kinase and obtained soluble, stable, catalytically active variants composed of only 13 amino acid types, most of which coincide with prebiotically plausible residues. Comparable reductions have been achieved in other systems: Yagi et al. [7] reconstructed the core fold of RNA polymerase from only seven amino acid types, and Makarov et al. [8] engineered a catalytically active dephospho-CoA kinase devoid of Phe, Tyr, and Trp; earlier, Longo et al. [9] designed a stable, foldable protein from a prebiotically plausible alphabet. Together, these results transform the question from whether amino acid reduction is possible to which amino acid is most dispensable and why. Answering this question has fundamental implications for understanding protein architecture and practical consequences for engineering organisms with deliberately reduced genetic codes, incorporating non-canonical amino acids, or implementing biocontainment strategies [2,10,11].

  Liu et al. [5] selected isoleucine on the basis of evolutionary conservation: across multiple sequence alignments of E. coli proteins, Ile is among the least conserved residues and is frequently substituted by the biochemically similar valine. This choice was reinforced by practical considerations — branched-chain amino acid redundancy, the higher biosynthetic cost of Ile relative to Val, and the availability of a clean auxotrophic knockout. Yet even with this favorable target, simple Ile-to-Val substitution succeeded in only 43% of essential proteins tested; the remaining cases required multiple generative models, compensatory mutations, and iterative experimental cycles involving hundreds of design variants. This difficulty raises a question their work did not address: is isoleucine actually the easiest amino acid to remove from the proteome, or would a different choice have been more tractable?

  Classical intuition about amino acid replaceability derives from substitution matrices such as PAM and BLOSUM, which capture evolutionary exchange patterns across protein families [12,13]. These matrices suggest that hydrophobic residues — particularly isoleucine, which frequently exchanges with leucine and valine — would be among the easiest to eliminate [14,15]. Similarly, studies on reduced amino acid alphabets have sought to identify groups of residues that can be merged without losing structural information. Murphy et al. [16] developed a hierarchy of simplified alphabets optimized for fold recognition, and Takabatake et al. [17] extended this approach for large-scale homology searches. In both cases, the first merger is Ile with Val — consistent with their well-known biochemical interchangeability — and the second is glutamine with glutamate, suggesting that these two residues carry partially redundant structural information. An information-theoretic analysis of long-range contact interactions reached a compatible conclusion: Solis [18] found that reduced alphabets of fewer than 10 amino acids preserve virtually all the structural information encoded in native contacts, and that optimal clusters derived from contact potentials converge with those derived from physicochemical intuition and sequence-alignment-based approaches. However, substitution matrices and reduced alphabets reflect the composite pressures of evolution — functional constraints, expression-linked selection, and lineage-specific biases — rather than purely structural considerations [19–21]. What evolution has tolerated in pairwise exchanges may differ fundamentally from what protein architecture actually requires. Testing this distinction demands a different approach: systematically evaluating each amino acid’s contribution to fold stability across an entire proteome and asking which one is least required.

  Recent advances in artificial intelligence make such an assessment computationally tractable. AlphaFold2 predicts protein structures with near-experimental accuracy [22,23], while ProteinMPNN performs inverse folding — designing sequences likely to adopt a target three-dimensional structure [24]. Crucially, ProteinMPNN evaluates each position independently, assigning probabilities to all 20 amino acids based on local and global structural context. This means that when evaluating the removal of an amino acid, each occurrence can be replaced with the optimal alternative at that specific position, rather than a single uniform substitution as in simple recoding schemes. That inverse-folding models can navigate severely restricted sequence spaces has been confirmed experimentally: Giacobelli et al. [25] used ProteinMPNN and RFdiffusion to design de novo protein folds from a 10-amino-acid alphabet lacking all basic and aromatic residues, obtaining structures that adopted their intended topologies and resisted thermal and chemical denaturation. Combined with AlphaFold2’s ability for rapid structural validation, these tools enable proteome-scale redesign experiments that were previously impossible [26,27]. Importantly, structure prediction models have also been shown to interpret reduced-alphabet sequences: Ieremie et al. [28] demonstrated that ESMFold can predict native-like structures from protein sequences translated into reduced alphabets of 12 or fewer amino acid types, supporting the validity of using deep-learning structure predictors to evaluate recoded sequences. Both ProteinMPNN and AlphaFold2 proved essential in the Liu et al. [5] study for generating functional Ile-free ribosomal proteins; here, we apply the same tools not to solve individual design problems but to answer the prior question of target selection.

  We asked whether an entire canonical amino acid could be eliminated from a proteome, and applied this approach to E. coli, developing quantitative metrics to identify the most promising target. We define “keep-score” as the fraction of positions where the native residue is deemed optimal — either the highest probability under ProteinMPNN or the most conserved in multiple sequence alignments — and “keep-propensity” as the margin by which the native residue is favored over its best alternative. Two independent methods converge on the same unexpected candidate: ProteinMPNN, a structure-based inverse-folding model, and MSA conservation, reflecting billions of years of evolutionary selection, both assign the lowest keep-score to glutamine — not isoleucine — with proline and glycine highest. This ranking contradicts predictions from substitution matrices and reveals a disconnect between evolutionary exchangeability and structural constraint. Glutamine’s replaceability is not explained by the availability of an obvious substitute: although reduced-alphabet analyses group it with glutamate, no single residue dominates its replacements — unlike isoleucine, which has an obvious substitute in valine yet ranks among the more constrained residues by keep-score. Replaceability, it appears, is not determined by similarity to a single alternative but reflects the degree to which a residue is structurally unconstrained — rarely imposing strict geometric requirements on the folds it occupies.

  Removing this candidate proteome-wide, we replaced every glutamine in 4,148 E. coli proteins with the optimal ProteinMPNN-predicted alternative and modeled the resulting sequences with AlphaFold2. The glutamine-free proteome maintained native-like structures indistinguishable from the rebuilt-native baseline within the resolution of the prediction method, and multiple complementary approaches — local confidence, Rosetta energetics, an orthogonal structure predictor (ESMFold), flexible alignments, and conformational-ensemble comparisons — revealed no evidence of systematic misfolding attributable to glutamine removal. Applying the same analysis to isoleucine — the residue eliminated by Liu et al. — confirmed that it is more disruptive to remove than glutamine, showing that the framework discriminates among amino acids rather than tolerating any substitution uniformly. As a consistency check, we engineered a glutamine-free TEM-1 β-lactamase that confers ampicillin resistance in E. coli, indicating that fold preservation can extend to biological function. Evolutionary evidence supports these findings: at least 24% of E. coli proteins have glutamine-free orthologs in prokaryotic genomes, and keep-scores computed independently for Saccharomyces cerevisiae rank glutamine lowest and correlate strongly with those of E. coli, indicating that the ranking reflects general features of protein architecture rather than a peculiarity of E. coli. We emphasize that this study is a computational analysis of structural replaceability, not a demonstration of biological dispensability — a protein that folds correctly without glutamine may still lack catalytic activity or binding specificity if glutamine participates directly in its mechanism [29,30]. Nevertheless, fold preservation is a necessary first step: a redesigned protein that cannot properly fold certainly will not function. That an entire amino acid can be removed from a proteome with folds broadly preserved raises a new question: why has evolution universally retained an amino acid that protein architecture, as assessed by current computational methods, does not appear to strictly require?


## Results
  We asked whether a bacterial proteome could eliminate a single canonical amino acid without compromising its ability to fold. Amino acid abundances vary widely across prokaryotes (S1 Fig); E. coli closely matches the prokaryotic median, making it a suitable model for this study. In E. coli, glutamine is the sixth least abundant amino acid—relatively rare, but not exceptionally so. To identify which residues are structurally dispensable, we asked how often each native amino acid is the optimal choice at its position. We define “keep-score” as the fraction of positions where the native residue is deemed best: either the highest probability under ProteinMPNN or the most frequent in the MSA column (Fig 1). Both criteria agree that Pro and Gly are the least removable, consistent with their unique roles in defining secondary structure. Conversely, both methods identify Gln as the most removable amino acid. Under ProteinMPNN, the keep-score for Gln is only 0.13, meaning this tool would replace 87% of all glutamines in the E. coli proteome. The convergence of two independent methods—one trained on structure, the other reflecting evolutionary selection—argues that glutamine’s dispensability is not an algorithmic artifact.

  Keep-score, however, reflects only a binary retain-or-replace call at each position: it reveals how often a residue is retained but not by how much it is favored or disfavored. To address this, we defined “keep-propensity” as the difference between the probability (or frequency) of the native amino acid and that of its best alternative, ranging from −1.0 (strong preference for replacement) to +1.0 (strong preference for retention). Under ProteinMPNN, only Ala, Gly, Leu, and Pro show distributions with prominent peaks near +1.0, reflecting their irreplaceable structural roles (S2A Fig). MSA-based distributions show retention peaks for all amino acids, but Gln has one of the smallest (S2B Fig). Thus, glutamine is not only replaced most often—it is also replaced with the least resistance.

  What would the E. coli proteome look like under optimal recoding? When we replaced every residue with the top choice from ProteinMPNN or the MSA consensus, the largest shift was for glutamine: its abundance would decrease approximately threefold under ProteinMPNN, from 3.8% to 1.2% (S3 Fig). MSA-guided recoding predicted more modest changes, with cysteine showing the largest relative shift. Examining replacement preferences reveals a counterintuitive pattern (S4 Fig). Isoleucine shows the strongest single replacement bias—over 30% of substituted isoleucines become valine—yet Ile is not among the most replaceable amino acids by keep-score, contradicting predictions from classical substitution matrices. Conversely, glutamine lacks a dominant substitute: glutamate is favored in only ~14% of cases. Thus, the availability of a physicochemically similar alternative does not determine dispensability. Glutamine’s replaceability appears to reflect a lack of strict positional constraints rather than the presence of an obvious substitute.

  The preceding analyses identify glutamine as structurally dispensable based on sequence-level metrics, but do the resulting proteins actually fold? To answer this, we used AlphaFold2 (AF2) to model mutant sequences and compared them to reference structures from AlphaFoldDB. First, we established a baseline by modeling the native E. coli sequences themselves (Fig 2A). These “rebuilt” models are necessary because AF2 does not always reproduce the same structure for flexible or disordered proteins. We found that 86% of rebuilt models have TMscore above 0.8 against their AlphaFoldDB references—a threshold we adopt throughout as indicating near-native agreement. Proteins falling below this threshold are not misfolded; their low TMscores correlate strongly with high predicted aligned error (PAE), indicating intrinsic flexibility (Fig 2B). We define each protein’s rebuilt TMscore as its “ease-score,” which quantifies how reliably AF2 reproduces that structure and allows us to control for flexibility in subsequent comparisons.

  We next asked whether keep-scores predict the structural impact of removing an amino acid. For adequate statistical power we assembled 300 proteins with very high ease-scores (the mut_each_300_msa set; Methods), and for each of the 20 amino acids in turn replaced every occurrence with the most frequent alternative in its MSA column, modeling the resulting sequences with AlphaFold2. We later use a second, independently designed set (mut_each_200; below) as a control against circularity. We quantified impact as the RMSD to the AlphaFoldDB reference, averaged over the five AF2 models per sequence; for these high-ease proteins TMscore saturates near 1.0 and cannot resolve small differences between amino acids, whereas RMSD remains sensitive. MSA-consensus replacements were used rather than ProteinMPNN-optimal ones, which minimize structural perturbation for every amino acid and thereby compress the differences we sought to measure.

  Ranking the amino acids by median removal RMSD reproduces the keep-score order: of 134 pairwise differences significant after Hochberg correction, 126 agree with the keep-score ranking and only 8 oppose it (Fig 3). Removing glycine, leucine, alanine, valine, or proline—all high-keep residues—caused the largest disruptions, consistent with their roles in backbone geometry and packing. Removing glutamine was among the least disruptive of all: it was significantly less disruptive than eleven higher-keep residues (R, S, T, F, D, I, A, V, L, G, and P) and never significantly more disruptive than any residue, though it was statistically indistinguishable from the other low-keep residues (H, M, C, K, N, W, E, and Y). Glutamine is therefore not uniquely dispensable by this measure, but it lies firmly among the least impactful residues and, having the lowest keep-score, is the best single target identifiable a priori.

  This ranking is not an artifact of using the same tool to design and to score. We repeated the analysis on an independent set of 200 proteins (the mut_each_200 set; Methods) in which each amino acid was removed by ProteinMPNN-optimal replacement rather than MSA consensus, and again ranked the residues by median removal RMSD. The two sets are designed by opposite criteria, yet in each the RMSD ranking agrees best with the other method’s keep-score: the MSA-designed mut_each_300_msa ranking correlates most strongly with the ProteinMPNN keep-score (Spearman ρ = 0.88), and the ProteinMPNN-designed mut_each_200 ranking with the MSA keep-score (ρ = 0.71; S5 Fig). Were the agreement between keep-score and removal impact merely a consequence of scoring sequences with the tool that designed them, each set would track its own design criterion rather than the other’s. That the association crosses methods indicates that keep-score captures a property of each position that is visible to structure-based design and to evolutionary conservation alike, independent of which one generated the sequence.

  Before removing glutamine specifically, we tested whether proteome-wide optimal recoding preserves structure. We rebuilt every E. coli protein using either the highest-probability amino acid at each position (ProteinMPNN-ideal) or the MSA consensus, then modeled both with AF2. Both strategies preserved native folds, with median TMscores of 0.96 (ProteinMPNN) and 0.95 (MSA) against AlphaFoldDB references (S6 Fig). Notably, ProteinMPNN achieved this at much lower sequence identity: 0.46 versus 0.66 for MSA consensus (S7 Fig). This confirms ProteinMPNN’s ability to design highly divergent sequences that remain structurally compatible with the native fold—a prerequisite for the proteome-wide glutamine removal that follows.

  We now arrive at the central experiment: can the E. coli proteome fold without glutamine? We replaced every glutamine with the highest-probability alternative under ProteinMPNN (all 4,148 proteins) or with the most frequent non-glutamine residue in the conservation MSA (the 3,960 proteins with an available MSA), then built AF2 models for both (Fig 4). The results are striking. ProteinMPNN-designed sequences without glutamine reached a mean TMscore of 0.914, virtually identical to 0.917 for the rebuilt native models; 85.5% exceeded the 0.8 threshold, compared with 85.9% for natives. Grouped by ease-score, the No-Gln models matched the rebuilt baseline across the entire range—from the most rigid to the most flexible proteins. MSA-based designs performed worse, with a mean TMscore of 0.895 and only 78% above threshold. In short, ProteinMPNN can remove every glutamine from a bacterial proteome matching the same structural accuracy of native sequences.

  We validated the structural integrity of the No-Gln proteome through three complementary approaches, each probing a different potential failure mode (Fig 5). First, we asked whether AF2’s confidence changed near substitution sites. For each replaced glutamine, we computed the mean pLDDT of residues within 7 Å in both No-Gln and Rebuilt models. The correlation is near-perfect (slope = 0.954, R2 = 0.95; Fig 5A), indicating that AF2 predicts local geometry with equal certainty before and after glutamine removal. Second, we evaluated energetic stability using Rosetta’s all-atom energy function. After constrained relaxation, No-Gln and Rebuilt energies correlated almost perfectly (slope = 0.995, R2 = 0.985), with No-Gln models showing marginal mean stabilization (−4.2 REU; Fig 5B). Third, we tested whether an entirely different folding method would reach the same conclusion. ESMFold, a protein language model that does not use explicit sequence alignments, produced TMscores for No-Gln models that were indistinguishable from Rebuilt (slope = 0.971, R2 = 0.955; Fig 5C). Across all three metrics—local confidence, energetic stability, and an orthogonal folding algorithm—the glutamine-free proteome is structurally indistinguishable from the native within the resolution of these methods.

  About 14% of No-Gln models fall below TMscore 0.8—do these represent misfolded structures? Three analyses indicate they do not (Fig 6). Alignment with FoldMason, which scores local structural similarity independently of global superposition, classified 99.2% of ProteinMPNN No-Gln models as perfect (mean LDDT = 1.0) and 99.7% as good (mean LDDT > 0.9), virtually identical to Rebuilt natives (Fig 6A). Flexible alignment—which allows domain rearrangements while preserving local structure—rescued most low-scoring proteins (Figs 6B and S8). We analyzed 548 proteins whose No-Gln models had rigid TMscore below 0.8; of these, 60 had Rebuilt TMscore above the threshold, indicating that glutamine removal lowered their scores. Under flexible alignment, 333 No-Gln models (61%) and 341 Rebuilt models (62%) crossed the 0.8 threshold, showing equivalent rescue rates regardless of starting point. Finally, we asked whether low-scoring No-Gln models were statistically distinguishable from native conformational variation. Using BioEmu, we generated 50 conformational samples from each native sequence, obtained their TMscores to the Reference models, and compared these distributions to the No-Gln TMscores (Figs 6C and S9). Of 497 proteins tested, only nine scored significantly lower than the native ensemble (P < 0.05), reduced to two after correction for multiple testing (q ≤ 0.05). Visual inspection of these two proteins (P37619 and P64439) confirmed that all three models—AlphaFoldDB reference, Rebuilt, and No-Gln—share the same fold (S10 Fig). In summary, we found no evidence that removing glutamine causes misfolding in any E. coli protein (S11 Fig).

  The preceding analyses are computational; can natural proteins fold without glutamine? To address this, we searched for glutamine-free orthologs of E. coli proteins across 6,369 complete prokaryotic proteomes from RefSeq. Orthologs were defined as reciprocal best hits with MMseqs2 (E-value < 1e-15). Of the 4,363 E. coli proteins, 103 already lack glutamine. Among the remaining 4,260, we found 948 with at least one ortholog completely lacking this amino acid. Thus, at least 24% of E. coli proteins (1,051/4,363) have natural orthologs that lack glutamine. Collectively, proteins with glutamine-free orthologs contain 16% of all glutamines in E. coli (9,405/59,955). We then asked whether glutamine-free orthologs are more or less common than expected. Across all 20 amino acids, the fraction of proteins with amino-acid-free orthologs decreases with abundance in E. coli—rare amino acids are more likely to be absent by chance (Fig 7). Three amino acids lie notably above this trend: Lys, Asn, and Gln, with more amino-acid-free orthologs than the fitted abundance relationship would place them. This places glutamine among the few residues that appear unusually dispensable in natural proteomes. Even within this limited sample of 6,369 proteomes, hundreds of E. coli proteins have glutamine-free orthologs; larger datasets will likely reveal many more.


### Keep-score discriminates: Isoleucine is harder to remove than glutamine
  A recurring concern with pipelines that both design and evaluate sequences is circularity: if ProteinMPNN and AlphaFold2 share structural priors, a glutamine-free proteome might fold well simply because the design tool avoids whatever the folding tool would penalize — in which case any amino acid, not glutamine specifically, would appear replaceable. We therefore tested whether the framework discriminates among amino acids of differing keep-score. We compared glutamine removal with removal of isoleucine, a higher-keep-score residue that Liu et al. eliminated experimentally from the E. coli ribosome [5], and included glycine — among the least replaceable residues — as a positive control that must register as disruptive. Across 571 proteins (the QIG_571_msa set; Methods), with each residue replaced by its most frequent MSA alternative and modeled with AlphaFold2, structural impact increased in the order Q < I << G (Fig 8A). All three differences were significant (paired Wilcoxon P = 1.7 × 10−11 for Q vs I, and P < 10−51 for both comparisons against G): glutamine removal was less disruptive than isoleucine removal in 65% of proteins, and glycine was substantially more disruptive than either. The pipeline is therefore not uniformly permissive; it ranks these residues in the order their keep-scores predict.

  We extended the glutamine-versus-isoleucine comparison to the whole proteome, removing each residue from all 4,148 proteins with ProteinMPNN-optimal replacements (the IQ_full set; Methods; Fig 8B). Here the two variants were nearly indistinguishable by TMscore (mean 0.9076 vs 0.9073; paired t-test P = 0.57) — an expected consequence of the design method, since ProteinMPNN selects replacements that reproduce the native fold regardless of which residue is removed, compressing the very difference we sought to measure. This is why the MSA-based comparison above, which does not optimize for structure, resolves the per-residue difference more cleanly. By the more sensitive RMSD metric, however, glutamine removal remained the less disruptive of the two across the full proteome (lower RMSD in 57.4% of proteins; median ΔRMSD = −0.024 Å; paired Wilcoxon P = 1.1 × 10−15). This advantage tracked structural reliability: binning proteins by ease-score, the fraction in which glutamine removal was less disruptive rose from ~0.53 in the lowest-ease decile to ~0.69 in the highest. Where AlphaFold2 determines structure most reliably, and prediction noise is smallest, the systematic advantage of removing glutamine over isoleucine emerges most clearly — as expected if the difference is real but subtle. Together these results show that the framework does more than rank glutamine lowest: it correctly orders residues of differing replaceability, confirming that the near-native folding of the glutamine-free proteome reflects a genuine property of glutamine rather than an artifact of the design-and-evaluate pipeline.

  Having shown that keep-score predicts structural impact within the E. coli proteome, we asked whether the ranking itself generalizes beyond it. We computed ProteinMPNN keep-scores for Saccharomyces cerevisiae, a eukaryote separated from E. coli by over a billion years of evolution (Fig 9). The keep-scores of all 20 amino acids correlate strongly between the two organisms, and in both cases glutamine has the lowest keep-score by a wide margin. This consistency across more than a billion years of evolutionary divergence indicates that, from ProteinMPNN’s perspective, keep-score reflects intrinsic properties of protein structure rather than the composition of any single proteome.


### A glutamine-free β-lactamase is enzymatically active
  The preceding analyses identify glutamine as the residue with the lowest keep-score and, by both structural and evolutionary criteria, among the most replaceable. As an independent consistency check that a glutamine-free protein can fold and function in vivo, we selected TEM-1 β-lactamase, a well-characterized periplasmic enzyme whose activity requires correct folding, Sec-dependent export, signal-peptide cleavage, and precise assembly of the catalytic triad (Ser70, Lys73, Glu166) and the oxyanion hole — none of which involves a glutamine. The mature enzyme contains eight glutamines; we replaced all eight with ProteinMPNN-optimal alternatives, retaining the single glutamine of the signal peptide, which is cleaved from the mature protein, to produce the variant TEM1-Q. Modeled without the leader peptide, TEM1-Q was predicted to adopt a structure essentially identical to native TEM-1 (TMscore 0.999, RMSD 0.16 Å) with the catalytic triad superposed, and had a lower Rosetta score than native TEM-1 after relaxation (−878.4 versus −865.4 REU). We therefore tested directly whether this predicted glutamine-free enzyme folds and functions.

  Both constructs — the standard pQE30 vector, whose ampicillin-resistance marker is native TEM-1, and a derivative in which that resident coding sequence was replaced by TEM1-Q — were transformed into E. coli BL21 and conferred similar ampicillin resistance: each grew at up to 400 µg/mL ampicillin, and neither grew at 800 µg/mL. Because the two plasmids are identical except for the glutamine codons of a single gene, the comparison is exact. To compare activity at matched extract loading, we resolved soluble-fraction cell extracts and detected β-lactamase in situ with the chromogenic substrate nitrocefin (S12 Fig). Native TEM-1 and TEM1-Q produced activity bands of comparable intensity, and halving the load halved the signal for both, while the untransformed host showed none. At the expected position, neither enzyme produced a band above the cellular-protein background in the total-protein stain, so activity — not an abundant band — is the readout. A glutamine-free β-lactamase therefore folds into a catalytically competent enzyme, conferring native-like ampicillin resistance and comparable in-gel activity at matched extract loading.


## Discussion
  The central finding of this study is that an entire canonical amino acid can be removed from a bacterial proteome without large structural consequences for essentially any of its proteins, and that both keep-scores singled out glutamine, a priori, as the best target for removal. Within the resolution of current computational methods, the Escherichia coli proteome achieves its native three-dimensional folds without it. When every glutamine was replaced with the highest-probability alternative under ProteinMPNN, the resulting sequences produced AlphaFold2 models that, as a population, matched the rebuilt-native baseline: mean TMscore of 0.914 versus 0.917 for rebuilt natives, with 85.5% exceeding the 0.8 threshold compared with 85.9% for the baseline (Fig 4). This result acquires particular significance in light of Liu et al. [5], who experimentally eliminated isoleucine from the E. coli ribosome as a first step toward a 19-amino-acid organism. Their work establishes that amino acid alphabet reduction is biologically feasible; ours addresses the prior question of which amino acid to target and provides a systematic framework for making that choice. The convergence of these two independent lines of work — one experimental, one computational — strengthens the conclusion that the 20-amino-acid alphabet is not a hard constraint of cellular life.

  The identification of glutamine as the best a priori target for removal emerges from the agreement of two independent methods. ProteinMPNN, trained to predict sequences compatible with a given backbone structure, and MSA-derived conservation, reflecting billions of years of evolutionary selection, both assign glutamine the lowest keep-score among the 20 canonical amino acids (Fig 1). We do not claim that this definitively establishes glutamine as the “most dispensable” amino acid in a biological sense — dispensability encompasses function, regulation, and metabolic integration beyond structural integrity.

  Rather, we report two distinct but consistent findings: glutamine has the lowest keep-score of any amino acid under both methods, and its removal is among the least structurally perturbing across the proteome — significantly less disruptive than removal of eleven higher-keep-score residues, yet indistinguishable from the small group of other low-keep-score residues (Fig 3). Both methods also agree that proline and glycine are the least removable, consistent with their unique roles in defining backbone geometry. This convergence argues against the possibility that glutamine’s replaceability is an artifact of any single algorithm. When two orthogonal approaches — one optimizing for structural compatibility, the other summarizing evolutionary constraint — reach the same conclusion, the signal likely reflects genuine biophysical properties of protein architecture. The keep-score and keep-propensity metrics introduced here provide a general framework for ranking amino acid replaceability at proteome scale and should prove useful for guiding future alphabet-reduction efforts.

  A striking feature of glutamine’s replaceability is that it cannot be explained by the availability of a physicochemically similar substitute. When glutamine is replaced, glutamate — its closest chemical relative and the residue with which it is grouped in reduced-alphabet hierarchies [16,17] — is favored in only approximately 14% of cases (S4 Fig). This contrasts sharply with isoleucine, where over 30% of replacements favor valine, yet isoleucine is not among the most removable amino acids by keep-score. Classical substitution matrices, which quantify pairwise similarity based on evolutionary exchange rates, would predict that residues with obvious substitutes are most replaceable. Our data contradict this intuition: replaceability appears to be an emergent property of context-dependent structural constraints rather than a consequence of chemical similarity to a single alternative. This lack of a dominant substitute is itself informative: it points to the absence of a unique structural requirement rather than to any single obvious replacement. Glutamine’s surface amide contributes to solvation, helix propensity, or polar turn-bridging depending on position, and each of these functions can be satisfied by any of several residues — so different positions recruit different substitutes and none predominates. In contrast, glycine and proline show prominent retention peaks in their keep-propensity distributions (S2 Fig), reflecting their irreplaceable roles in backbone flexibility and rigidity, respectively.

  If that constraint is context-dependent, it should vary from position to position, and the glutamines that conservation and design retain should be those in the more constrained settings. This is what we find. Partitioning every reference glutamine by solvent accessibility, buried glutamines (relative accessibility below 0.20) are retained far more often than exposed ones: glutamine is the most conserved residue in its alignment column at 68% of buried positions versus 42% of exposed ones, and ProteinMPNN likewise retains it more often when buried (28% versus 9%; S13 Fig). By MSA conservation the margin favoring glutamine is positive at buried positions and essentially absent at exposed ones (median per-position keep-propensity +0.35 versus −0.05). ProteinMPNN’s continuous keep-propensity, by contrast, does not separate the two groups — it remains negative for both — so buried glutamines are retained more often, though not more strongly favored by every metric. Secondary-structure context shows the same directionality far more weakly: by retention rate, glutamines in helices and strands are kept somewhat more often than those in coil under both methods, though the effect is small (Cramér’s V = 0.0824) and explains little of the variation in retention. This coupling of glutamine retention to burial has an independent, pre-AI precedent. Analyzing conservation across sequence alignments, Fiser et al. [31] reported that glutamine is the least conserved of all twenty amino acids and that, across residues, conservation declines with solvent accessibility — with glutamine falling off that trend, less conserved than even its high exposure would predict. Their population-level observation, reached decades earlier and without reference to any structure-prediction model, is consistent with our per-position finding that it is the buried minority of glutamines that tend to be retained. Taken together, these observations indicate that whether a glutamine is retained is governed largely by its local structural environment — chiefly whether the position is buried — rather than by any intrinsic property of the residue in isolation. Surface exposure may also offer a common root for the agreement of our two keep-scores: an exposed glutamine tends to be both structurally unconstrained, which ProteinMPNN registers as a low retention probability, and weakly conserved across homologues, which the alignments register directly — even though structural and evolutionary exchangeability, convergent here, are distinct properties.

  A potential confound in ranking amino acids by removal impact is abundance: the residues whose removal perturbs structure most — glycine, valine, alanine, leucine — are also among the most common, and across the twenty amino acids removal impact is correlated with frequency (Spearman ρ = 0.73). This correlation is unsurprising, since residues under strong structural constraint should be both harder to remove and, over evolutionary time, more abundant; it nonetheless raises the question of whether keep-score predicts impact only because it tracks abundance. It does not. Controlling for amino-acid frequency, keep-score remains strongly associated with median removal RMSD (partial Spearman ρ = 0.74; Freedman–Lane P = 0.0003), whereas frequency controlling for keep-score is weak and not significant (ρ = 0.43, P = 0.065; S14 Fig). Keep-score therefore carries structural information beyond composition, while composition adds little beyond keep-score: glutamine is the best a priori target because its low keep-score reflects genuine structural permissiveness, not merely the ease of removing a rare residue.

  Consistent with this picture, the global topological complexity of a fold does not predict how well it tolerates glutamine removal. Across the proteome, a protein’s relative contact order — a measure of how non-local its stabilizing contacts are — predicts neither which glutamines are retained (Spearman ρ = −0.002 for keep-score and 0.010 for keep-propensity; both non-significant) nor how well the fold tolerates their removal (ρ = −0.044; P = 0.009, n = 3,581): a significant but negligible correlation, if anything toward greater tolerance — indicating no penalty for recoding topologically complex proteins. We also examined proteins predicted to contain knots, an extreme topological feature that affects only about 0.4% of proteins in any proteome, E. coli included [32]. Only 9 of the 1,589 well-ordered chains we analyzed (Rebuilt PAE < 5) were predicted to be knotted, and glutamine removal in these did not measurably affect their structures (change in RMSD) relative to the unknotted set (one-sided Mann–Whitney P = 0.383; one-sided Welch t-test P = 0.138). Knotted chains did, however, retain glutamine somewhat more than unknotted ones (median Q-keep-score 0.67 vs 0.50; Q-keep-propensity 0.36 vs 0.10; two-sided Mann–Whitney P = 0.075 and 0.012; Welch two-sample t test P = 0.042 and 0.028, respectively), though with only nine knotted chains these differences are tentative. Knotting, where detectable, therefore tracks greater glutamine retention rather than reduced tolerance to its removal. Any effect of discrete topology on removal tolerance is thus at most marginal and does not alter the proteome-wide conclusion. Structural tolerance of glutamine removal is thus governed by local environment rather than global fold complexity.

  The comparison with isoleucine removal is instructive beyond the question of target selection. Liu et al. [5] found that simple Ile-to-Val substitution — the most obvious replacement for a branched-chain amino acid — succeeded in only 43% of essential proteins tested. The remaining cases required iterative application of ESM2, MSA Transformer, ProteinMPNN, and AfDesign, with compensatory mutations at structural neighbor positions, and a median of four design attempts per gene. Three ribosomal genes resisted all computational approaches entirely and were solved only by random mutagenesis, yielding substitutions (Ile to Gly, Trp, or Phe) that no model had predicted. Correspondingly, our proteome-wide glutamine removal — using only the top ProteinMPNN prediction at each position, without compensatory mutations or iterative optimization — preserved native folds in 85.5% of proteins, matching the rebuilt baseline. These are different measures — computational fold preservation versus experimental fitness — not the same scale. We therefore tested the comparison directly, removing isoleucine across the same proteome with the same pipeline used for glutamine: isoleucine is the more disruptive of the two to remove (Fig 8), a computational counterpart to the experimental isoleucine removal of Liu et al. This is what the keep-score framework predicts — a lower keep-score corresponds to easier elimination, because fewer positions impose strong constraints on the resident amino acid.

  A recognized concern with pipelines that both design and evaluate sequences is circularity: ProteinMPNN and AlphaFold2 are trained on overlapping structural data, so a glutamine-free proteome might fold well in silico simply because the design tool avoids what the evaluation tool would penalize, and because the AlphaFoldDB references are themselves predictions, the comparison is in part of predicted models against predicted models. Several features of our analysis argue that the result is not merely this artifact. First, the framework discriminates: removing isoleucine — a higher-keep-score residue — is measurably more disruptive than removing glutamine across the same proteome and pipeline (Fig 8), and glycine, a positive control, is more disruptive still. A pipeline that only reproduced shared training bias could not separate these residues in the order their keep-scores predict. Second, the two keep-scores are methodologically independent: MSA conservation uses no structural model and shares no training data with ProteinMPNN or AlphaFold2, yet converges on the same ranking (Fig 1), and sequences designed by one criterion are predicted as replaceable by the other. Third, our baseline is an independent reproducibility metric rather than model self-confidence — the ease-score is the TMscore of the native rebuild against its AlphaFoldDB reference, computed without recourse to pLDDT or PAE, so it normalizes precisely for the predicted-versus-predicted concern by asking only whether a redesigned sequence reproduces its own reference as faithfully as the native sequence does. We nonetheless concede a real limit of the design method: because ProteinMPNN selects replacements that reproduce the native backbone whatever residue is removed, it folds an isoleucine-free proteome nearly as well as a glutamine-free one by TMscore, compressing the per-residue difference (Fig 8). This is why we resolve that difference with the more sensitive RMSD metric and with MSA-guided designs, which do not optimize for structure — and why ProteinMPNN’s fold-reproducing tendency is itself the reason we cross-validate every conclusion against evolutionary conservation.

  We validated the structural integrity of the glutamine-free proteome through three complementary computational approaches, each targeting a different potential failure mode: local confidence at substitution sites, Rosetta energetics, and an orthogonal structure predictor (ESMFold) — whose capacity to interpret reduced-alphabet sequences has been independently demonstrated by Ieremie et al. [28]. All three approaches confirmed that glutamine removal does not compromise fold integrity (Fig 5). For the approximately 14% of No-Gln models falling below the TMscore 0.8 threshold, FoldMason local alignment, flexible superposition, and comparison with BioEmu conformational ensembles consistently indicated that low scores reflect intrinsic protein flexibility rather than misfolding (Fig 6). We found no evidence that removing glutamine causes structural failure in any E. coli protein.

  The TEM1-Q experiment provides a single but informative data point. It does not validate the proteome-wide analysis — that would require systematic experimental testing of essential proteins, as Liu et al. [5] performed for isoleucine in the ribosome. What it does show is that the computational pipeline produces predictions consistent with biological function in at least one well-characterized case, and that glutamine removal is compatible with the full chain of events required for a periplasmic enzyme: translation, secretion, processing, and catalysis. Expressed from the same vector and regulatory context as native TEM-1, so that the two differ only by the eight glutamine substitutions, TEM1-Q matched the native enzyme’s resistance profile: both supported growth at ampicillin concentrations up to 400 µg/mL and neither grew at 800 µg/mL. An in-gel activity assay corroborated this: total cell extracts of the two strains showed comparable β-lactamase (nitrocefin) activity at the same migration position (S12 Fig). By both a whole-cell resistance phenotype and an in-gel activity assay, glutamine removal leaves folding and catalytic function intact to the resolution of these assays.

  ProteinMPNN-guided glutamine removal substantially outperformed MSA-based removal, with 85.5% of models above threshold compared with 78% for MSA-guided designs. This difference likely reflects the distinct information captured by each method. ProteinMPNN leverages structural context and long-range packing constraints to identify substitutions compatible with the native fold, whereas MSA consensus preserves residues that have persisted through evolutionary history — a signal that includes functional constraints, translational efficiency, and phylogenetic inertia beyond fold stability alone. Notably, in the optimal-recoding analysis, ProteinMPNN preserved native folds at much lower sequence identity: 46% compared with 66% for MSA consensus (S7 Fig). This demonstrates that the sequence space compatible with natural protein folds is vastly larger than what evolution has explored, and that structural compatibility is less restrictive than evolutionary conservation suggests. That inverse-folding models can navigate severely restricted sequence spaces has been confirmed experimentally: Giacobelli et al. [25] used ProteinMPNN and RFdiffusion to design de novo proteins from a 10-amino-acid alphabet, obtaining stable structures that adopted their intended topologies and resisted thermal and chemical denaturation. Liu et al. [5] reached a similar conclusion from the experimental side: their most challenging ribosomal designs required structural models (ProteinMPNN, AfDesign) rather than evolutionary ones (ESM2, MSA Transformer), and the successful designs often occupied regions of sequence space distant from any natural homologue. The convergence of these observations across two independent studies and two different amino acids reinforces the view that inverse-folding models capture design principles that evolutionary sampling has not fully explored.

  The computational finding that glutamine is dispensable aligns with natural evolutionary evidence. Among the 4,363 E. coli proteins, 103 already lack glutamine natively, and among the remaining 4,260, we identified 948 with at least one glutamine-free ortholog across 6,369 prokaryotic proteomes. Thus, at least 24% of E. coli proteins have natural orthologs that lack this amino acid. When we examined all 20 amino acids, glutamine was among three residues — alongside lysine and asparagine — lying above the fitted abundance trend in amino-acid-free orthology (Fig 7). This places glutamine among the few residues that appear unusually dispensable in natural proteomes, consistent with our computational results reflecting genuine biological possibility. The keep-score ranking is not confined to E. coli: ProteinMPNN keep-scores for the Saccharomyces cerevisiae proteome correlate strongly with those of E. coli across all twenty amino acids, with glutamine lowest and proline and glycine highest in both (Fig 9). That the ranking holds across more than a billion years of evolutionary divergence indicates that, from ProteinMPNN’s perspective, keep-score reflects intrinsic properties of protein structure rather than the composition of any single proteome.

  If glutamine is so readily replaceable, why has every known organism retained it in the genetic code? Several considerations may resolve this apparent paradox. First, glutamine serves essential metabolic functions as the primary carrier of organic nitrogen in cellular metabolism; its abundance in proteomes may be driven by biosynthetic convenience and metabolic integration rather than structural necessity. Second, our study demonstrates fold preservation and, in the case of TEM-1, catalytic activity, but glutamine residues in other active sites, protein-protein interfaces, allosteric networks, or post-translational modification sites may be essential for function even when the surrounding fold is maintained. A protein that folds correctly without glutamine may nonetheless be catalytically dead or unable to participate in essential interactions — a distinction that genome-wide experimental testing will be needed to resolve. Third, theories of genetic code evolution often propose that glutamine was a late addition to the amino acid alphabet, biosynthetically derived from glutamate after the core protein architecture had already been established [1,33]. Our finding that modern proteins can be recoded without glutamine while preserving their folds is consistent with this hypothesis: if the ancestral proteome evolved before glutamine entered the repertoire, contemporary structures may retain the capacity to function without it. Finally, the genetic code may simply be frozen in a suboptimal state — removing an amino acid would require coordinated changes across thousands of proteins, a barrier that may be insurmountable through natural evolution even if the endpoint is structurally viable. The experimental framework of Liu et al. [5] shows that synthetic biology can overcome this barrier one protein complex at a time.

  Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. This study is primarily a computational analysis; apart from the single TEM1-Q consistency check, all evidence for fold preservation comes from structure prediction algorithms. AlphaFold2 may systematically underestimate the structural impact of mutations because it was trained predominantly on native sequences, potentially biasing outputs toward folded conformations. We mitigated this concern through validation with ESMFold, Rosetta energetics, and comparison with BioEmu conformational ensembles, but we cannot exclude that some proteins scored as correctly folded would in fact misfold experimentally.

  A further limitation concerns the design step rather than the evaluation. Because each glutamine replacement was chosen from the ProteinMPNN probability table conditioned on the native sequence (Methods), the substitutions represent per-position marginal optima and do not model second-order coupling among the co-substituted positions; the recoded proteins were not jointly re-optimized. This concern is mitigated by the readout: because fold evaluation was performed on the fully recoded sequence, destabilization arising from coupling among the simultaneous substitutions would register in the AlphaFold2, ESMFold, Rosetta, and BioEmu comparisons, and none was detected. Consistent with this, Liu et al. [5] found that isoleucine removal frequently required compensatory mutations at structural-neighbour positions, whereas single top-choice glutamine replacement, without compensatory changes, preserved native folds in 85.5% of proteins — as expected if glutamine tends to occupy the less constrained, less coupled positions. The AlphaFoldDB references against which we compare are themselves predictions, and our ease-score normalization, while controlling for prediction variability, does not eliminate this dependency. Additionally, our analyses excluded proteins shorter than 50 or longer than 890 residues. Within these bounds, we found no evidence of misfolding attributable to glutamine removal using any available computational method — but absence of evidence from prediction tools is not evidence of absence of misfolding in vivo. A further limitation is intrinsic to the readout rather than the tools. Our analysis evaluates whether reduced-alphabet sequences are compatible with the native structural state, not the kinetics by which that state is reached; preservation of the predicted native fold therefore does not establish preservation of folding rates, kinetic stability, folding cooperativity, or the avoidance of off-pathway intermediates. This distinction is not merely formal. Using alphabet size as a proxy for evolutionary time, Faísca and co-workers found that the folding consequences of a reduced alphabet depend strongly on topology: unknotted and shallowly knotted proteins largely retained two-state cooperativity and efficient folding under an ancestral ten-amino-acid alphabet, whereas a deeply knotted protein had its cooperativity and folding ability significantly compromised [34]. A companion study, isolating topology in structure-based simulations, found that kinetic stability rises with knot depth and that enlarging the amino-acid alphabet predominantly enhances kinetic stability [35]. Native-state structure can thus appear preserved while kinetic and cooperative properties are not — a caveat that applies directly to conclusions, such as ours, drawn from structural and stability metrics alone.

  Enzymes in which a glutamine residue participates directly in catalysis would provide the most stringent tests of whether fold preservation translates to functional preservation. It would also be informative to ask where glutamine persists despite low keep-score. The three metrics used here are complementary by design: ProteinMPNN keep-score reflects structural constraint alone, MSA keep-score summarizes the evolutionary behaviour of glutamines in bulk, and the fraction of positions conserved in over 99% of orthologues isolates the minority retained across evolution despite low structural constraint — a set enriched for glutamines essential for reasons beyond fold stability, such as catalysis, binding, post-translational modification, or regulation. The ~ 7% of glutamine positions so conserved (S15 Fig) may therefore mark functionally critical sites worthy of targeted investigation. Two further questions remain open: whether lysine and asparagine — the other amino acids lying above the abundance trend in amino-acid-free orthology — are similarly eliminable, and whether multiple amino acids can be removed simultaneously before a hard structural barrier is reached. On the latter, the demonstration by Shibue et al. [6] that an ancestral nucleoside kinase retains solubility, stability, and catalytic activity with only 13 amino acid types suggests that the barrier may lie well beyond single-amino-acid removal, at least at the level of an individual protein.

  The iterative design-build-test framework established by Liu et al. [5] for isoleucine-free ribosomal proteins provides a direct experimental path to test whether the keep-score ranking predicts biological outcome. Systematic replacement of glutamine in essential E. coli genes, followed by fitness measurements, would reveal whether computational structural replaceability translates to experimental viability — and whether glutamine removal is indeed more tractable than isoleucine removal, as our analysis predicts. Liu et al.'s demonstration that an essential complex can be rebuilt without one canonical amino acid, combined with the computational framework developed here for rational target selection, together provide a roadmap toward identifying the minimal amino acid alphabet compatible with cellular life.


## Materials and methods

### External data sources
  Reference 3D models for the complete proteome of Escherichia coli K-12 (UniProt Proteome ID UP000000625) were downloaded as PDB files from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database version 4 (AlphaFoldDB) [36] as a tar archive (https://ftp.ebi.ac.uk/pub/databases/alphafold/latest/UP000000625_83333_ECOLI_v4.tar), which includes 4,363 single-chain models. Native sequences were extracted from these PDB files with a Python script.

  The Saccharomyces cerevisiae proteome was similarly downloaded from AlphaFoldDB (UniProt Proteome ID UP000002311) with 6,055 single-chain models.

  From UniProt Reference Proteomes (https://www.uniprot.org/proteomes) we downloaded all 9,285 Eubacterial proteomes as of September 14, 2024. This “eubacterial” collection was the source of all multiple sequence alignments (see below).

  From the same database we downloaded all 9,371 Eubacterial and 349 Archaeal proteomes as of October 26, 2024. This “prokaryotic” collection was used only to determine per-species amino acid compositions.

  From NCBI RefSeq (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/refseq/about/prokaryotes/), we downloaded 6,369 complete reference prokaryotic proteomes as of October 1, 2025. These were used to identify orthologs of E. coli proteins lacking glutamine (see below).


### Conservation multiple sequence alignments
  Proteins from the eubacterial collection were made non-redundant at 95% identity using MMseqs2 cluster [37]. We then searched this non-redundant database for homologs of the 4,363 E. coli native sequences with MMseqs2, run with its default --max-seqs 300; because the target database was split three ways, each query returned at most 420 hits. Homologs were read from the MMseqs2 tabular output (.m8), which is ordered by alignment quality. For queries with more than 200 homologs, every second entry of the quality-sorted.m8 (odd-indexed, i % 2) was dropped, halving alignment depth while preserving the quality-rank distribution; this decimation is deterministic, using no random selection and no seed. A conservation MSA was then built from each E. coli query and its retained homologs using MUSCLE [38]; no MSA was built for queries with fewer than 30 homologs. Alignments were trimmed to remove columns where the E. coli query had a gap. These conservation MSAs are the source of every sequence-conservation quantity in this work — keep-score and keep-propensity, replacement tendencies, MSA-consensus recoding, and MSA-guided glutamine replacement — and are distinct from the a3m alignments used as input to AlphaFold2 (see “In-house Prediction of 3D Structures”).


### ProteinMPNN probability tables
  For each protein, the inverse-folding probability table was obtained with LigandMPNN version 1.5.5 [39] as the average of 10 scoring runs. All inputs were ligand-free protein backbones, and these probabilities are referred to as ProteinMPNN probabilities throughout this work. Parameters: --model_type 'ligand_mpnn' --seed 111 --single_aa_score 1 --use_sequence 1 --batch_size 1 --number_of_batches 10. With --single_aa_score 1 --use_sequence 1, each position’s amino-acid probabilities are evaluated in the context of the native residues at all other positions, so the per-position scores are structure-derived but conditioned on native sequence context rather than computed from backbone geometry alone. Scoring was therefore always performed on native, glutamine-containing backbones in native sequence context; no glutamine-free sequence was ever scored by ProteinMPNN, and each glutamine replacement was taken as the highest-probability non-glutamine residue from this native-context table. Averaging over 10 runs with a fixed seed makes the per-position probabilities reproducible while accounting for the stochastic decoding order. A position-specific scoring matrix (PSSM) was similarly derived from normalized amino acid frequencies in the corresponding conservation-MSA columns.


### Keep-propensity and keep-score
  For any residue in a protein, its keep-propensity measures how strongly it is favored or disfavored at that position. From ProteinMPNN, it is the predicted probability of the native amino acid minus the probability of the highest-probability alternative. From MSAs, it is the frequency of the native amino acid minus the frequency of the most frequent alternative. Keep-propensity ranges from −1.0 (strong preference for replacement) to +1.0 (strong preference for retention).

  An amino acid’s keep-score is the fraction of positions where the native residue scores higher than the best alternative—equivalently, the area under the positive region of its keep-propensity distribution. Keep-scores for the S. cerevisiae proteome were calculated similarly from ProteinMPNN probability tables.


### Conservation and replacement tendencies
  For amino acid X, we calculated its conservation tendency as the mean frequency of X across all MSA columns where X is native (or, for ProteinMPNN, the mean P(X) at those positions). We also calculated 19-component replacement tendency vectors: for each amino acid Y ≠ X, the vector element is the mean frequency (or probability) of Y at positions where X is native. The 19 replacement tendencies were normalized to sum to 1.0.


### Solvent accessibility and glutamine burial
  Solvent accessibility was computed for the AlphaFoldDB reference structures with DSSP [40]. For each glutamine, relative solvent accessibility (RSA) was taken as the DSSP absolute solvent-accessible surface area divided by 225 Å², the maximum accessibility reported for glutamine by Tien et al. [41]. Glutamines with RSA < 0.20 were classified as buried and the remainder as exposed. DSSP residues were mapped to one-based sequence positions within each chain (by chain identifier, residue number, and insertion code) so that structural and sequence records could be joined by protein identifier and position; the same DSSP output also provided the per-residue secondary-structure state used below.

  For each glutamine position we drew two retention measures from its conservation-MSA column: an MSA keep indicator, set to 1 when glutamine was the most frequent standard amino acid in the column and 0 otherwise, and the per-position keep-propensity defined earlier (glutamine’s gap-normalized column frequency minus that of its most frequent non-glutamine alternative). ProteinMPNN retention was defined analogously from the probability table (glutamine the highest-probability residue, and its keep-propensity). Keep fraction and keep-propensity were then summarized separately for buried and exposed glutamines.

  The association of burial with the binary keep indicator was tested by chi-square and point-biserial correlation (exposed coded as 1); keep-propensity was compared between buried and exposed positions by two-sided Mann–Whitney test and Spearman rank correlation.

  We further tested whether glutamine retention depends on secondary structure, reading the DSSP state from the same records. States were grouped as helix (H, G), strand (E, B), and coil (I, T, S, the DSSP blank state, and poly-proline-II, P). Retention was summarized per class as the keep fraction (the MSA keep indicator) and as the gap-normalized keep-propensity defined above. The keep fraction differed across the three classes (chi-square = 398.4, 2 df, P = 3.0 × 10−87; Cramér’s V = 0.0824), as did keep-propensity (Kruskal–Wallis H = 413.3, 2 df, P = 1.8 × 10−90; epsilon-squared = 0.0070). Both effects are small despite the decisive P-values: glutamines in helices and strands are retained modestly more often than those in coil, with strands marginally above helices, and the association explains little of the position-to-position variation in retention.


### Contact order and fold topology
  To ask whether global fold topology influences glutamine removability, we computed relative contact order (RCO) for the AlphaFoldDB reference structures following Plaxco et al. [42]. Intrachain contacts were defined between Cβ atoms (Cα for glycine): a residue pair was a contact when its representative atoms lay within 8 Å and the residues were separated by at least three positions in sequence. RCO was calculated as the sum of sequence separations |i − j| over all qualifying contacts, divided by the product of chain length and contact count. Residues were ordered by their PDB ATOM records, non-primary alternate locations were ignored, and proteins without qualifying contacts were assigned a missing RCO and excluded from correlation analyses.

  We related RCO to two per-protein quantities. First, to test whether glutamine conservation tracks topology, we joined finite RCO values to the MSA-derived glutamine keep-score and keep-propensity (defined above), restricting to proteins with finite RCO and at least one reference glutamine, and repeated the analysis retaining only proteins with at least three reference glutamines. Second, to test whether topology predicts the structural cost of glutamine removal, we quantified per-protein sensitivity as ΔRMSD = RMSD(No-Gln) − RMSD(Rebuilt), restricting to proteins with finite RCO and Rebuilt TMscore > 0.8, and repeated the rank analysis at Rebuilt TMscore thresholds of 0.90 and 0.95. Associations were assessed with two-sided Spearman rank and Pearson product-moment correlations.

  As an extreme topological feature, we also tested chain knotting. Backbone entanglement was classified with Knoto-ID [43], which evaluates open protein backbones as knotoids without imposing an arbitrary chain closure. We used the precomputed Knoto-ID output for the models whose Rebuilt structures had mean PAE < 5 Å, since disordered or flexible chains can mislead the knotoid classification; all 1,589 such chains returned a valid classification. Chains were assigned to the knotted or unknotted group by the Knoto-ID prediction field, yielding 9 knotted and 1,580 unknotted; “knotted” here denotes a predicted, not experimentally determined, topology. We asked whether knotted proteins were more sensitive to glutamine removal, quantifying sensitivity as ΔRMSD = RMSD(No-Gln) − RMSD(Rebuilt), computed independently for the ProteinMPNN (pMPNN_no_gln) and MSA (MSA_no_gln) glutamine-removal sequences, each against its own Rebuilt baseline and including only proteins with both RMSD values present. Because the a priori hypothesis was directional, knotted and unknotted groups were compared by one-sided Mann–Whitney and Welch two-sample t tests (alternative: greater). We separately compared the two groups’ glutamine keep-score and keep-propensity — with keep-score counting a position as Q-retained when Q was the most frequent residue in its alignment column (ties to Q) — by two-sided Mann–Whitney and Welch two-sample t tests, restricted to the 1,570 chains with a conservation MSA and at least one reference glutamine.


### In-house prediction of 3D structures
  Sequences were converted to 3D structures with AlphaFold2 using LocalColabFold [26], by running colabfold_batch <a3m_input> <outdir> with default parameters (five models per sequence); no parameters were overridden.

  In both regimes a single a3m was built per protein, from the native sequence, and reused for every variant of that protein by replacing only the query line with the sequence to be modeled (native or variant) while preserving the alignment gaps; the a3m columns are therefore identical across all of a protein’s variants. Consequently, model-to-model comparisons are always made within a single a3m source — within each new dataset every variant of a protein shares one ColabFold a3m — and no model built from one a3m source is ever compared with a model built from the other.

Unless otherwise noted, analyses used the 4,148 proteins between 50 and 890 amino acids. Each dataset was modeled from one of two a3m sources (see “In-house Prediction of 3D Structures”), and models are never compared across a3m sources; the datasets are therefore grouped below by a3m source.

### Structural evaluation
  All structural models were compared to their AlphaFoldDB reference with USalign [44], run as USalign <query_pdb> <reference_pdb> -outfmt 2. From the tabular output we used two fields: TM1 as the TMscore (TM1 and TM2 are identical here, because query and reference have the same length and no gaps, so TMscore normalized by query length equals TMscore normalized by reference length) and the RMSD field as the RMSD in Å. The reference was always the corresponding model from the AlphaFoldDB E. coli proteome.

  AlphaFold2 produces five models per sequence. TMscore, ease-score, and Rebuilt RMSD were taken from the best-ranked model (rank 001). For datasets modeled from ColabFold a3m, the RMSD reported for a sequence is the mean RMSD over all five models; the added averaging is used because these datasets support the fine per-residue RMSD comparisons where single-model prediction noise would otherwise dominate. The two conventions are never mixed within a single comparison.

  Throughout this work, models with TMscore ≥ 0.8 are classified as structurally correct; those below 0.8 are flagged for further analysis. This threshold is deliberately conservative: a TMscore above 0.5 already indicates a shared fold [45], and we adopt 0.8 so that “structurally correct” denotes close backbone agreement rather than merely shared topology. The choice is also calibrated to our own baseline: 86% of native rebuilds exceed 0.8 against their AlphaFoldDB references (the ease-score; Fig 2A), and sub-threshold proteins have systematically high PAE (r = −0.81; Fig 2B), indicating intrinsic flexibility rather than misfolding.


### Ease-score and squared mean PAE
  We define the ease-score of a protein as the TMscore of its Rebuilt model against its AlphaFoldDB reference. It measures how reliably AlphaFold2 reproduces the same structure from an identical sequence and serves as a baseline for interpreting TMscores of models from non-native sequences. By analogy, a protein’s Rebuilt RMSD is the RMSD of its Rebuilt (rank-001) model against its AlphaFoldDB reference — an RMSD counterpart to the ease-score. It is used as a selection covariate for the mut_each_300_msa and QIG_571_msa sets.

  Squared mean PAE is calculated as the arithmetic mean of all pairwise predicted aligned error (PAE) values from AlphaFold2, squared: (mean PAE)². This transformation linearizes the relationship with TMscore.

  For binned analyses (Figs 4 and S6), proteins were sorted by ease-score into 50 equal-sized bins, and each protein keeps its bin across recoding strategies, so strategies are compared on the same proteins within each bin — except the MSA-based series, which cover only the proteins with an available MSA.


### Local confidence analysis
  To assess whether glutamine removal affected local structural confidence, we computed the mean pLDDT of residues within 7 Å of each substitution site. Distance was measured between Cα atoms: a residue was included if its Cα was within 7 Å of the Cα of the replaced glutamine. Mean neighborhood pLDDT was compared between Rebuilt and No-Gln models.


### Energetic stability analysis
  Thermodynamic impact of glutamine removal was evaluated using PyRosetta (PyRosetta-4 2025.51; Rosetta Commons) [46]. Coordinate constraints were applied to all Cα atoms using a harmonic potential with standard deviation 1.0 Å, tethering the backbone to the initial AlphaFold2 prediction while allowing side-chain repacking and minor backbone adjustments. ### Flexible domain alignments
  Using a custom piecewise alignment script, we performed flexible superpositions between query models and their AlphaFoldDB references. Backbone atoms and per-residue pLDDT scores were extracted, and “ordered” segments were defined as contiguous regions with smoothed pLDDT ≥ 50 spanning at least 30 residues. Each ordered segment was aligned separately using USalign (-TMscore 1). Segmentation was retained only if it improved the coverage-weighted TMscore by ≥ 0.04 with median Cα deviation ≤ 3 Å. When only one ordered block was present, a single split at the largest local deviation peak was tested under the same criteria. Final scores were aggregated over the union of aligned residues, yielding a single coverage-weighted TMscore per structure pair. The procedure returned no result for 52 of the 600 sub-threshold proteins (S11 Fig).


### FoldMason alignments
  Flexible alignments were also performed using FoldMason “easy-msa” (https://github.com/steineggerlab/foldmason, [49]) to compare Rebuilt and recoded models against AlphaFoldDB references. The primary metric was mean LDDT. Models were classified as perfect (mean LDDT = 1.0) or good (mean LDDT > 0.9).


### Conformational ensembles with BioEmu
  BioEmu (https://github.com/microsoft/bioemu, [50]) was used to generate 50 conformational samples from the approximate equilibrium distribution of native-sequence structures. Unrealistic trajectories were filtered, leaving a mean of 38.6 (median 43) usable samples per protein. No usable ensemble was obtained for 103 of the 600 sub-threshold proteins (S11 Fig). Each sample was aligned to the AlphaFoldDB reference using USalign, and TMscore distributions were computed. Because each protein has a single No-Gln model, its No-Gln TMscore was tested as a one-sided outlier against the native BioEmu ensemble. For each protein the No-Gln value was standardized as (x − m)/(s·√(1 + 1/n)), where m, s, and n are the mean, standard deviation, and number of retained trajectory samples of the native ensemble, and a one-sided P-value was obtained from the t-distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom (testing whether the No-Gln value lies below the ensemble mean). Per-protein P-values were corrected across proteins by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.


### ESMFold validation
  As an orthogonal structure prediction method, we used ESMFold [51] via EsmForProteinFolding from the HuggingFace Transformers library (version 4.46.3) with the esmfold_v1 checkpoint. Both Rebuilt (native) and No-Gln sequences were modeled for all 4,148 proteins. Default parameters were used, except for proteins longer than 780 residues, where chunk_size was set to 128. TMscores were computed against AlphaFoldDB references using USalign with the same parameters as for AlphaFold2 models.


### Natural proteins without glutamine
  Using the MMseqs2 easy-rbh pipeline, we searched each of the 6,369 RefSeq prokaryotic proteomes for orthologs (reciprocal best hits) of E. coli proteins with E-value ≤ 1e-15. Orthologs lacking glutamine were identified with Python. The same reciprocal-best-hit ortholog sets were then screened for the absence of each amino acid individually: for every amino acid X, we counted the E. coli proteins with at least one ortholog containing no X. These per-amino-acid fractions are the points in Fig 7; glutamine is one of the 20. To summarize the relationship between an amino acid’s abundance and the fraction of E. coli proteins with an orthologue lacking it (Fig 7, green curve), we fitted the empirical trend across the 20 amino acids with y = a + b·x−p, where x is amino-acid abundance and y is the fraction of queries with at least one orthologue lacking that amino acid. The three parameters were estimated together by nonlinear least squares (scipy.optimize.curve_fit), giving a = −0.099, b = 0.013, p = 0.933 (residual sum of squares = 0.153). The fit is descriptive, providing a reference trend against which individual amino acids are compared, and does not represent a null expectation of amino-acid-free orthology.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed in Python 3.11.14 with pandas 2.3.3, NumPy 1.26.4, SciPy 1.16.3, and statsmodels 0.14.6; plotting and the remaining dependencies are listed under “In-house scripts.” Statistics specific to a single analysis are defined where that analysis is introduced (see below); the procedures common to several analyses are collected here.

  Correlation and regression. Straight-line fits used numpy.polyfit (degree 1) for panel slopes, with the coefficient of determination computed as R² = 1 − SS_res/SS_tot (equal to the squared Pearson correlation for an ordinary-least-squares line, Fig 5A–5C); regressions reported together with a correlation coefficient used scipy.stats.linregress (Figs 1 and 9). Product-moment correlations used numpy.corrcoef or scipy.stats.pearsonr, and rank correlations used scipy.stats.spearmanr — the latter as the primary statistic for the E. coli–S. cerevisiae keep-score comparison (Fig 9) and for the supplementary correlations of S5 Fig. Pairwise sequence identity was computed directly, without alignment, because recoded and native sequences are the same length with no gaps.

  Paired comparisons of removal effect. Per-protein differences in structural score between recoding strategies (Figs 3, 8A and 8B) were tested with the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (scipy.stats.wilcoxon). For the proteome-wide glutamine-versus-isoleucine comparison (IQ_full set), the paired t test (scipy.stats.ttest_rel) was additionally applied to TMscore.

  Multiple-testing correction. Three procedures were applied, matched to the structure of each family of tests. The per-protein BioEmu outlier screen (Fig 6C; see “Conformational Ensembles with BioEmu”) was corrected for false-discovery rate by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. The 190 amino-acid-pair Wilcoxon tests of Fig 3 were corrected for family-wise error by the Hochberg step-up procedure, and the three pairwise secondary-structure comparisons (see “Solvent Accessibility and Glutamine Burial”) by the Holm procedure (statsmodels.stats.multitest.multipletests, method holm). We used false-discovery-rate control for the large per-protein screen, where a few false positives among hundreds of tests are acceptable, and family-wise control for the small, structured pairwise families, where any single false claim of difference would be misleading.

  Effect sizes. For contingency comparisons we report Cramér’s V = √(χ²/[n·min(r − 1, c − 1)]); for the Kruskal–Wallis comparison of keep-propensity across secondary-structure classes we report epsilon-squared = (H − k + 1)/(n − k). Both were computed directly in NumPy.

  Partial correlation (abundance control). To separate an amino acid’s conservation (keep-score) from its natural abundance as predictors of per-protein removal sensitivity, we computed partial Spearman correlations: each variable was rank-transformed, the two variables of interest were residualized on the control variable by ordinary least squares (numpy.linalg.lstsq), and the residual ranks were correlated. Significance was assessed by a two-sided Freedman–Lane residual permutation test (99,999 permutations), and a 95% confidence interval by resampling proteins with replacement (10,000 bootstrap replicates); both were implemented in NumPy.

  Percentile envelope (Fig 5C). For the ESMFold analysis, the ease-score range [0.15, 1.0] was divided into 50 equal-width bins; within each bin containing at least 12 proteins, the 5th and 95th percentiles of No-Gln TMscore defined a 90% envelope.

  Tests defined with their analyses. The chi-square, point-biserial, Mann–Whitney, and Spearman tests for glutamine burial and the chi-square/Cramér’s V and Kruskal–Wallis/epsilon-squared tests for secondary structure are described under “Solvent Accessibility and Glutamine Burial”; the Spearman and Pearson correlations for contact order and the one-sided Mann–Whitney and Welch t tests for chain knotting under “Contact Order and Fold Topology”; the descriptive nonlinear-least-squares trend of Fig 7 under “Natural Proteins Without Glutamine”; and the one-sided predictive-t outlier test for the BioEmu ensembles under “Conformational Ensembles with BioEmu.”

### Design and validation of a glutamine-free β-lactamase (TEM1-Q)
  As an experimental consistency check, we designed a TEM-1 β-lactamase variant (UniProt P62593) lacking all glutamine residues in the mature protein while retaining the glutamine in the signal peptide, using ProteinMPNN [39]. The selected design (TEM1-Q) carried eight substitutions: Q37R, Q86E, Q88D, Q97E, Q203R, Q204L, Q265T, and Q274A. The structure was predicted with AlphaFold3 [53] and assessed by pLDDT, RMSD and TMscore relative to the wild-type structure (USalign; [44]), and predicted thermodynamic stability was evaluated with PyRosetta (PyRosetta-4 2025.51, Rosetta Commons [46]) using the same constrained-relaxation protocol applied to the proteome-wide analysis.


### Plasmid construction
  The TEM1-Q gene was synthesized by Gene Universal Inc. (Newark, DE, USA). It was first cloned into pET-28a(+), whose inducible T7 promoter permits IPTG-driven high-level expression; this construct served only as an expression-insurance intermediate against the possibility of low TEM1-Q activity and provided no data reported here. For a promoter-matched comparison with native TEM-1, the TEM1-Q coding sequence was then transferred into pQE-30 (Qiagen), where it exactly replaced the native β-lactamase (ampR) coding sequence while preserving the weak constitutive ampR promoter. Because the resulting pQE-30–TEM1-Q and pQE-30–native plasmids are identical except for the eight glutamine codons and are expressed from the same promoter, the comparison controls for expression level and construct context.

  Subcloning into pQE-30 used NEBuilder HiFi DNA assembly. The pQE-30 backbone (2,600 bp) was linearized by inverse high-fidelity PCR with primers BB-Fwd (5’-ACTCTTCCTTTTTCAATATT-3’) and BB-Rev (5’-CTGTCAGACCAAGTTTACTC-3’) to excise the native bla open reading frame (nt 2396–3256). The TEM1-Q CDS (861 bp) was amplified from the pET-28a–TEM1-Q template with primers Ins-Fwd (5’-TCAATAATATTGAAAAAGGAAGAGTATGAGCATTCAGCATTTTCG-3’) and Ins-Rev (5’-TATATGAGTAAACTTGGTCTGACAGTTACCAATGTTTAATCAGGC-3’), adding 25-nt terminal overhangs homologous to the linearized backbone to yield a 911 bp amplicon. The backbone PCR was treated with DpnI (10 U, NEB) to remove methylated parental template. Insert and backbone were assembled with NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (50 °C, 60 min) and transformed into competent E. coli BL21 (DE3), with selection on ampicillin agar. Clones carrying TEM1-Q were identified by a BglII site absent from the native TEM1 CDS, and the resulting construct (3,461 bp) was verified by Sanger sequencing across both flanking junctions.


### Ampicillin-resistance assay
  Growth medium for plates and liquid cultures was Luria–Bertani (LB: 10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L NaCl). Ampicillin resistance was compared between E. coli carrying pQE-30–native TEM-1 and pQE-30–TEM1-Q; because both genes are expressed constitutively from the ampR promoter, no inducer was used. Overnight cultures were diluted 102- to 106-fold and plated (50 µL per plate) on LB agar containing ampicillin at graded concentrations from 0 to 800 µg/mL. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 20 h.


### β-lactamase zymography with nitrocefin
  β-lactamase activity was detected in-gel following non-reducing SDS-PAGE. Cells were lysed with BugBuster (Novagen, Millipore) supplemented with egg-white lysozyme (1,000 U/mL; Sigma) and Benzonase nuclease (25 U/mL; MilliporeSigma); lysates were centrifuged and the clarified supernatant was used directly, with no further purification. Supernatant was mixed with sample buffer (1/5 volume; 125 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 25% glycerol, 6.25% SDS, 0.05% bromophenol blue) lacking reducing agents and loaded without boiling — 40 µL per lane as the standard load and 20 µL as the half load — onto 12.5% polyacrylamide resolving gels, alongside prestained molecular-weight markers (Thermofisher) and positive and negative controls. Electrophoresis used a standard Tris-glycine system at 4–10 °C. Gels were renatured in 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.0) with 2% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 2 h at room temperature with gentle agitation, then 2 h at 37 °C in fresh buffer, and equilibrated with two 5-min washes in 100 mM potassium phosphate (pH 7.0). Activity was visualized by immersing the gel in freshly prepared 0.5 mM nitrocefin in 100 mM potassium phosphate (pH 7.0) at room temperature, protected from light; active bands appeared rapidly as a pink-to-red hydrolysis product against the yellow substrate and were documented immediately under white light. Total protein was visualized on a duplicate gel stained with 0.2% Coomassie Brilliant Blue (R-250, BioRad), in 10% acetic acid, and 50% methanol.
