# Messenger RNA vaccine platforms from molecular optimization to infectious disease and cancer immunotherapy: a narrative review


## Background and Objective
  Messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines now encompass licensed respiratory vaccines, a self-amplifying ribonucleic acid (RNA) product, and individualized cancer immunotherapies in late-stage development. The expansion of the field has made platform-level comparisons increasingly important because efficacy, tolerability, manufacturability, and tissue targeting are influenced by the RNA architecture as well as by the delivery system. This narrative review examines molecular engineering, delivery, emerging RNA architectures, infectious-disease translation, and cancer immunotherapy, with emphasis on developments reported through 2026.


## Methods
  Targeted literature searches were conducted in PubMed through 31 August 2026, complemented by reference chaining and targeted searches of official regulatory sources. English-language mechanistic studies, clinical trials, regulatory documents, and focused reviews were prioritized. Particular attention was given to evidence published from 2023 to 2026 on self-amplifying RNA (saRNA), circular RNA (circRNA), molecular optimization, and individualized neoantigen vaccines.


## Key Content and Findings
  Nucleoside-modified non-replicating mRNA formulated in lipid nanoparticles remains the most clinically validated architecture. Molecular optimization now extends beyond nucleoside substitution to cap chemistry, untranslated-region selection, coding-sequence and RNA-structure design, poly(A) control, and removal of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) impurities. saRNA has reached regulatory authorization and offers dose-sparing potential, whereas circRNA remains predominantly preclinical and still faces unresolved manufacturing and quality-control questions. Infectious-disease programs have established reusable capabilities in antigen design, lipid nanoparticle formulation, manufacturing, and regulatory comparability. In oncology, intismeran autogene plus pembrolizumab has produced durable recurrence-free survival (RFS) benefit in resected melanoma, while pancreatic-cancer studies of autogene cevumeran show that vaccine-induced T cell responses vary substantially between patients. Tumor clonality, neoantigen quality, antigen presentation, the immune microenvironment, treatment setting, and manufacturing time are therefore as important as the number of encoded mutations. Artificial intelligence (AI) can improve neoantigen ranking but does not remove the need for biological validation.


## Conclusions
  No RNA architecture is uniformly superior across indications. Further clinical progress will depend on matching transcript design and delivery to the intended immune response, improving predictive antigen selection and patient stratification, and transferring validated manufacturing and regulatory capabilities from infectious-disease vaccines to individualized oncology without assuming that platform maturity alone is sufficient for antitumor efficacy.


## Introduction
  Messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccination developed from a series of observations showing that exogenous in vitro-transcribed ribonucleic acid (RNA) can direct protein synthesis in vivo, followed by work defining the pharmacology, immunogenicity, and delivery requirements of synthetic transcripts (1-3). The platform is attractive because the encoded antigen can be changed without rebuilding a cell-based production process, the RNA does not require nuclear entry, and expression is transient. These advantages initially coexisted with three practical liabilities: rapid extracellular degradation, inefficient cellular uptake, and innate immune activation that could suppress translation. The subsequent development of sequence engineering, modified nucleosides, purification methods, and lipid-based carriers converted these liabilities from fixed properties into variables that can be tuned for a defined clinical purpose (4,5).

  The clinical landscape is now broader than the first generation of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccines. Non-replicating nucleoside-modified mRNA is established in licensed products, self-amplifying RNA (saRNA) has entered routine regulatory frameworks, and circular RNA (circRNA) is being evaluated as a more persistent but technically less mature alternative. In parallel, oncology programs have shifted from shared tumor-associated antigens toward patient-specific neoantigens selected from individual tumor genomes. These developments make it difficult to assess the field using a single linear narrative of “mRNA vaccines”. The relevant unit of comparison is increasingly the complete platform: RNA architecture, molecular design, purification, carrier, route, target cell, manufacturing process, and clinical setting.

  This review therefore has four objectives. First, it reorganizes the technical basis of mRNA vaccination in the sequence in which a product is designed and functions: transcript architecture and optimization, innate sensing, delivery, and biodistribution. Second, it compares conventional mRNA, saRNA, and circRNA as distinct technical routes. Third, it examines the current global research and development landscape across infectious disease and cancer. Fourth, it evaluates recent individualized cancer-vaccine data, including response heterogeneity and the role of artificial intelligence (AI) in neoantigen prioritization. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1598/rc).

Search concepts included “mRNA vaccine”, “messenger RNA”, “self-amplifying RNA”, “circular RNA”, “lipid nanoparticle”, “mRNA optimization”, “double-stranded RNA purification”, “neoantigen vaccine”, “pembrolizumab”, “PD-1”, “artificial intelligence”, and “neoantigen prediction”. ## Molecular and pharmacological foundations of mRNA vaccines

### Transcript architecture and intracellular pharmacology
  A conventional in vitro-transcribed mRNA vaccine contains a 5' cap, a 5' untranslated region (UTR), an open reading frame (ORF), a 3' UTR, and a poly(A) tail. These elements are not passive structural features. The cap recruits translation-initiation machinery and protects the 5' end, UTRs influence ribosome loading, localization, and decay, the coding region determines antigen sequence as well as local RNA structure, and the poly(A) tail contributes to transcript stability and translational competence (6,7). After cytosolic delivery, the RNA is translated without nuclear entry. Antigen produced in transfected cells can be processed for major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I presentation; secreted or released antigen can also be captured by antigen-presenting cells and presented through MHC class II or cross-presentation pathways. Figure 1 summarizes this sequence from molecular design to antigen expression and adaptive immunity.


### Molecular optimization: from sequence design to impurity control
  The molecular optimization problem is best considered as a balance among expression magnitude, expression duration, innate sensing, translation fidelity, and manufacturability. Early work established that incorporation of pseudouridine can reduce Toll-like receptor recognition and improve translation and biological stability (8,9). N1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ), subsequently adopted in leading nucleoside-modified mRNA vaccine platforms, further illustrates how nucleotide chemistry can suppress innate sensing while supporting protein expression (5,8,9). Sequence engineering can also support high expression without chemical nucleoside substitution in some settings (10), underscoring that nucleotide chemistry, sequence context, and delivery should be optimized as an integrated system. This benefit does not remove the need to examine translation fidelity. Mulroney and colleagues showed that m1Ψ can promote sequence-dependent +1 ribosomal frameshifting at susceptible ribosome-slippery sites, producing off-frame peptides and measurable T cell responses; synonymous redesign of those sites reduced frameshifting (11). The frameshifting finding is best interpreted as a sequence-engineering and quality-control constraint rather than evidence of a class-wide safety problem.

  Cap engineering has also moved beyond post-transcriptional enzymatic capping and early cap analogues. Co-transcriptional Cap 1 systems such as CleanCap can reduce processing steps while achieving high capping efficiency and correct cap orientation, which is useful for scalable in vitro transcription (IVT) workflows (6,12). The 5' and 3' UTRs remain major determinants of transcript stability and ribosome recruitment, and poly(A)-tail engineering is increasingly treated as a reproducibility problem as well as a stability problem because tail length and architecture influence both expression kinetics and manufacturing heterogeneity (7,13).

  Recent optimization work has moved toward joint sequence-structure design. Synonymous codon choice changes guanine-cytosine (GC) content, local secondary structure, ribosome kinetics, and the probability of unintended translation from alternative contexts. Accordingly, optimization algorithms increasingly score sequence and predicted structure together rather than maximizing codon frequency alone; recent work has emphasized multi-objective design that balances translation, structural stability, and manufacturability (13). This approach is especially relevant for long or highly structured transcripts, including saRNA, for which local sequence changes can affect both antigen expression and replication-associated RNA structures.

  Product-related impurities also belong within molecular optimization because they alter the effective pharmacology of the same nominal RNA sequence. dsRNA by-products of IVT can activate endosomal and cytosolic sensors, induce type I interferon, stimulate protein kinase R, and suppress translation. Purification therefore affects both tolerability and potency. Reverse-phase, ion-exchange, affinity, cellulose-based, and other chromatographic strategies are being developed to reduce dsRNA while preserving full-length product recovery (14). The practical implication is that sequence design, transcription conditions, capping, poly(A) control, and downstream purification should be evaluated as a coupled process rather than as independent manufacturing steps.


### Innate immune sensing: useful adjuvanticity versus translational arrest
  Innate sensing of RNA is neither uniformly desirable nor uniformly detrimental. Endosomal Toll-like receptors and cytosolic receptors such as retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I), melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5), and protein kinase R can promote dendritic-cell activation and cytokine production, but excessive signaling can reduce antigen production and increase reactogenicity. Nucleoside modification and impurity removal shift this balance toward higher expression with less type I interferon signaling, as illustrated in Figure 2 (8,9). The required balance also differs by indication. Prophylactic vaccines may benefit from a controlled inflammatory signal that supports germinal-center responses, whereas protein-replacement applications usually seek minimal innate activation. Cancer vaccines occupy an intermediate position because efficient antigen expression must coexist with sufficient antigen-presenting-cell activation.


### Delivery and biodistribution
  Delivery should follow molecular optimization in the technical sequence because the carrier is asked to protect a defined RNA product and place it in cells capable of using that product. Naked RNA, protamine complexes, polymers, lipoplexes, and lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have all been investigated; the major contemporary platform is the LNP. Typical LNPs contain an ionizable lipid, phospholipid, cholesterol, and a polyethylene-glycol lipid. Ionizable lipids reduce persistent surface charge at physiological pH while supporting RNA complexation during formulation and endosomal disruption after cellular uptake (15,16). Figure 3 places LNP delivery alongside older ex vivo dendritic-cell and direct in vivo approaches.

  LNP composition changes expression, tissue distribution, and inflammatory activity. Screening of biodegradable ionizable lipids for intramuscular vaccines showed that improved tolerability can be achieved without simply maximizing innate stimulation, because stronger LNP-driven inflammation does not necessarily produce greater vaccine immunogenicity (17). Conversely, LNPs are not immunologically inert. In preclinical studies, the ionizable lipid component and interleukin-6 signaling contributed to T follicular helper-cell and humoral responses (18). Biodistribution is therefore a design variable rather than a passive consequence of injection. Intramuscular formulations favor local uptake and drainage to regional lymphoid tissue, whereas intravenously administered charge-tuned lipoplexes can be redirected toward splenic antigen-presenting cells, an approach developed in cancer immunotherapy (19).


## Emerging RNA vaccine architectures

### Conventional non-replicating mRNA
  Conventional non-replicating mRNA remains the reference architecture because its pharmacology is comparatively simple and its clinical manufacturing history is extensive. The transcript encodes the antigen without an RNA replicase, so dose and expression depend on delivery efficiency, transcript stability, and translation. Its principal strengths are modular design, established LNP manufacturing, and a comparatively mature analytical and regulatory framework. Limitations include the amount of RNA needed for some indications, the need for repeated dosing in many settings, and sensitivity of the RNA-LNP product to formulation and storage conditions.


### saRNA
  saRNA incorporates replicase genes, usually derived from an alphavirus replicon, while replacing viral structural genes with the vaccine antigen. After entry into the cytoplasm, replicase increases the number of antigen-encoding RNA templates, permitting antigen expression at lower administered RNA doses than conventional mRNA in many experimental settings (20,21). The regulatory trajectory is now genuinely international. Japan approved ARCT-154 in November 2023, making it the first saRNA vaccine to receive full regulatory approval worldwide (22). The same platform, marketed as Kostaive (zapomeran), subsequently received European Union authorization in February 2025 and United Kingdom approval in January 2026 (23,24). These milestones provide proof that a replicating RNA architecture can meet regulatory standards, while also emphasizing that approval remains product-, formulation-, manufacturing-, and jurisdiction-specific.


### circRNA
  circRNA differs from both conventional mRNA and saRNA because its covalently closed backbone lacks free 5' and 3' ends and is comparatively resistant to exonuclease-mediated decay. Translation is generally cap-independent and may rely on internal ribosome entry sites or related translation elements. Sustained antigen availability could reduce dose or dosing frequency, and circRNA has been explored for prophylactic antigens and cancer targets. Its development, however, is less mature than that of saRNA. Efficient circularization, removal of linear precursors and aberrant products, translation consistency, innate-sensing profiles, and analytical definition of product identity remain active technical problems (25). As of this review, circRNA vaccines should therefore be described as an emerging, predominantly preclinical platform rather than as a clinically validated alternative.

  Table 2 compares these three architectures. The comparison argues against ranking them on a single axis. Conventional mRNA has the strongest clinical evidence and the simplest regulatory precedent; saRNA offers an established route to dose reduction at the cost of a more complex transcript; circRNA offers prolonged stability but has the least mature clinical and manufacturing evidence. Platform selection should follow the intended antigen, route, dosing schedule, and acceptable level of innate stimulation.


## Global research and development landscape and infectious-disease translation

### Global platform differentiation
  The global mRNA vaccine landscape is now differentiated by product architecture, intended use, and regulatory setting rather than by a single race to reproduce COVID-19 vaccines. BioNTech/Pfizer and Moderna established non-replicating nucleoside-modified mRNA-LNP at population scale, and the platform has since extended to respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and seasonal influenza. In parallel, saRNA has moved from dose-sparing theory into licensed products, whereas individualized oncology programs have shifted the manufacturing problem toward rapid, patient-specific release. Table 3 summarizes these technical routes and their current maturity (5,23,32).

  Regional trajectories show why a practical global assessment must include more than the United States and European Union. Japan supplied the first full approval of a saRNA vaccine in 2023, followed by European Union authorization in 2025 and United Kingdom approval in 2026 (22-24). In China, CSPC Pharmaceutical Group reported that SYS6006, a domestically developed non-replicating mRNA COVID-19 vaccine, was included for emergency use in March 2023 (26). In India, Gennova developed the thermostable, lyophilized saRNA booster GEMCOVAC-OM, which received emergency authorization in June 2023 and was evaluated in a randomized phase 2/3 study (29). These routes differ in regulatory mechanism, storage requirements, manufacturing localization, and target population. They therefore provide a more useful implementation perspective than a company-by-company pipeline list.


### From rabies and influenza proof-of-concept to licensed respiratory vaccines
  Early human studies already showed that delivery could determine whether the same RNA concept succeeded clinically. In the first-in-human CV7201 rabies study, protective neutralizing titers were achieved in substantial proportions of participants when mRNA was delivered by needle-free devices, whereas conventional needle-syringe delivery of the same buffer-formulated vaccine was largely ineffective (33). Preclinical influenza studies similarly showed that sequence-defined RNA vaccines could be manufactured rapidly and induce protective responses across animal models (34). LNP-formulated modified mRNA subsequently produced robust immune responses against H10N8 and H7N9 influenza antigens in animals and humans (35). These programs established technical capabilities in antigen swapping, dose selection, formulation, and clinical assay development before the COVID-19 pandemic.

  Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) then provided large-scale validation. Structure-guided antigen design enabled rapid development of prefusion-stabilized spike vaccines (36). In pivotal trials, BNT162b2 and mRNA-1273 showed high efficacy against symptomatic COVID-19 during the initial pandemic period (37,38). The enduring importance of these trials is not the historical efficacy percentage alone; it is that they demonstrated that sequence selection, nucleoside-modified mRNA, LNP formulation, commercial manufacturing, lot release, pharmacovigilance, and global distribution could be operated as one integrated platform.

  The post-COVID portfolio now provides stronger evidence that the platform can be reused, but it also illustrates how regulatory conclusions can differ within a single labeled indication. For seasonal influenza, the phase 3 mRNA-1010 P304 trial randomized 40,805 adults aged 50 years or older; 40,703 received study vaccine or comparator and constituted the vaccinated safety population. Relative vaccine efficacy against the licensed standard-dose comparator was 26.6% [95% confidence interval (CI): 16.7–35.4%] (39). On 5 August 2026, the US FDA licensed mFLUSIVA through two regulatory pathways: traditional approval for adults aged 50–64 years based on clinical efficacy and supportive safety data, and accelerated approval for adults aged 65 years or older based primarily on immunogenicity against a preferentially recommended high-dose comparator, with a required postmarketing confirmatory comparative-efficacy study (27). For RSV, mRESVIA is licensed for adults aged 60 years or older and for adults aged 18–59 years who are at increased risk of lower respiratory tract disease caused by RSV (28). These decisions demonstrate regulatory maturation of the platform while also showing that evidentiary standards remain product-, age-, comparator-, and indication-specific. ## mRNA cancer immunotherapy

### From shared tumor antigens to individualized neoantigens
  Cancer vaccination places different demands on an RNA platform. Prophylactic infectious-disease vaccines usually target a predefined antigen shared across recipients, whereas a personalized cancer vaccine must identify tumor-specific targets, manufacture a patient-specific product, and generate effective T cells in a host with established disease or residual micro metastatic disease. Earlier mRNA programs targeted shared tumor-associated antigens and established the feasibility of dendritic-cell loading, direct injection, RNA lipoplexes, and combination immunotherapy (40,41). The field increasingly favors neoantigens arising from somatic alterations because they are absent from normal tissues and can reduce central tolerance, although only a fraction of mutations produces immunogenic, presented epitopes (42).

  A contemporary individualized workflow begins with tumor and matched-normal sequencing, transcript-level confirmation of expressed variants, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) typing, peptide-processing and HLA-binding prediction, immunogenicity ranking, and assembly of selected targets into a vaccine construct. The difficulty is not mutation detection but prioritization. A high mutational burden increases the candidate pool but does not guarantee that selected peptides will be expressed, processed, presented, recognized by available T cell clones, or retained as the tumor evolves. The practical endpoint of the computational pipeline is therefore a ranked set of biologically plausible targets rather than a definitive list of immunogenic neoantigens.


### Delivery routes and antigen-presenting-cell targeting
  Route of administration is part of the immunologic design of a cancer RNA vaccine. Early programs used intradermal or intranodal administration of naked or protamine-complexed mRNA and ex vivo electroporation of dendritic cells (DCs), approaches that placed antigen near professional antigen-presenting cells and established the feasibility of repeated therapeutic vaccination (40,41). Systemic intravenous RNA lipoplexes were later engineered to exploit charge-dependent biodistribution and target splenic DCs, coupling antigen delivery to antiviral innate sensing and T cell priming (19). Local and intratumoral approaches can concentrate antigen or immunomodulatory RNA within the tumor microenvironment, whereas intranasal delivery remains an experimental option for selected mucosal settings. These routes are not interchangeable: biodistribution, antigen-presenting-cell access, inflammatory context, feasible dose, and compatibility with checkpoint blockade or chemotherapy all differ. ### AI-assisted neoantigen prioritization
  AI is most useful when treated as an incremental improvement to this selection problem. Machine-learning and deep-learning models can integrate sequence context, HLA binding, antigen processing, expression, clonality, and immunopeptidomic information, potentially reducing false-positive candidates (43). They can also support RNA sequence optimization after antigen selection. However, model performance is constrained by training-set composition, HLA representation, assay heterogeneity, and incomplete labels for true human immunogenicity. AI should therefore not be described as independently “selecting” a clinically effective vaccine. Its defensible role is to rank candidates and focus experimental or clinical validation on a smaller, better-characterized set.


### Clinical evidence from 2023–2026
  The strongest randomized evidence for an individualized mRNA neoantigen vaccine combined with programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade comes from intismeran autogene (mRNA-4157/V940) plus pembrolizumab in resected high-risk melanoma. In the phase 2b KEYNOTE-942 trial, 157 patients were randomized 2:1 to the combination or pembrolizumab alone. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) favored the combination, with a hazard ratio (HR) for recurrence or death of 0.561 (95% CI: 0.309–1.017) and 18-month RFS of 79% versus 62% (44). At approximately 3 years, the combination continued to favor recurrence-free and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) (45). In the peer-reviewed 5-year update, the median planned follow-up was 60.3 months; the HR was 0.510 (95% CI: 0.294–0.887) for RFS and 0.411 (95% CI: 0.200–0.843) for DMFS, with a favorable but imprecise overall-survival trend and no new safety signal (46). On 19 August 2026, Merck and Moderna announced that the phase 3 INTerpath-001 trial met its primary RFS endpoint and a key secondary DMFS endpoint (30). Because full phase 3 effect estimates had not yet been presented in a peer-reviewed report at the search cutoff, this result is treated here as positive topline evidence rather than as a fully assessable published dataset.

  Positive PD-1-combination signals are not confined to individualized neoantigen programs. BNT111 is a fixed RNA-lipoplex vaccine encoding four shared melanoma-associated antigens rather than patient-specific neoantigens. In July 2024, BioNTech reported that a randomized phase 2 trial in advanced melanoma that had relapsed after or was refractory to PD-1 or programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) blockade met its primary efficacy endpoint for BNT111 plus cemiplimab, with a statistically significant improvement in overall response rate relative to a historical-control benchmark; both monotherapy arms also showed activity (47). Because full peer-reviewed trial results were not available by the 31 August 2026 search cutoff, this finding is treated here as company-reported topline evidence. Its relevance is comparative: it indicates that antigen strategy itself, shared tumor-associated antigens versus individualized neoantigens, is one source of heterogeneity across mRNA cancer-vaccine programs.

  Evidence from pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is mechanistically informative even though it is earlier phase and uses PD-L1 blockade rather than PD-1 blockade. In a phase 1 study, autogene cevumeran, an individualized uridine mRNA-lipoplex vaccine encoding up to 20 neoantigens, was given after surgery with atezolizumab and subsequently modified FOLFIRINOX (folinic acid, fluorouracil, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin). Eight of 16 vaccinated patients developed high-magnitude vaccine-induced T cell responses. Responders had a median RFS that was not reached, whereas non-responders had a median RFS of 13.4 months (48). Extended follow-up showed persistence of vaccine-expanded cluster of differentiation 8-positive (CD8+) T cell clones for years (49). These findings support biological durability but do not establish efficacy in a randomized comparison.

  The broader development record also argues for caution. Autogene cevumeran has not produced uniformly positive results across indications. In August 2026, BioNTech reported termination of a phase 2 study of autogene cevumeran monotherapy in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)-positive resected colorectal cancer after a data-monitoring recommendation based on futility and a numerical overall-survival imbalance; no new safety signal was identified (31). This outcome does not negate the PDAC findings, but it shows that individualized mRNA vaccination cannot be evaluated independently of tumor type, disease setting, co-therapy, and biological selection. Table 5 therefore separates randomized efficacy evidence, early-phase mechanistic evidence, and company-reported topline information rather than combining them into a single category of “positive trials”.


### Why response rates differ between personalized neoantigen vaccines
  Response heterogeneity begins with antigen strategy and antigen quality. Shared-antigen programs such as BNT111 test whether recurrent tumor-associated antigens can support a more standardized product, whereas individualized programs select mutation-derived targets for each patient (41,47). Within individualized vaccination, the PDAC study is particularly instructive because responders and non-responders had similar numbers of nonsynonymous mutations and vaccine-encoded neoantigens, whereas responders had more clonal tumors and immunogenicity correlated with neoantigen quality (48). Thus, mutation count alone is an incomplete predictor: the fraction of tumor cells carrying a target, peptide processing and presentation, non-self-recognition, and the available T cell repertoire may matter more than the absolute number of encoded mutations.

  A second determinant is antigen presentation. HLA genotype changes the space of peptides that can be displayed; defects in HLA expression, beta-2 microglobulin, antigen-processing machinery, or interferon signaling can reduce presentation after vaccination. Third, the host immune context matters. A vaccine can prime or expand neoantigen-specific T cells without ensuring their entry into a tumor that is depleted of antigen-presenting cells, enriched in suppressive myeloid cells, or dominated by exhausted T cell states. These barriers help explain why immunogenicity and clinical benefit may diverge (50,51).

  Disease setting and treatment sequence are equally relevant. The strongest melanoma data arise after complete resection, when tumor burden is low and pembrolizumab can relieve PD-1-mediated inhibition during expansion of vaccine-induced T cells. In advanced tumors, high antigen heterogeneity and immunosuppression create a less favorable environment. Manufacturing time introduces a further biological delay because the tumor can evolve between biopsy, sequence analysis, vaccine production, and dosing. A useful future response model will therefore need to combine antigen-level variables (clonality, expression, HLA presentation, immunogenicity), host variables (T cell repertoire and immune competence), tumor-microenvironment variables, and operational variables such as manufacturing interval. Current evidence does not support a single biomarker that captures all four levels.


## Cross-technology integration of mRNA vaccine platforms
  The most transferable contribution of infectious-disease mRNA development to cancer vaccination is not a specific antigen or immune endpoint but a set of platform capabilities. First, sequence-defined antigen manufacturing established that a common IVT and formulation process can accommodate frequent antigen changes. Seasonal influenza now extends this principle to planned strain updates while preserving the underlying LNP and manufacturing platform (27). Personalized oncology applies the same modularity at a smaller batch scale, replacing a population-level strain update with a patient-specific neoantigen set.

  Second, infectious-disease programs accelerated LNP development, analytical control, and understanding of reactogenicity. Those capabilities can shorten formulation development for cancer vaccines, but they cannot simply be copied without considering route and target cell. Intramuscular prophylactic vaccination often seeks robust systemic antibody and T cell responses, whereas cancer programs may require efficient dendritic-cell targeting, cytotoxic T cell priming, and repeated dosing in patients receiving checkpoint blockade or chemotherapy. Charge-tuned intravenous lipoplexes and other antigen-presenting-cell-directed systems illustrate why oncology may need to depart from the standard intramuscular LNP solution (16,19).

  Third, COVID-19 created mature quality systems for deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA)-template control, IVT, capping, impurity removal, LNP assembly, fill-finish operations, cold-chain management, and post-marketing safety surveillance. Economic modeling has shown that RNA dose is a major determinant of drug-substance scale and that low-dose saRNA can reduce upstream manufacturing requirements, while fill-finish can remain a bottleneck (52). These lessons are relevant to individualized cancer products because rapid release and predictable turnaround may influence whether a patient can receive the intended vaccine before recurrence. The transfer is therefore operational as well as molecular.

  Regulatory learning is also transferable, although only in part. Established assays for RNA identity, integrity, encapsulation, lipid composition, potency, sterility, and comparability can provide a starting framework for personalized lots. What cannot be transferred directly is the evidence standard for clinical benefit. Infectious-disease vaccines act against a shared pathogen in generally immunocompetent recipients; therapeutic cancer vaccines encounter heterogeneous tumors, immune escape, prior treatment, and a different benefit-risk context. Cross-technology integration should therefore reduce platform development time while preserving indication-specific biological validation.


## Manufacturing, stability, regulatory and safety considerations

### Manufacturing and quality control
  A typical manufacturing sequence includes plasmid template preparation, linearization, IVT, capping, poly(A) generation or template encoding, purification, concentration, buffer exchange, LNP formulation, sterile filtration, fill-finish, and release testing. The process is cell-free at the RNA synthesis stage and can therefore be standardized across antigens, but product quality is sensitive to transcript length, capping efficiency, residual DNA, truncated RNA, dsRNA, encapsulation efficiency, lipid impurities, particle size, and RNA integrity. Larger saRNA constructs and patient-specific multiepitope transcripts can place additional demands on transcription yield and purification. For this reason, platform claims of rapid manufacturing should be evaluated together with analytical release time rather than by transcription time alone (14,52).


### Stability and distribution
  RNA hydrolysis, lipid oxidation or hydrolysis, particle aggregation, leakage, and freeze-thaw stress can reduce potency during storage. Early COVID-19 products made these constraints visible because frozen distribution became part of the clinical technology rather than a background logistics issue. Formulation work has since improved storage flexibility, but stability remains product-specific and must be demonstrated for each RNA-lipid combination (53). For individualized cancer vaccines, stability interacts with turnaround time: longer shelf life is useful, but rapid manufacturing and administration may be more important than long-term stockpiling.


### Safety and regulatory interpretation
  Common vaccine reactions include injection-site pain, fatigue, headache, myalgia, chills, and fever. Rare adverse events associated with particular products require product-specific pharmacovigilance rather than assumptions about the entire RNA class. The same principle applies to LNP components and repeated exposure. For cancer vaccines, the acceptable benefit-risk balance differs from prophylactic vaccination, and adverse events must be separated from those caused by checkpoint inhibitors, chemotherapy, or other combined treatments. Regulatory evaluation is therefore increasingly platform-informed but remains product- and indication-specific.


## Strengths and limitations of this review
  This review integrates molecular engineering, RNA architecture, delivery, current regulatory status, infectious-disease translation, and individualized cancer immunotherapy within one platform framework. It also incorporates developments through 31 August 2026, including authorization of a self-amplifying vaccine, FDA approval of an mRNA influenza vaccine, long-term melanoma follow-up, positive phase 3 topline melanoma results, and the termination of a colorectal autogene cevumeran study. This combination reduces the risk of presenting technological maturity solely through older COVID-19 literature and allows positive and negative translational signals to be interpreted together (23,27,30,31).

  Several limitations remain. The narrative search was targeted rather than exhaustive and did not use formal risk-of-bias scoring. The evidence base is also uneven across platforms: conventional mRNA has extensive human data, saRNA has limited but regulatory-grade clinical experience, and circRNA evidence is predominantly preclinical. Oncology evidence is especially heterogeneous in antigen strategy, disease setting, combination therapy, and endpoints. The 5-year KEYNOTE-942 follow-up was available as a peer-reviewed report, whereas the phase 3 INTerpath-001 result and the colorectal autogene cevumeran trial termination were available only through company communications at the search cutoff. These constraints limit direct cross-platform efficacy comparisons and make mechanistic interpretation more reliable than numerical ranking of products.


## Conclusions
  mRNA vaccination has matured from a single transcript format into a family of related platforms. The central design problem is no longer whether synthetic RNA can function as a vaccine, but how RNA architecture, sequence and structure, purification, delivery, tissue targeting, and manufacturing should be matched to a clinical objective. Conventional nucleoside-modified mRNA-LNP has the deepest clinical validation; saRNA provides a clinically established dose-sparing alternative; circRNA remains promising but less mature. In infectious disease, reusable manufacturing and regulatory infrastructure now support vaccines beyond COVID-19. In cancer, individualized neoantigen vaccines have generated durable T cell responses and randomized evidence of benefit in resected melanoma, but inconsistent outcomes across patients and indications show that antigen quality, tumor biology, immune context, and treatment timing remain decisive. The next phase of the field will depend less on a universally “better” RNA format than on indication-specific integration of molecular design, delivery, biomarkers, and operationally realistic manufacturing.
