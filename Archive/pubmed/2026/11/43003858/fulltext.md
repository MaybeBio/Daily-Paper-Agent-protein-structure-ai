# Hydration water drives elastin’s entropic elasticity


## Abstract
  Elastin endows arteries, skin and lungs with elasticity, yet the molecular origin of its entropic recoil remains debated. Using multi-microsecond all-atom simulations of a representative elastomeric region of human tropoelastin, the monomeric precursor of elastin, we quantify protein conformational entropy, hydration-water entropy, and protein- and water-mediated interaction enthalpies during stretching. Protein conformational entropy decreases modestly overall, with heterogeneous local entropic responses governed by secondary structure and sequence context. In contrast, hydration water provides the dominant unfavorable entropic contribution because stretching recruits many water molecules from bulk into a persistently low-entropy first hydration layer. Stretching also disrupts favorable intra-protein interactions while strengthening protein–water interactions, producing opposing enthalpic contributions that largely compensate the entropic penalty. Together, these results support a hydration-coupled thermodynamic mechanism in which water is the dominant entropic contributor to elastin recoil within a strongly compensating enthalpy–entropy balance.


## Introduction
  Elastic tissues in vertebrates–including arterial, dermal, and pulmonary–derive their elasticity from elastin, a long-lived extracellular matrix protein with long-term durability and mechanical compliance1–6. Elastin’s ability to undergo billions of extension/contraction cycles with minimal permanent deformation is central to its physiological function as failure in these attributes can lead to ventricular hypertrophy and stroke6. With age, elastic tissues progressively become more rigid and less capable of recoiling, giving rise to the hypothesis that age-related degradation of elastin fibers may dictate the apparent 100–120-year limit on human life expectancy4.

  Despite the biological importance of its function, the molecular driving forces that power elastin’s elasticity have been controversial for over 50 years7–13. Nevertheless, several observations are known to be critical for its elasticity. First, elastin becomes elastic when hydrated, whereas dry elastin is brittle13–16. Second, several thermoelastic measurements have indicated that elastin recoil is largely entropically driven7,8,17,18. This entropic recoil has been attributed to elastin’s disordered molecular structure and highly hydrophobic amino acid composition, implicating both protein conformational entropy and hydration-water entropy as possible sources of the restoring force19. Within this entropic framework, the relaxed (free) state is expected to have higher entropy than the stretched state (Fig. 1a).   For over fifty years, the central dispute in this field has been whether the dominant entropic contribution to elastic recoil arises from the protein’s conformational entropy or from the hydration entropy of water12,13,19–22. Two mechanistic models shape the debate. Rubber-like elasticity models attribute recoil primarily to a loss of protein conformational entropy upon extension7–10,17,18,23,24 (Fig. 1b–c). Hydrophobic-hydration models attribute recoil to a loss of hydration-water entropy as extension exposes hydrophobic surfaces of elastin that are proposed to order surrounding water11–14,16,25 (Fig. 1d). In the hydrophobic-hydration model, this entropic penalty is coupled to favorable enthalpic changes and strong enthalpy–entropy compensation, thermodynamic signatures associated with increased solvent exposure of nonpolar surface area13.

  Across biophysics, these two entropy channels are invoked in various contexts. Conformational entropy has been measured for intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) during binding to folded partners, revealing how disorder enables responsive binding26; increases in conformational entropy upon binding can also stabilize IDPs without imposing global order27. During protein folding, conformational-entropy losses have been quantified and mapped at the residue and secondary-structure level, and a hydration-entropy contribution favoring folding is inferred28. Several studies have linked conformational entropy to sequence composition and local structure, for example, by explaining why Gly-rich regions relative to Ala-rich regions promote disorder29, developing sequence-only predictors of conformational entropy from triad (three-residue) motif entropies30, and quantifying the entropy penalty when intrinsically disordered regions locally order31. Conversely, changes in hydration entropy have been invoked to rationalize liquid–liquid phase separation of IDPs, highlighting cases where water entropy can make a major thermodynamic contribution relative to protein conformational entropy32,33. On the ribosome, nascent-chain expansion and increased hydration have been shown to lower both hydration and conformational entropies, destabilizing the unfolded ensemble and reducing the net folding-entropy penalty34. Hydration waters retained in deep protein pockets can gain entropy upon ligand binding, favoring association35, and spatial variation in entropy within biomolecular hydration layers has been linked to modulation of folding and binding. Some of these studies probe both entropy channels and make an explicit comparison; others probe a single channel. Collectively, the literature indicates that either entropy channel can dominate and that entropy changes can be coupled to compensating enthalpic contributions. This coupling is especially relevant for processes involving hydration of highly hydrophobic proteins and proteinaceous materials, motivating a direct comparison of protein- and water-mediated entropic and enthalpic changes underlying elastin’s elasticity.

  Historically, earlier work supported rubber-like elasticity models for elastin. Thermoelastic measurements by Hoeve and Flory on elastin swollen in glycol–water solvent pointed to a mainly entropic recoil, similar to an ideal elastomer such as rubber7. Based on these observations, they postulated that elastin’s structure is an isotropic, rubber-like polymer network consisting of crosslinked random chains7,8,23 (Fig. 1b). In their model, upon stretching, conformational entropy of the polypeptide chains is greatly reduced. Subsequent thermoelastic measurements of hydrated elastin across a broad temperature range have further supported a predominantly entropy-driven recoil centered on protein conformational changes10,17,18,24. Later, Urry and colleagues analyzed a β-spiral model of the elastin polypentapeptide (VPGVG)n by evaluating its accessible low-energy conformations. They showed that dynamic β-turn segments librate among many low-energy states in the relaxed state, whereas stretching suppresses these motions, producing a > 10-fold drop in conformational entropy10 (Fig. 1c).

  Challenging rubber-like elasticity, several groups have proposed models powered by hydrophobic hydration11,13,15,36,37. Pioneering this perspective, Weis-Fogh and Andersen showed that stretching elastin in aqueous phosphate buffer releases heat in excess of the mechanical work performed, suggesting that deformation perturbs elastin–water interactions rather than only a dry polymeric chain network36. The prevailing view in these studies is that stretching exposes highly hydrophobic surfaces of elastin. This exposure was proposed to order surrounding water and generate low-entropy water of hydrophobic hydration (Fig. 1d), making water a critical driver of elastin’s elasticity. In this framework, stretching-induced hydration-water entropy loss is partially compensated by favorable enthalpic changes, consistent with thermodynamic signatures of transferring a hydrophobic moiety from a nonpolar phase into water38. Recent nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and thermomechanical measurements on purified elastin fibers in water show a large entropy loss upon stretching, accompanied by a pronounced increase in the fraction of ordered hydration water and strong enthalpy–entropy compensation13, supporting a major role for hydration entropy in elastin recoil. However, because these thermomechanical analyses report system-level entropy and enthalpy changes for hydrated elastin, they cannot quantitatively separate protein- and water-mediated contributions, leaving their relative magnitudes unresolved13. To our knowledge, no study has provided an atomistic, direct, and quantitative comparison of conformational and hydration-water entropies alongside intra-protein and protein–water interaction enthalpies underlying elastin’s elasticity, nor resolved the molecular origins of the entropic contributions that have been debated for decades.

  Elastin’s elasticity arises from its structure, which is ultimately dictated by its monomeric precursor, tropoelastin19. Tropoelastin is largely disordered39, among the most hydrophobic proteins in nature13, and highly extensible, capable of stretching up to ~ 8 × its resting length without hysteresis40. Single-molecule atomic force microscopy force–extension measurements showed that monomeric tropoelastin behaves as a near-ideal entropic polymer spring40. It comprises alternating glycine- and proline-rich hydrophobic domains and lysine-bearing cross-linking domains (Fig. 2a)41. Three-dimensional organization of crosslinked tropoelastin monomers underlies the elastin polymer network5. Decades of studies have addressed tropoelastin assembly and the organization of the mature elastic fiber by heterogeneous crosslinks, yielding important insights into molecular structure, assembly, and fiber formation42–46. Nevertheless, the atomistic mechanisms that generate its elasticity remain unresolved.   Using our validated atomistic model of tropoelastin40,47 (Fig. 2b. i), we simulate the central domains (10–19), hereafter mini-tropoelastin (Fig. 2a, b. ii). Mini-tropoelastin (i) captures the alternating domain organization of tropoelastin; (ii) lies in the highly coiled and flexible region of tropoelastin expected to impart elasticity40,47; and (iii) is ~3 × smaller than the full-length monomer, enabling long-timescale all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) and more efficient conformational sampling. Since thermodynamic properties are state functions, independent simulations of mini-tropoelastin in the free (Fig. 2b. ii) and fully extended (Fig. 2b. iii) states define the endpoints needed to compute local and global entropic and enthalpic changes upon complete extension of this molecular system. The fully extended state serves as an upper-bound molecular reference for isolating the global and local structural and thermodynamic consequences of chain extension, rather than as a direct representation of physiological elastin deformation.

  In practice, conformational entropy is commonly measured from backbone and side-chain dihedral-angle distributions26–29,31,48, whereas hydration-water entropy is quantified from water molecules’ translational and rotational velocity spectra32,34,35,49. Protein- and water-mediated interaction enthalpies can be estimated from interaction-energy changes in MD simulations. We apply these approaches to resolve protein- and water-mediated entropic and enthalpic changes underlying elastin’s elasticity. Using multi-microsecond all-atom MD, we quantify protein conformational entropy28 (Fig. 2c. i) and hydration-water entropy50 (Fig. 2c. ii), enabling an atomistic, side-by-side comparison of their contributions and the molecular origins of their stretch-induced changes. We further quantify intra-protein and protein–water interaction enthalpies, placing these entropic changes within a thermodynamic decomposition of mini-tropoelastin stretching.

  Conformational entropy–dominated by the backbone component–exhibits a comparatively small global yet highly localized response to stretch, modulated by stable and labile secondary-structure elements and sequence context. These local features, rather than wholesale backbone ordering, govern the protein’s conformational response. Stretching induces a large hydration-entropy loss–arising from an increased fraction of ordered water molecules, while substantially retaining low molar water entropy in the hydration layer–that decisively outweighs the conformational-entropy decrease. Atomistic analysis of elastin’s hydration layer shows that stretching reorganizes hydration water, increasing water–water hydrogen bonding per water molecule, reducing water–protein hydrogen-bond coordination, and modestly accelerating rotational and translational water dynamics. Stretching mini-tropoelastin gives rise to large but opposing enthalpic contributions from protein and water that partially compensate each other. Upon extension, favorable intra-protein interactions are disrupted, leading to an unfavorable intra-protein enthalpic change. In contrast, exposure of hydrophobic residues to water yields a favorable protein–water enthalpic change. These competing contributions result in a net enthalpy change that favors stretching and largely compensates the entropic penalty, yielding a comparatively small net free-energy change. Together, our results define an atomistic, building-block mechanical signature that points to hydration water as the dominant entropic contributor to elastin recoil, operating within a compensating thermodynamic balance.

  Exploration of the accessible conformational ensemble in the free state is slow, as expected for a largely disordered protein with stable and labile secondary-structure elements. We recomputed conformational entropy over non-overlapping trajectory segments for blockwise analysis. In the free state, sampling uncertainty approaches a plateau for the largest blocks (~12 μs), suggesting that mini-tropoelastin explores distinct conformational sub-ensembles on ~10-μs timescales (Fig. S1a). In contrast, the fully extended state shows substantially lower sampling uncertainty and more stable entropy estimates across block sizes, indicating a restricted conformational ensemble for which the 4-μs trajectory provides adequate sampling (Fig. S1b). Thus, the uncertainty in the conformational-entropy change is dominated primarily by the free-state ensemble, motivating the longer sampling used for this state.

  Slow (μs–scale) excursions in the free state likely arise from labile secondary structures, particularly α-helices and β-sheets, which, on average, have ~ 14 J/mol-K-res lower conformational entropy than disordered regions29. We expect these excursions to persist in mini-tropoelastin over time due to its stable and labile secondary structures and largely disordered nature (Fig. S2–S5)19,52. Per-residue backbone entropy of mini-tropoelastin in the free state is approximately 13 J/mol-K-res higher than that of rigidly-folded ubiquitin in its native-state ensemble, both computed with a 10° dihedral bin size28. This large difference underscores the substantial conformational entropy reservoir in tropoelastin, which is absent from rigidly folded ubiquitin, and indicates that the free state remains a high-entropy disordered ensemble rather than a folded, low-entropy state. This conformational disorder has been proposed to be one of the critical molecular features underlying elastin’s elasticity19.

  Historically, elastin’s Gly/Val/Pro-rich hydrophobic domains–highly flexible and disordered–are thought to primarily confer elasticity, while Lys-bearing cross-linking domains have been suggested as structural modulators21,41,42. Our results align with that view but reveal that substantial backbone conformational entropic penalties upon extension are not confined to hydrophobic domains; they also occur in flexible cross-linking domains. Specifically, hydrophobic domain 11 and cross-linking domain 13 show large per-residue backbone entropic penalties, with additional positive penalties in domains 12 and 16 (Fig. 3c); these domains also exhibit high free-state backbone conformational entropies (Fig. 3d). These domains are highly disordered (Fig. S5), containing transient turns, loops, and coils, supporting the view that flexible, disordered regions store substantial backbone conformational entropy and resist extension entropically. Domain 17, containing the most persistent α-helix (Fig. S4a), shows a large backbone entropy gain upon extension rather than an entropy loss, owing to increased conformational freedom when this ordered structure is unraveled. Domain 18, the largest domain of mini-tropoelastin, is more ordered than the two other hydrophobic domains, 11 and 16, likely because of the long-lived β-sheet it forms with domain 15 (Fig. S4b), which may moderate its backbone entropic penalty. While unraveling ordered domains increases conformational entropy, the majority of the largely disordered mini-tropoelastin exhibits the opposite trend. Overall, the domain-level conformational-entropy response is highly heterogeneous across mini-tropoelastin and is strongly shaped by local secondary-structure elements and their stability.

  Tropoelastin’s amino acid sequence has low complexity19: in mini-tropoelastin, 83% of residues are Gly/Ala/Pro/Val/Lys (Fig. 4a). Gly enhances backbone flexibility26,29,42, Pro disrupts secondary structures55, and Val’s high hydropathy promotes burial/turns55. Ala, particularly when repeated, promotes α-helix formation29,47,56, while Lys provides cross-linking19. In the hydrophobic domains, Gly, Pro, and Val residues are often found in tandem and pseudo-repeat sequences–including 2- and 3-residue motifs of GX, PX, GGX, and PGX, with X being mostly Gly, Ala, and Val19. In particular, Pro-bearing motifs (e.g., PGX/VPG) are suggested to maintain conformational flexibility and elevated backbone hydration critical for elasticity57,58. We find that Gly and Gly/Ala-containing motifs generally show large backbone entropic penalties upon extension.   In both free and fully extended states, prolines on average exhibit the lowest backbone conformational entropy per residue (Fig. 4a)–apart from alanines in stable α-helices–because their cyclic side chains restrict backbone dihedral angles (Fig. 4b. i). Alanines in the stable α-helix of domain 17 have a backbone entropy of ~17.4 J/mol-K-res, in close agreement with α-helical residues in ubiquitin (~17 J/mol-K-res)28 (Fig. 4a, b. ii). Prolines show only a small backbone entropic response upon extension, consistent with their intrinsically restricted Ramachandran space. Glycines, abundant in disordered regions, are the most flexible residues due to their lack of side chains (Fig. 4a), as reflected in their ability to sample four distinct and wide conformational basins in Ramachandran space (Fig. 4b. iii). On average, glycines lose substantial backbone conformational freedom upon extension. However, sequence context modulates the flexibility of glycines: those following prolines are more flexible (SBB~39.9 J/mol-K-res; Fig. 4b. iii) than those preceding prolines (SBB~32.4 J/mol-K-res; Fig. 4b. iv). This context dependence reflects steric and torsional constraints imposed by the proline ring on neighboring backbone dihedrals. Backbone entropy distributions for each residue type are notably broader in the free state than in the fully extended state (Fig. 4a), reflecting the influence of stable and labile secondary structures in the free state that are absent in the fully extended state.

  Poly-Ala motifs (AA and AAA) of elastin exhibit a conformational entropy gain upon unraveling, reflecting their participation in α-helices (Fig. 4c, d). The presence of Lys in Ala-rich motifs modulates this response, consistent with greater local flexibility relative to uninterrupted poly-Ala segments. Two-residue motifs KA and AK and three-residue motifs AAK and AKA incur only small changes in backbone entropy, unlike AA and AAA motifs. Two-residue motifs of elastin polypentapeptide sequences (PG, GV, and VG) incur moderate backbone entropy losses of approximately 0.8–1.2 kJ/mol-res upon stretching. While PG motifs lose substantial backbone entropy, GP motifs instead gain backbone entropy, behaving more similarly to ordered AA motifs than to PG motifs, likely because the following Pro restricts Gly. Similarly, VP polypentapeptide motifs lose only ~0.24 kJ/mol-res. Among two-residue motifs, GA shows the highest mean backbone-entropy loss, while several other Gly-containing hydrophobic motifs, including PG, GV, AG, and VG, show moderate and broadly comparable losses. Three-residue motifs of elastin polypentapeptide sequences (PGV, VPG, GVP, and GVG) lose moderate backbone entropy, ~0.8–1.1 kJ/mol-res. The largest losses are observed for PGA and GAG motifs, which reach ~2.0 and ~1.7 kJ/mol-res, respectively. Alanines are stable when clustered with other alanines, for example, in poly-Ala cross-linking domains (Fig. 4a, b. ii), but become flexible in disordered segments of elastin, such as near glycines in hydrophobic domains. Thus, canonical polypentapeptide motifs contribute to the entropy loss, but the Gly/Ala-rich motifs prevalent in hydrophobic domains of mini-tropoelastin (Fig. 2a) show the strongest conformational-entropy losses upon unraveling and therefore provide the largest conformational-entropic resistance to extension.

  Self-assembling elastomeric proteins—including tropoelastin, major ampullate spindroin 2, flagelliform silk, and abductin—provide extensibility and mechanical function in tissues and extracellular proteinaceous fibers and are enriched in Gly/Ala—and Pro/Gly—containing motifs59–63. A shared feature of these proteins is that they lie above a combined Pro/Gly composition threshold57. Above this threshold, the combined abundance of conformationally rigid prolines and highly flexible glycines prevents formation of a compact, water-excluding core, favoring aggregated domains that remain highly hydrated and disordered—a key requirement for elastomeric proteins57. By contrast, more rigid, amyloid-like proteins such as aciniform silk and tubuliform silk, which primarily serve as tough protective fibers, fall below this threshold and contain fewer Gly/Ala—and Pro/Gly—containing motifs, consistent with their stiff, less extensible behavior64,65. The elastomeric proteins described above are enriched in these sequence features, including PGX-type repeats and Gly/Ala-containing motifs such as PGA and GAG in tropoelastin, which our calculations identify as incurring large backbone-entropy losses upon stretching, whereas the protective proteins contain these motifs more sparingly. Clusters of small flexible residues may therefore serve as local conformational-entropy hotspots, while interspersed prolines frustrate amyloid-like packing and promote disordered and flexible protein assemblies that support elastomeric behavior.

  Variations in the per-rotamer side-chain entropies are more modest than the backbone-entropy trends. Lysine and tyrosine side-chain rotamers are among the most flexible in both states, while rotamers of leucine and a subset of valine residues, particularly those preceding prolines, are among the most restricted (Fig. 4e). Lysine and tyrosine are hydrophilic amino acids (Kyte–Doolittle hydropathy scores of −3.9 and −1.3, respectively) compared with the hydrophobic leucine and valine (3.8 and 4.2)55. Hydrophilic residues are typically more solvent-exposed than hydrophobic ones, and their greater exposure to fluctuating hydration environments may contribute to enhanced side-chain flexibility. The emergence of two clearly separated valine side-chain entropy populations in the fully extended state (Fig. 4e) further suggests that local sequence context, especially neighboring prolines, modulates not only the backbone but also the side-chain motions of nearby residues.

  Due to its largely disordered nature, tropoelastin is best described as an ensemble with both stable and labile secondary structures–not a single conformation–consistent with its large conformational-entropy reservoir and elastic function19 (Fig. 4f). Unraveling induces conformational entropy changes in both hydrophobic and cross-linking domains, with the local response modulated by secondary-structure content, structural stability, and sequence chemistry. The most disordered and flexible segments, including largely disordered domains, Gly-rich regions, and Gly/Ala motifs, provide strong conformational-entropic resistance to extension, with motif chemistry shaping these local responses. Overall, protein segments pay entropic costs in proportion to the conformational entropy they store before stretching (Fig. 4f).

Despite modest burial (0.47), domain 13 ( ~ 82% disordered; Fig. S5) shows a comparatively large hydration-entropic penalty relative to other low-burial domains, indicating that burial captures a major, but not exclusive, determinant of the domain-level response. Although hydrophobic domains incur substantial hydration-entropic penalties, several cross-linking domains–whether helical (stable/labile) or largely disordered–also rank among the largest contributors. These penalties are therefore not confined to hydrophobic domains and correlate strongly with the solvent exposure gained upon unraveling. Notably, the per-residue hydration-entropic penalty in every domain (Fig. 5c) is positive and exceeds the corresponding per-residue conformational-entropic changes in magnitude (Fig. 3c).

  Similar water-centric physics appears in other contexts: hydration entropy and interfacial restructuring contribute to condensate formation in elastin and IDPs. Upon condensation, some low-entropy hydration water molecules near proteins are expelled to a bulk-like dilute phase, where they regain higher translational and rotational entropy, whereas water molecules retained inside the condensate remain confined and have low molar entropies. Upon stretching elastin, related trends appear in the opposite direction: many bulk water molecules are recruited into the FHL, which remains decisively lower in entropy than bulk. LLPS concentrates protein chains, which has been shown to reduce water–water H-bonding and tetrahedral order and increase protein–water contacts. Stretching induces opposite structural rearrangements at the interface: with limited protein H-bonding sites, recruited water molecules preferentially form more water–water and fewer water–protein H-bonds, and tetrahedrality rises. Together, these parallel–but oppositely directed–trends in hydration-water entropy and structure show that LLPS and elastin’s elasticity share related water-reorganization physics, with hydration-water entropy being a major contributor in both processes.

### Thermodynamic summary
  Nearly the entire protein-wide conformational entropic penalty of mini-tropoelastin arises from the backbone, with minimal side-chain contribution (Fig. 7a). This is consistent with NMR studies on ELPs and elastin fibers showing that elastin’s backbone is largely disordered and highly flexible12,53,54,70, supporting our finding of a large conformational-entropy reservoir that is partially lost upon stretching. In contrast to folded globular proteins such as ubiquitin, which lose substantial conformational entropy upon folding28, mini-tropoelastin retains high conformational entropy in its relaxed state and shows a highly heterogeneous conformational-entropic response to extension.   Considering the entropic changes alone supports the hydrophobic-hydration view of elastin recoil: at the protein-wide level, both protein conformational entropy and hydration-water entropy oppose extension, but hydration water provides the dominant unfavorable entropic contribution. In this study, we primarily focus on the entropic terms because the long-standing mechanistic dispute in elastin elasticity has centered on the origin of the entropic contributions and how local protein- and water-mediated entropic changes arise at the molecular level. However, early calorimetry and thermomechanical studies of elastin stretching in water revealed large enthalpic changes, implicating important elastin–water interactions and motivating hydrophobic-hydration models over rubber-like elasticity models. We therefore examined whether the dominant unfavorable entropic contribution from hydration water is coupled to compensating interaction enthalpies.

  The thermodynamic decomposition shows that this entropic penalty is embedded in a compensating enthalpy–entropy balance (Fig. 7a, b). Stretching disrupts favorable protein–protein contacts, giving an unfavorable intra–protein enthalpic contribution of +20.50± 0.48+20.50±0.48 kJ/mol-res, while exposure of hydrophobic residues strengthens protein–FHL water interactions, giving a favorable protein–FHL water enthalpic contribution of -28.98± 0.87−28.98±0.87 kJ/mol-res. This atomistic picture is consistent with thermomechanical measurements of hydrated elastin fibers, which show strong enthalpy–entropy compensation during stretching, a hallmark of the hydrophobic-hydration mechanism13. Our results provide a molecular decomposition of protein- and water-mediated contributions and identify the molecular-level origins of the entropic changes associated with elastin stretching, which are difficult to resolve experimentally and have been debated for decades.


### Implications, limitations, and outlook
  In this study, we provide an atomistic biophysical framework that quantifies protein- and water-mediated entropic and enthalpic contributions to elastin stretching and identifies hydration water as the dominant unfavorable entropic contributor within a compensating thermodynamic balance.

  Several limitations define the scope of our work. First, the fully extended mini-tropoelastin state should be interpreted as an upper-bound molecular reference state rather than a direct representation of physiological tissue deformation. In native elastic tissue, individual tropoelastin chains are cross-linked into a polymer network and embedded within a complex extracellular matrix; they are unlikely to become fully extended or fully solvated as isolated monomers. Nevertheless, single-molecule AFM experiments showed that full-length human tropoelastin can be stretched close to its estimated contour length while retaining worm-like-chain behavior and minimal hysteresis, supporting the use of an extended reference state to isolate the protein-wide and local molecular consequences of chain extension40. Within this framework, mini-tropoelastin serves as a sequence-encoded building-block model for identifying hydration-coupled thermodynamic signatures that can later be tested in more native-like cross-linked elastin systems.

  Second, the absolute water-entropy values obtained from the 2PT method depend on the chosen water model. In this study, we used the CHARMM36m protein force field with the CHARMM-modified TIP3P water model because this combination is widely used for folded and intrinsically disordered proteins71. Different water models give different absolute bulk-water entropies, reflecting their known differences in water structure, diffusion, and hydrogen-bond coordination50. While absolute entropy values are useful for benchmarking and necessary for estimating hydration-entropy changes, our conclusions rely primarily on excess hydration entropies, which measure how much the entropy of water molecules hydrating the protein is penalized relative to bulk water. A systematic comparison of excess hydration entropies, the resulting hydration-entropy changes, and other thermodynamic properties across different water models would be valuable, but is beyond the scope of the present study.

  Third, bulk water is used here as a well-defined thermodynamic reference, not as a complete representation of all water reservoirs present in native elastic tissue. Hydrated elastin fibers contain multiple dynamically distinct water populations, including bulk-like external water, intrafibrillar water, and more restricted elastin-associated water66. Because our hydration-entropy estimate treats newly recruited FHL waters as being drawn from a bulk-water reference, using a lower-entropy biological reference-water reservoir would reduce the absolute magnitude of the hydration-entropy penalty, especially because stretching substantially increases the FHL water population. However, this would not alter the qualitative conclusion for the present model: the hydration-water entropic penalty remains substantially larger than the protein conformational entropic penalty.

  The framework presented here provides a transferable foundation for bioinspired material design and future studies of elastin mechanics in tissues. There is growing interest in recombinant elastin-like polypeptides72–74 and elastin domain-derived proteins75 that mimic elastin’s elasticity and resilience for biocompatible, mechanically tunable applications in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. Our results may inform design by tuning amino acid sequence, domain organization, and hydration to program mechanics. In wet environments, designs could adjust the balance between flexible/disordered domains and motifs (e.g., Gly/Ala motifs) and less-flexible ones (e.g., helical cross-linking domains or poly-Ala motifs) to modulate local and global extensibility. For dry or low-humidity devices, such as wearable stretchable electronics and coatings, water’s essential role may be emulated with non-volatile polyols (e.g., glycerol, low-MW PEG, sorbitol) that hydrogen-bond to the backbone and help create a quasi-hydration layer, potentially helping to preserve entropic recoil76–79. Such additives can also impart application-specific advantages, including improved conductivity or freeze resistance76,77, enabling elastin-inspired materials that remain soft, resilient, and functional across diverse environments.

  Beyond materials design, application of this framework to native elastic tissues entails additional layers of complexity. In tissue, tropoelastin monomers are cross-linked into a polymer network19, interact with multiple components of the extracellular matrix (e.g., fibrillin microfibrils, lysyl-oxidase, proteoglycans, and collagens)43,80, and undergo post-translational and age-related chemical modifications (e.g., carbamylation81, lipid peroxidation82, and advanced glycation83). Developing atomistic models of native-like cross-linked elastin with experimentally consistent cross-link chemistry and density is therefore an important next step. The present work establishes the molecular thermodynamic signature of the monomeric elastin building block, providing a foundation for future studies of how cross-linking, tissue-level confinement, solvent conditions, extracellular-matrix interactions, and age-related modifications reshape the protein–water thermodynamic balance underlying elastin function in native tissues.


## Methods

### Molecular dynamics simulations of mini-tropoelastin
  We simulated the central domains of human tropoelastin (domains 10–19; referred to as mini-tropoelastin), comprising 225 amino acid residues and 2840 protein atoms in total. The initial atomic coordinates for mini-tropoelastin were extracted from the tropoelastin structure of ref. 47. All protein parameters were taken from the CHARMM36m force field71. Standard protonation states were used for the protein: Lys and Arg side chains were protonated, the single Glu side chain was deprotonated, and the N- and C-termini were modeled in their charged NH3+ and COO- forms, respectively, yielding a net protein charge of +13e. Systems were solvated with the CHARMM-modified TIP3P water model71,84 and neutralized with 13 chloride ions to achieve electroneutrality.

  Two conformational states of mini-tropoelastin were studied: (i) the free state and (ii) the fully extended state. Because entropy and free energy are state functions, the thermodynamic changes considered here depend on the initial and final states. The free state models mini-tropoelastin in the absence of mechanical forcing/stress, while in equilibrium with water (Fig. 2b. ii). The fully extended state serves as an upper-bound molecular reference state for the completely unraveled chain, with no discernible secondary structures remaining and with the protein surface maximally exposed to water (Fig. 2b. iii). This reference state was used to isolate the structural and thermodynamic consequences of chain extension at the molecular level. For the initial structure of the fully extended state, we confirmed that the backbone covalent bonds were not stretched beyond their equilibrium lengths.

  The free state used a cubic periodic box (13.63 × 13.63 × 13.63 nm3) with 85,031 water molecules and the fully extended state used a cuboid periodic box (6 × 6 × 84 nm3) with 97,246 water molecules (Supplementary Table 1). Pre-production stages for both states were carried out on our local HPC with GROMACS 2024.4 software85. Both systems were energy-minimized by steepest descent until the maximum force was less than 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−1. Equilibration stages comprised 10 ns NVT and 10 ns NPT with positional restraints on protein heavy atoms (1000 kJ mol−1 nm−2), followed by an additional 10 ns NPT without restraints for the free state and with only the terminal C α atoms restrained for the fully extended state. Temperature was controlled with the velocity-rescale thermostat (300 K, time constant: 0.1 ps)86, and pressure with the Parrinello–Rahman barostat (1 bar, time constant: 2 ps)87. Dynamics were propagated using the leap-frog integrator with a time step of 1 fs88. The LINCS algorithm was used to constrain all covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms85. Long-range electrostatic interactions were computed using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method with a 0.16 nm grid spacing and fourth-order spline interpolation86. A cutoff distance of 1.2 nm was used for Lennard-Jones interactions, and the neighbor list was updated every 40 fs.

  In the production stage of the free state, none of the protein atoms were restrained, whereas in the production stage of the fully extended state, only the C α atoms of the N- and C-termini of the protein were restrained using a force constant of 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−2 in three directions. The production stage of the free state was run on the Anton 3 supercomputer89,90 for a total duration of 60 μs in the NPT ensemble. This trajectory length was chosen to ensure adequate sampling and to resolve the temporal evolution of a key observable, the conformational entropy of the protein, in the free state (Fig. S1a). Achieving an equivalent duration on our local HPC was impractical given performance constraints. Due to the Anton 3 box-shape constraints89,90, the production stage of the fully extended state was not run on Anton 3 but on our local HPC. Simulating the fully extended state on Anton 3 would require an Anton-compatible cubic box of ~84 nm on each side, containing approximately 2.0×107 water molecules, which was computationally prohibitive. Therefore, the production stage of the fully extended state was run on our local HPC for a total duration of 4 μs in the NPT ensemble. This trajectory length was sufficient to achieve adequate sampling of the fully extended state (Fig. S1b). For this state, all settings from the equilibration stages were kept the same except that the integration time step was updated to 2 fs.

  The free state’s production stage was run with Anton’s default settings89,90: a RESPA integrator with a time step of 2.5 fs, an antithetic thermostat with a time constant of 0.12 ps and a target temperature of 300 K, and an isotropic barostat with a time constant of 1.2 ps and a target pressure of 1 bar91. Electrostatics were treated with the Gaussian split Ewald (GSE) method92 with a cutoff of 1.24 nm and a specified k-space grid of 323, which was chosen to match the PME accuracy of GROMACS. Covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained with the M-SHAKE algorithm93. For the free state, trajectories were saved at Anton-default output intervals: protein-only trajectories were saved every 120 ps for conformational-entropy and secondary-structure analyses, whereas solvated trajectories were saved every 1.2 ns for interaction-energy analyses. For the fully extended state, both protein-only and solvated trajectories were saved every 100 ps and used for conformational-entropy and interaction-energy analyses.

### Definition of the first hydration layer
  Water molecules whose oxygen atoms lie within 4.1 Å of any protein atom were assigned to the first hydration layer (FHL; Fig. 2c. ii. a). This cutoff was derived from the radial distribution functions of water-oxygen atoms relative to protein atoms in both the free and fully extended states (Fig. S6). Under this definition, the FHL comprises (i) waters directly hydrogen-bonded to the protein and (ii) near-surface waters not hydrogen-bonded to the protein.

### MD simulations to obtain the 2PT-derived water entropies
  The 2PT method requires femtosecond-scale sampling of positions and velocities to resolve water’s high-frequency modes, and 2PT-derived entropies typically converge within ≲20 ps32,50. Our μs-scale production simulations saved at ≥0.1 ns intervals are unnecessarily long for 2PT and temporally too coarse. Thus, for both the free and fully extended states, we ran five independent 20-ps simulations starting from five distinct protein and water conformations extracted from the corresponding μs-production runs85. These 2PT simulations used the same box geometries and approximately the same numbers of water molecules as the μs-scale production simulations: a ~ 13.6-nm cubic box containing ~85,000 water molecules for the free state and a ~ 6 × 6 × 84-nm cuboid box containing ~97,200 water molecules for the fully extended state. In the DoSPT implementation of the 2PT method, NVT conditions are recommended: pressure coupling can corrupt VACFs and yield unrealistic entropies94,95. The velocity–Verlet integrator is preferred over leap-frog because leap-frog velocities are offset by half a time step relative to the saved atomic positions94,95. During the 2PT runs, the barostat was disabled and the thermostat retained (NVT). Dynamics were propagated with the velocity-Verlet integrator (time step 2 fs), and positions and velocities were saved every 4 fs for both states, consistent with previous studies using the 2PT method to calculate hydration entropy of proteins32,34. During these short entropy simulations, all protein atoms were position-restrained with a force constant of 50,000 kJ mol−1 nm−2 to isolate local water dynamics around representative, essentially fixed protein conformations. Water molecules that remained in the FHL for the entire 20-ps simulation were selected to compute the average molar entropy within the FHL, following ref. 34. Entropies of neat water, which served as the bulk reference, were derived from five independent control simulations of neat CHARMM-modified TIP3P water in a cubic box of ~5 nm, resulting in 4055 water molecules. In addition, bulk-like waters in the protein-containing systems were identified as water molecules that remained at least 1.0 nm away from the protein throughout the 20-ps entropy simulations; these bulk-like waters were used to validate that the protein-containing boxes recovered neat-water entropies away from the protein surface (Fig. S7). We further verified that the protein-wide hydration-entropy penalty is insensitive to whether neat-water or protein-box bulk-like water is used as the reference state (Fig. S9) and remains robust across reasonable variations in FHL cutoff (Fig. S10).


### Spatially resolved 3D-2PT analysis
  To independently validate the FHL entropy trends obtained from the classical 2PT/DoSPT workflow, we also implemented the grid-based, spatially resolved extension of the 2PT method, 3D-2PT, developed by Heyden et al.99, to estimate the excess FHL entropies in the free and fully extended states (Fig. S8). The same short entropy trajectories used for the classical 2PT analysis were also analyzed using 3D-2PT. The entire simulation volume was discretized into ~1 Å voxels, and local translational and rotational water entropies were computed from voxel-resolved velocity correlations following the 3D-2PT framework. FHL voxels were defined as those within 0.41 nm of the protein surface, whereas bulk-like voxels were defined as those at least 1.0 nm from the protein. Regional FHL and bulk-like entropies were calculated as water-number-density-weighted averages over voxels with nonzero translational, rotational, and total entropy values. Excess FHL entropies were then computed relative to the corresponding bulk-like water reference. The 3D-2PT analysis was used as a spatially resolved validation of the hydration-entropy trends, whereas the classical 2PT/DoSPT workflow was retained as the primary quantitative basis for the reported hydration-entropy changes.


### Interaction energy and estimation of enthalpic contributions
  Enthalpic contributions to mini-tropoelastin stretching were estimated from interaction-energy changes between the free and fully extended states. Because the simulated systems are nearly incompressible, the pΔ VpΔV contribution to the enthalpy change was neglected, and enthalpic changes were approximated by the corresponding changes in force-field interaction energies. Interaction energies were obtained by post-processing the μs-scale production trajectories using the mdrun -rerun routine implemented in GROMACS85.

  The protein–protein enthalpic contribution was calculated from intra-protein interaction energies because the free and fully extended systems contain a single mini-tropoelastin molecule. For this term, Coulomb and van der Waals cutoffs were increased to 2.8 nm in the rerun calculations. This cutoff was chosen to include longer-range intra-protein interactions while remaining compatible with the narrow ( ~ 6 nm) width of the fully extended simulation box.

  Because the hydration-entropy analysis is defined for first-hydration-layer (FHL) water, the protein–water enthalpic contribution was calculated for the same spatially defined water population. At each trajectory frame, FHL water molecules were selected dynamically as waters whose oxygen atoms were within 0.41 nm of any protein atom. The protein–FHL water interaction energy was calculated as the sum of short-range Coulomb and Lennard-Jones interaction energies between the protein and FHL water molecules. For this local protein–FHL water term, a 1.2-nm nonbonded cutoff was used to obtain an enthalpic counterpart spatially matched to the 0.41-nm FHL hydration-entropy calculation, while avoiding contributions from more distant waters outside the corresponding hydration-entropy definition. Both enthalpic changes were calculated from five non-overlapping trajectory blocks and reported as extended-minus-free interaction energy differences.

### Error analysis and statistical testing
  For conformational entropy, values used for the main analysis were computed from the full retained trajectories to maximize sampling of the dihedral-angle probability distributions: the final 59 μs of the 60-μs free-state trajectory and the 4-μs fully extended trajectory. Sampling uncertainties for conformational-entropy changes were estimated from five non-overlapping blockwise entropy-difference calculations and reported as the standard error of the mean (s.e.m.). For enthalpic changes, reported values correspond to the mean of five non-overlapping blockwise extended-minus-free interaction-energy differences, and uncertainties correspond to the blockwise s.e.m. For hydration-water entropy quantities, reported values correspond to the mean across five independent 20-ps 2PT simulations, and uncertainties correspond to the s.e.m. across simulations. Statistical significance was assessed using two-sided Welch’s unequal-variance t-tests only for selected comparisons. For combined thermodynamic quantities and free-energy changes, uncertainties were propagated in quadrature from the corresponding component s.e.m. values.
