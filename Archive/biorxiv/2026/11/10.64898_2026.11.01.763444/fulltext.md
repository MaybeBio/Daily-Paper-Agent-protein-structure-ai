## Abstract

Recent advances in protein language models, geometric deep learning, and structure prediction frameworks have the potential to accelerate computational drug discovery. However, it remains unclear how well these methods generalize beyond their training sets. Mutation-dependent drug responses present a challenge to these methods because there are often large effects from relatively small perturbations. Here, we systematically benchmark diverse mutational effect and drug-target affinity prediction models using deep mutational scanning datasets spanning 11 inhibitor conditions for the MET kinase domain. Across both mutational effect prediction and drug–target affinity tasks, current AI models produce largely ligand-invariant predictions despite experimentally distinct inhibitor-dependent fitness landscapes. Ligand-aware geometric models, including LigandMPNN and LASErMPNN, partially recover experimentally validated resistance-associated hotspots, highlighting the importance of explicitly modeling protein–ligand interactions and local structural flexibility. In contrast, deep learning affinity prediction models underestimate mutation-induced changes in binding affinity. This deficiency likely reflects mutation-invariant structural priors derived from co-folded structure prediction frameworks and the absence of mutation-conditioned supervision in current affinity datasets. Finally, we further extended our analysis to several kinase-inhibitor deep mutational scanning datasets and confirm the overall trends. Our findings suggest that a primary limitation in improving performance on this task lies in the scarcity of mutation-conditioned protein-ligand training data rather than model architecture alone.


## Introduction

Computational approaches can substantially reduce the experimental burden of early-stage drug discovery by enabling in silico prediction of protein–ligand binding affinities. (Viet-Khoa Tran-Nguyen 2025; Walters and Murcko 2020 ; Schneider 2018 ; Vamathevan et al. 2019 ) Computer-aided drug design (CADD) has contributed to the development of therapeutics by providing atomistic insights into protein-ligand interactions, which can help rationalize structure-activity relationships (SAR) and guide the design of improved compounds (W. Yu and MacKerell 2017 ). A range of physics-based computational methodologies are widely employed to characterize protein-ligand interactions. These methods span molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, rigorous free energy methods such as free energy perturbation (FEP) ( Zwanzig 1954 ; L. Wang et al. 2012 ) and thermodynamic integration (TI) ( Kirkwood 1935 ; den Otter and Briels 1998 ), enhanced sampling techniques such as metadynamics ( Laio and Parrinello 2002 ) and umbrella sampling ( Torrie and Valleau 1977 ), as well as approximate end-point approaches (e.g., MM-PBSA/GBSA) ( Genheden and Ryde 2015 ). In addition, molecular docking methods ( Shoichet et al. 2002 ) are commonly used for rapid screening and pose prediction, although their scoring functions generally provide only approximate estimates of binding affinity.

However, physics-based methods face several inherent limitations. In particular, MD simulations require sequential integration of Newton’s equations of motion, making them computationally expensive and difficult to scale. Their predictive accuracy also depends on factors such as force-field quality and accurate treatment of entropic contributions, both of which remain challenging. Advances in machine learning and computing hardware have enabled data-driven approaches to emerge as powerful alternatives. Classical machine learning models, including neural networks ( Jain et al. 1996 ), random forests ( Breiman 2001 ; Ballester and Mitchell 2010 ), and gradient-boosted trees ( Chen and Guestrin 2016 ; J. Lu et al. 2019 ) have been widely applied to predict binding affinities and related biochemical properties from knowledge-based hand-crafted features defined a priori . ( Rodrigues et al. 2021 ; Sun et al. 2021 ; Singh et al. 2025 ; Ballester and Mitchell 2010 ; Zilian and Sotriffer 2013 ; Svetnik et al. 2003 ; Durrant and McCammon 2011) More recently, deep learning (DL) methods have significantly advanced the related field by enabling high-throughput prediction and design across structural biology. These include protein structure prediction ( Jumper et al. 2021 )), mutational effect prediction using protein language models (C. Wang and Lu 2023 ; Lin et al. 2023 ; Bhatnagar et al. 2025 ; Frazer et al. 2021 ; Hesslow et al. 2022 ), drug-target affinity predictions ( Jiang et al. 2026 ; R. Zhang et al. 2025 ; López-Ríos de Castro et al. 2026 ), and generative protein ( Dieckhaus et al. 2024 ; Justas Dauparas et al. 2025 ; Fry et al. 2025 ; Birnbaum and Keating 2026 ; Ingraham et al. 2019 ; J. Dauparas et al. 2022 ; Ding et al. 2022 ; Scutteri et al. 2025 ; Kortemme 2024 ; Nijkamp et al. 2023 ) and small-molecule ( Mistryukova et al. 2025 ; Özçelik et al. 2025 ; Atz et al. 2024 ; Xing et al. 2026 ; Bennett et al. 2023 ; Cremer et al. 2025 ; Dunn and Koes 2026 ; Sumita et al. 2026 ) design. In particular, AlphaFold ( Jumper et al. 2021 ) and related structure prediction frameworks ( Discovery et al. 2024 ; Passaro et al. 2025 ; Y. Zhang et al. 2026 ; Butcher et al. 2025 ; Krishna et al. 2024 ; L. Liu et al. 2024 ; Candido et al. 2026 ) have been extended to predict protein complexes and, more recently, protein-ligand co-folded structures, providing valuable structural priors for downstream CADD applications. These advances are especially impactful, as many computational drug discovery pipelines critically depend on accurate starting structures for downstream prediction of protein–ligand interactions.

Despite this success, accurately predicting the effects of single amino acid substitutions remains challenging in part because these perturbations induce subtle local changes that are underrepresented during pretraining ( Feldman and Skolnick 2026 ; Huynh et al. 2026). Protein language models (pLMs), particularly single-sequence pLMs, offer an attractive alternative for modeling mutational effects at scale. ( Elnaggar et al. 2020 ; Brandes et al. 2022 ; Bepler and Berger 2019 ; Ferruz et al. 2023 ) Among these, the Evolutionary Scale Modeling (ESM) ( Lin et al. 2023 ; Hayes et al. 2025 ; Rives et al. 2021 ; Candido et al. 2026 ) family has demonstrated strong performance in zero-shot prediction of residue-level mutational effects, typically using log-likelihood ratio between wild-type and mutant sequences. The resulting representations have also proven effective for supervised transfer learning across a wide range of downstream tasks. ( Ouyang-Zhang et al. 2025 ; Dinh et al. 2026 ) Recent work has further extended protein representations to incorporate more expressive tokenizations, including structure-aware and multi-scale representations. ( Su et al. 2023 ; M. Li et al. 2024 ; Tan et al. 2025 ; Cheng et al. 2023 ; Ruffolo et al. 2024 ; Jantos et al. 2022 ). The mechanisms underlying the excellent performance of pLMs in mutational prediction remain an active area of investigation, with proposed explanations ranging from learned evolutionary information ( Hayes et al. 2025 ), interacting sequence motifs (Z. Zhang et al. 2024 ), in-context positional effects ( Vieira et al. 2026 ), or learned representations corresponding to structural, functional, and protein-family-level biological features ( Parsan et al. 2025 ; Simon and Zou 2025 ; Gujral et al. 2025 ; Adams et al. 2025 ; Candido et al. 2026 ). In parallel, a growing class of transformer-based models has been developed for small molecules, encoding ligand information directly from Simplified Molecular Input Line System (SMILES) ( Weininger 1988 ) strings or molecular graphs. ( Chithrananda et al. 2020 ; Irwin et al. 2022 ; Ross et al. 2021 ; Born and Manica 2023 ) Together, these developments provide the foundation for integrated modeling of protein–ligand interactions using learned representations of both proteins and small molecules.

Many modern drug-target affinity models integrate representations learned by pretrained protein and ligand encoders. ( Jiang et al. 2026 ; Vefghi et al. 2025 ) These models combine learned embeddings using fusion strategies, including simple concatenation ( Öztürk et al. 2018 ), attention-based mechanisms such as bi-directional attention ( Huang et al. 2022 ) and cross-attention (X. Zhang et al. 2024 ; Kawaharada et al. 2026 ), as well as higher-order interactions through bilinear attention ( Bai et al. 2023 ). These approaches typically operate on sequence- or graph-level representations and aim to predict scalar binding affinities without explicitly modeling binding geometry. When protein-ligand structures are available, whether from experiments, simulations, or co-folding prediction, geometry-based approaches can provide a more physically grounded alternative. In this setting, interactions are modeled explicitly using spatial representations such as binding pocket graphs or equivariant neural networks that capture atomic-level interactions (Z. Xie et al. 2025 ; Graber et al. 2025 ; J. Liu et al. 2026 ; Sim and Lee 2026 ; J. Yu et al. 2026 ) More recently, diffusion and flow-matching models have emerged as powerful generative frameworks for protein-ligand docking. These methods iteratively refine ligand poses (and in some cases protein conformations) through learned stochastic or deterministic dynamics, ultimately generating bound complexes that can be used for downstream binding affinity estimation. This paradigm offers a flexible alternative to traditional docking approaches by directly modeling the distribution of plausible binding configurations. ( Corso et al. 2022 , 2024 ; Passaro et al. 2025 ; Cremer et al. 2025 ; Morehead and Cheng 2024 ; W. Lu et al. 2022 ; Stärk et al. 2022 )

Although protein-ligand affinity datasets span diverse protein families and chemical scaffolds, it is unclear how well predictive methods generalize. Contamination in training abounds for deep learning methods and the physics-based methods are rarely tested prospectively at scale. One potential source of information that has not been leveraged is deep mutational scanning data in the presence of different chemicals. Deep mutational scanning (DMS) provides a complementary experimental framework for evaluating model generalization by systematically measuring the phenotypic consequences of large numbers of sequence variants in parallel ( Fowler and Fields 2014 ). Unlike conventional affinity benchmarks that span many unrelated proteins and ligands, DMS densely samples sequence space around a common protein background, often at single-amino-acid resolution. When the same mutational library is screened under multiple inhibitor conditions, it additionally measures how the functional consequence of an identical mutation changes across chemical environments. These effects emerge from the interplay between sequence variation, protein structure, and protein-ligand interactions. Although measured indirectly through fitness assays, the differential fitness relative to an unperturbed control experiment can be used as a proxy for affinity. More importantly, systematic matched measurements of wild-type and single-mutant protein-ligand interactions are sparse in commonly used affinity datasets and may be further depleted by sequence-identity-based filtering or clustering procedures designed to prevent train–test leakage. Protein language models have demonstrated strong zero-shot performance in predicting mutational effects across diverse protein fitness assays. However, this performance primarily reflects prediction of mutation-dependent fitness in a fixed experimental context; whether the same representations generalize to changes in mutational effects across ligand environments remains unclear ( Hayes et al. 2025 ; Z. Zhang et al. 2024 ; Vieira et al. 2026 ; Parsan et al. 2025 ; Simon and Zou 2025 ; Gujral et al. 2025 ; Adams et al. 2025 ).

To investigate mutation- and ligand-dependent functional effects, we evaluated several state-of-the-art (SOTA) deep learning models on a recent Deep Mutational Scan (DMS) ( Fowler and Fields 2014 ; Araya and Fowler 2011 ) dataset measuring the effects of single-residue mutations within the kinase domain of hepatocyte growth factor receptor (MET), a clinically important drug targets ( Ou et al. 2024 ; Jiao et al. 2018 ), across 11 inhibitor conditions. ( Estevam et al. 2025 ; Rao et al. 2026 , 2024 ) This dataset captures functional changes arising from the interaction between single-point mutations and inhibitor environment. We benchmarked models originally developed for either mutational effect prediction or drug-target affinity estimation to assess their ability to predict functional changes under combined mutation and ligand perturbations. Using activity splitting, we further analyzed model performance in identifying sensitizing and drug-resistance mutations across different inhibitor conditions. To evaluate the generalizability of these observations, we expanded the benchmark to additional kinase–inhibitor datasets involving FGFRs ( Tangermann et al. 2025 ), EGFR ( Kim et al. 2024 ), SRC ( Chakraborty et al. 2024 ), and CDKs ( Persky et al. 2020 ; Gould et al. 2025 ). Collectively, this framework enables a systematic assessment of how current deep learning models capture mutation-dependent protein–ligand interactions and provides guidance for the development of more robust predictive models for drug response.


## Results


## MET DMS data for mutant-specific ligand binding estimates

We first characterized the MET deep mutational scanning (DMS) dataset used for benchmarking deep learning models by examining the distribution of variant fitness scores across 11 inhibitor conditions ( Figure 1A ). Relative to the dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) control, individual inhibitors produced distinct shifts (computed by mean absolute bin migration, ⟨|Δb|⟩ , relative to the DMSO control) in the mutational fitness landscape, indicating substantial ligand-dependent differences in the functional consequences of single-residue substitutions. Although all inhibitors bind within or adjacent to the adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-binding pocket of MET, they stabilize different kinase conformations ( Estevam et al. 2025 ). Type I inhibitors bind the active DFG-in conformation and, more subtly, they can be classified into type Ia or Ib by whether the molecules interact with the solvent front residue G1163 ( Jean Cui 2013 ). Type II inhibitors extend into the hydrophobic back pocket and stabilize an inactive kinase state by disrupting the regulatory R-spine. Type I½ inhibitors combine features of both binding modes, whereas type III inhibitors bind an allosteric site without directly competing with ATP. Notably, the type III inhibitor tivantinib produced the smallest overall shift in variant fitness relative to the DMSO control ( Figure 1A ), consistent with its limited effect on suppressing MET mutant enrichment. We therefore retained tivantinib in the benchmark because it provides a useful reference for evaluating whether models distinguish ligand-dependent functional effects across mechanistically distinct inhibitor classes.


## Structure predictions on cofolding complexes

To compare across protein-ligand affinity prediction models, we needed to generate structures of the relevant protein-ligand complexes for those models reliant on structural inputs. Because many experimentally determined protein structures deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB; ( Berman et al. 2000 )) contain unresolved residues or partially resolved domains, we evaluated whether deep learning-based structure prediction could generate complete protein– ligand complexes suitable for this task. Specifically, we predicted ATP-bound structures of the MET kinase domain to represent its experimental conformation under DMSO control condition using three state-of-the-art co-folding models: AlphaFold3 ( Abramson et al. 2024 ), Boltz-2 ( Passaro et al. 2025 ), and Chai-1 (Chai Discovery et al. 2024 ). For each, we produced predictions with and without template information. All predicted complexes aligned well with the experimentally determined reference structure (PDBID 3DKC; ( Buchanan et al. 2009 ), Figure 1B ), with template guidance consistently improving reconstruction accuracy. AlphaFold3 achieved the highest structural accuracy, reducing the global backbone RMSD from 3.7 Å without templates to 1.2 Å with template matching. Boltz-2 and Chai-1 also benefited from template guidance, with backbone RMSDs improving from 2.8 to 2.5 Å and from 4.0 to 3.0 Å, respectively. The large deviation in backbone RMSDs is likely due to the conformation change in A-loop where the deposited structure introduced two stabilizing mutations and the structure predictions were made with the wildtype sequence ( Figure 1—figure supplement 1 ). Although the predicted ligand poses differed among models along with the A-loop region, ATP remained deeply buried within the binding pocket in all cases, consistent with previous observations that current co-folding models memorize binding pocket locations and do not always recapitulate detailed interactions ( Masters et al. 2025 ). These results indicate that AI-predicted co-folded structures have the potential to provide structural inputs for evaluating geometry-aware models in subsequent analyses for ligands, like ATP, that are in the training set, but that such models may not be as accurate as experimentally determined structures.

We next generated predicted MET kinase complexes for all 11 inhibitor conditions using the three structure prediction frameworks described above ( Figure 1C ). Because experimentally resolved template structures were unavailable for some inhibitor-bound complexes, all structures were predicted without template guidance and subsequently used as inputs for the structure-aware models evaluated in this study. The unavailable 3-dimension structures in PDB are cabozantinib (type II), glesatinib (II), capmatinib (Ib), and glumetinib (Ib). For the other complexes, we note that the specific ligand-bound structures were available in the training sets for these predictors. To validate whether structures predicted with or without template guidance will affect models’ predicted fitness landscape, we evaluated several complexes whose structures are available in PDB. Our results suggest the predicted fitness landscapes among these complexes did not differ significantly between structures predicted with template-free and template-guided ( Figure 1—figure supplement 2 ) and indicating these structure-based models are robust to the structure generation protocol. We therefore used the template-free predicted complexes from identical inference settings for all subsequent analyses which provides a consistent basis for downstream benchmarking.


## Models without explicit ligand representations do not predict ligand-specific mutational fitness

Having established a consistent set of predicted structures across inhibitor conditions, we next asked whether sequence and protein structural information alone are sufficient to recover ligand-dependent mutational fitness. To establish this baseline, we evaluated three representative zero-shot mutational effect predictors: the sequence-only protein language model ESM, the structure-based geometric model ProteinMPNN, and the energy-based diffusion model ProteinEBM ( Roney et al. 2025 ) ( Figure 2A ). ESM and ProteinMPNN models estimate mutational effects from changes in log masked marginal probabilities between wild-type and mutant residues, computed from either sequence representations (ESM) or protein graph representations (ProteinMPNN). ProteinEBM is a more recent approach that scores conformations on free-energy landscapes using diffusion-based generative modeling. Importantly, ESM and ProteinEBM receive identical protein sequences for every inhibitor condition and therefore cannot explicitly encode ligand-dependent effects. In contrast, for ProteinMPNN, we input AlphaFold3-predicted protein conformations generated for each inhibitor-bound complex. In this manner, ProteinMPNN does not explicitly account for interactions with the ligand for its message passing and consequential scoring steps. However, the ligand interactions are accounted for in the input model and can therefore indirectly change the probabilities for each amino acid. Under the DMSO control condition, the ESM2 model achieved moderate agreement with experimentally measured fitness as our baseline method (Spearman’s ρ ≈ 0.50). However, its performance decreased by an average of 53% across inhibitor-treated conditions, indicating limited sensitivity to ligand-dependent functional changes despite strong baseline performance. ProteinEBM showed reduced performance under both untreated and inhibitor-treated conditions, but showed a smaller average decline in predicting the inhibitor-treaded conditions with average decreases of 27%. This may suggest that ProteinEBM can identify stable variants that are insensitive to inhibitor conditions. ProteinMPNN also exhibited a large reduction in performance (a decrease of 54%) despite receiving distinct inhibitor-specific structural inputs, suggesting that differences in predicted protein conformations alone are in-sufficient to recover ligand-specific mutational effects. Collectively, these results suggest that although zero-shot mutational effect predictors do well on predicting protein fitness in the unperturbed condition, they cannot predict the effects of ligand perturbations because the main driving force, protein-ligand interactions, remains missing from their input.

We next evaluated recently developed protein language models that augment sequence representations with structural information ( Su et al. 2023 ; M. Li et al. 2024 ; Ouyang-Zhang et al. 2025 ), evolutionary context from multiple sequence alignments ( Jantos et al. 2022 ), or both ( Tan et al. 2025 ). In addition, we assessed recent graph- and geometry-based generative models originally developed for protein design ( Birnbaum and Keating 2026 ) and subsequently adapted for mutational thermostability prediction ( Dieckhaus et al. 2024 ). Despite these architectural differences, mutational effect predictions improved by less than 0.05 in Spearman correlation compared to the baseline ESM2-650M model in the control condition ( Figure 2B and Figure 2—figure supplement 1A ) and the next generation ESM model ( Candido et al. 2026 ), ESMC, outperforms all these PLM variants. Similar to the baseline, all models showed performance reductions of approximately 50% under inhibitor-treated conditions relative to the DMSO control. Notably, providing full-length protein sequences, and corresponding predicted full-length structures when required, did not consistently improve mutational effect prediction compared with using the isolated kinase domain ( Figure 2—figure supplement 2 ). Several factors may contribute to the limited improvement afforded by structure-aware models. Inhibitor binding largely preserves the overall kinase fold, such that structural differences between conditions are relatively subtle. In addition, learned structural representations may emphasize global fold similarity over these local conformational differences, making structure-derived features, including Foldseek tokens (van Kempen et al. 2024 ), insufficiently discriminative across inhibitor conditions. Finally, the predicted input structures may themselves attenuate ligand-induced perturbations, further limiting the structural signal available to downstream models. Thus, providing three-dimensional protein structure alone does not necessarily ensure that the ligand-dependent information relevant to mutational fitness is represented or used by the model.

To better understand whether prediction accuracy deteriorates under inhibitor treatment in the same way for different models, we analyzed model behavior from two complementary perspectives: inter-model agreement and sensitivity to ligand context. For the inter-model analysis, Spearman’s correlation coefficients were computed between predictions from different models across all inhibitor conditions ( Hou et al. 2026 ) ( Figure 2C lower triangle; condition-specific analyses are shown in Figure 2—figure supplement 3 ). Sequence-based protein language models exhibited strong mutual agreement, and a similar pattern was observed among graph-based MPNN-family models, likely reflecting shared training objectives and architectural principles. In contrast, models developed for distinct prediction tasks, including ThermoMPNN ( Dieckhaus et al. 2024 ), PottsMPNN ( Birnbaum and Keating 2026 ) and ProteinEBM ( Roney et al. 2025 ), showed substantially weaker agreement with the remaining methods. We next assessed ligand-context sensitivity by correlating predictions across inhibitor conditions ( Figure 2C upper triangle). Although the experimentally measured fitness landscapes differed substantially between inhibitors (pairwise Pearson correlations ranging from approximately 0.3 to 0.8; Figure 2—figure supplement 2 ), most models produced highly similar predictions across conditions. In particular, structure-aware protein language models frequently generated predictions with pairwise correlations exceeding 0.9, indicating limited sensitivity to ligand-dependent functional effects. Together, these analyses suggest that current models primarily encode fitness while remaining largely insensitive to ligand-specific functional perturbations. Moreover, the similarities in architectures and approaches render the predictions highly correlated with each other.


## Ligand-aware models do not greatly improve predictions

Next, we evaluated recently developed ligand-aware models, including the protein-chemical language model, ProtLigand, ( Kalifa et al. 2025 ) and graph-based models, LigandMPNN (Justas Dauparas et al. 2025 ) and LASErMPNN ( Fry et al. 2025 ). ProtLigand connects the structure-aware latents of SaProt ( Su et al. 2023 ) with the hidden embeddings of a ligand from the pretrained ChemBERTa transformer ( Chithrananda et al. 2020 ) using cross attention ( Vaswani et al. 2017 ). This design allows ProtLigand, in principle, to predict mutational effects in the presence of ligand molecules with a log-likelihood ratio trained on the masked token reconstruction task ( Meier et al. 2021 ). In contrast, LigandMPNN extends ProteinMPNN’s (J. Dauparas et al. 2022 ) architecture through the introduction of k-nearest (k=25) connected residue-ligand graphs which explicitly accounts for the influence of ligand atoms to residue likelihood prediction. Meanwhile, LASErMPNN ( Fry et al. 2025 )introduces the ligand representation along with protein-ligand interaction graphs into message-passing layers. This was originally developed for zero-shot design of ligand-binding proteins can therefore score a per residue likelihood from a geometric graph of protein-ligand complex. Despite explicitly incorporating ligand information, these approaches also exhibited performance reductions exceeding 50% under inhibitor-treated conditions. Again, this suggests that current ligand representations, or input wild-type structure accuracies, remain insufficient to capture mutation-dependent functional responses ( Figure 2B ). ProtLig remains highly correlated with ligand-unaware models across inhibitor treatments, suggesting only minimal interaction between the specific mutations and the ligand. In contrast, LigandMPNN and LASErMPNN provide more distinct predictions across inhibitor conditions than protein-only models, which indicates potential for these approaches to explicitly model ligand interactions when provided an accurate structural input.

Although overall improvements in prediction accuracy remained modest, ligand-aware models more accurately recovered several experimentally validated resistance-associated hotspots than their corresponding protein-only models (e.g. ProteinMPNN). Under the type I inhibitor crizotinib, LASErMPNN more consistently classified known resistance mutations than ProteinMPNN ( Figure 3A , upper panel). We attribute that this success is due to the fact that ligand input information is directly encoded and interacts with the associated protein embedding during message passing layers. In particular, mutations at the solvent-front hotspot G1163 ( Jean Cui 2013 ; C. Wang and Lu 2023 ; Fujino et al. 2019 ; Recondo et al. 2020 ); Figure 3A ), which were predicted as phenotypically neutral or mildly sensitizing by protein-only models such as ProteinMPNN and SaProt, shifted toward experimentally observed resistance phenotypes once explicit ligand information was incorporated. A similar trend was observed for the deeply buried binding-pocket residue L1140, where LASErMPNN consistently predicted mild resistance toward both type I and type II inhibitors, whereas ProteinMPNN remained largely insensitive to ligand context. Structural comparisons ( Figure 3B ) provide a mechanistic explanation for this behavior, local protein conformations surrounding L1140 remain nearly indistinguishable across protein-only predicted structures despite different inhibitor conditions, indicating that protein geometry alone provides insufficient information to distinguish ligand-specific effects. Ligand-aware models were also successful in predicting the profiles for another prominent mutation hotspot (Y1230) ( Recondo et al. 2020 ; Fujino et al. 2021 ), which directly contacts several type I inhibitors and participates in π-stacking interactions within the binding pocket. However, these improvements were less apparent for type II inhibitors.

ProtLigand similarly identified Y1230 as a resistance-associated site under type I inhibitor conditions ( Figure 3B ). However, the performance was similar for SaProt, suggesting that the masked sequence reconstruction objective inherited from conventional protein language models is driving performance. In this case, conditioning protein embeddings on ligand representations through cross-attention does not explicitly optimize protein-ligand interaction representations ( Alley et al. 2019 ; Gordon et al. 2024 ). Consequently, ligand information appears to exert only a weak influence on the learned embeddings, limiting the ability of current ligand-aware protein language models to capture inhibitor-dependent mutational effects.

In summary, explicit incorporation of ligand information can improve prediction of several clinically relevant resistance hotspots. However, these improvements remained localized to known sites directly adjacent to the ligand (solvent-front, hinge-region, and activation-loop residues). This observation reflects the local nature of the graph construction and indicates that current ligand-aware architectures and/or structure predictions fail to fully capture mutation-dependent protein–ligand interactions. These observations are in agreement with recent work that suggests that many mutational effects arise through long-range allosteric mechanisms rather than large structural rearrangements ( Liao and Lehner 2026 ). It further raises the question of whether current structure prediction models adequately capture the interaction-level perturbations required for downstream protein–ligand prediction. Nonetheless, we continued exploring the utility of predicted mutant structures for estimating ligand affinity.


## Geometric protein-ligand affinity models perform better when single-point mutations are closer to the active site

While in earlier sections we frame the question as reconstructing fitness landscapes under different inhibitor conditions, the task can also be formulated as predicting affinity landscapes of inhibitor molecules to different receptors, in this case different mutants of MET. The conventional approach to this task is geometry-based molecular docking, wherein the binding orientation and affinity between a drug and a protein are estimated based on several physical non-covalent interactions ( Kuntz et al. 1982 ; DesJarlais et al. 1986 ; Allen et al. 2015 ). We therefore docked individual inhibitors against mutant structures as our baseline using AutoDock-GPU ( Santos-Martins et al. 2021 ). We also examined deep learning models for drug-target affinity prediction. These deep learning models are typically trained on a broad chemical space and provide a complementary framework for evaluating ligand-dependent mutational effects ( Figure 4A ) (Wei and McCammon 2024; Pan et al. 2026 ). These approaches represent conceptually distinct approximations of molecular recognition: docking evaluates binding using explicitly defined, simplified physical interaction terms, whereas deep learning affinity models learn statistical representations of protein-ligand interactions from structural and affinity data. Despite this distinction, both approaches remain dependent on the structural representation of the protein-ligand complex. Consequently, when experimentally resolved mutant structures are unavailable, as in most of our benchmark, their sensitivity to mutational effects may ultimately be constrained by the accuracy and mutation responsiveness of the predicted structures used as input.

Since many modern affinity models require explicit protein-ligand structures, we used Boltz-2 predicted complexes as inputs to all structure-based methods, which were generated directly with the Boltz-2 structure module. ( Passaro et al. 2025 ; Wan et al. 2026 ; Bret et al. 2026 ; Dolorfino et al. 2026 ) Note that several affinity prediction models (such as TankBind (W. Lu et al. 2022 ) and FLOWR.root ( Cremer et al. 2025 )) rely on having a co-folded prediction of ligand and receptor. Instead, they were validated on apo protein structures and ligand SMILES strings as in conventional docking. Our evaluation scheme may therefore introduce underlying inductive biases from Boltz-2 predicted ligand-bound structures during inference. Moreover, recent study by ( Feldman et al. 2026 ) has reported that AlphaFold3-derived structures—and consequently Boltz-2 predictions—are relatively insensitive to single amino acid substitutions due to its dependent on the coevolutionary information derived from multiple sequence alignment augmentation. While most single substitutions do not dramatically reshape proteins, mutational effects in modern structure prediction models are local and their performance deteriorates for strongly perturbative mutations (Zhuoyi Liu et al. 2026 ). This raises the possibility that mutation-dependent affinity changes may likewise be attenuated in downstream structure-based affinity prediction (further validated with our mutant structures in Figure 4—figure supplement 1 )

To assess whether these affinity models recover biologically meaningful mutation-dependent effects, we first established two experimentally supported expectations ( Estevam et al. 2025 ). First, the type III inhibitor tivantinib exhibited fitness landscapes that were highly correlated with the DMSO control condition, consistent with comparatively weak target engagement relative to the other MET inhibitors in the panel. ( Figure 1A , Figure 2C and Figure 2—figure supplement 3 ) Accordingly, affinity models should predict substantially weaker binding for tivantinib than for the remaining inhibitors. Second, mutations located within or near the ATP-binding pocket were expected to produce larger perturbations in predicted binding affinity than distal mutations because local structural and interaction changes are more likely to disrupt protein-ligand binding. Consequently, affinity changes associated with binding-pocket mutations should generate broader and more extreme prediction distributions than mutations occurring elsewhere in the kinase domain.

Most structure- and language-based affinity models correctly identified tivantinib as the weakest MET binder, consistent with its DMS fitness landscape closely resembling the DMSO control condition where the control condition was approximated by MET’s binding affinity against ATP ( Figure 4B ). The remaining inhibitors were generally predicted to bind more strongly, with the clearest separation observed for Boltz-2 ( Passaro et al. 2025 ) and FLOWR.root ( Cremer et al. 2025 ) which predicted substantially larger affinity differences between tivantinib and the other tyrosine kinase inhibitors. ( Figure 4B and C ) More importantly, geometry-aware affinity models were considerably more sensitive than transformer-based language models to mutation-induced perturbations within the ATP-binding pocket despite both relying on predicted mutant structures. In particular, Boltz-2, TankBind and FLOWR.root produced broader affinity distributions for binding-pocket mutations than for mutations across the full kinase domain ( Figure 4 C). ( Dolorfino et al. 2026 ). These results indicate that local mutations exert stronger effects on inhibitor binding than distal mutations when evaluated using models capable of representing local conformational adaptations with the potential of modifying the direct interactions with the ligand molecule itself. This result is accomplished in different ways by the two most successful models: Boltz-2 and TankBind incorporate additional single and masked pair representations beyond predicted atomic coordinates, whereas FLOWR.root explicitly refines local protein–ligand conformations during affinity prediction. In contrast, FlowDock ( Morehead and Cheng 2024 ), did not exhibit comparable sensitivity to binding-site mutations, potentially reflecting stronger structural priors inherited from ESMFold-based representations ( Lin et al. 2023 ). Nevertheless, even the best-performing affinity models systematically underestimated the magnitude of mutation-induced affinity changes, likely reflecting either the limited sensitivity of predicted mutant structures, the scarcity of mutation-conditioned supervision in current drug-target affinity training datasets, or the difficulty in scaling between affinity and fitness from the DMS. Together, these findings indicate that current affinity models successfully distinguish broad differences in chemical potency which are likely dominated by properties of the ligand molecules itself, but remain substantially less effective at capturing subtle mutation-dependent perturbations and the corresponding actual protein-ligand interactions ( Dolorfino et al. 2026 ).

To directly compare predicted affinity changes with experimental DMS measurements, we computed the difference in fitness scores between inhibitor-treated and ATP-bound conditions (Δf v = f v,treatment - f v,control ), which quantify mutation-dependent changes in inhibitor sensitivity. For this evaluation, we further included two ΔΔG models which were trained or finetuned explicitly on experimental measurements of mutation effects ( Pires et al. 2015 ; Yang et al. 2023 ; Hauser et al. 2018 ), namely, PremPLI ( Sun et al. 2021 ) and MutDTA ( Hu et al. 2025 ). Across all missense variants, affinity predictions showed little global correlation with experimentally measured fitness changes. (Spearman ρ < 0.2; Figure 5A top). Notably, most models perform better with an input of just the binding pocket rather than the full domain( Figure 5A ; Figure 5—figure supplement 1 ).These observations are consistent with the notion that geometric models predominantly capture local interaction geometry, whereas transformer-based models rely on highly stable latent representations that are only weakly perturbed by single amino acid substitutions.

Following recent analysis by (J. Yu et al. 2026 ), where affinity predictions are evaluated by mutations that preserve experimentally observed binding conformations (i.e., tolerated mutations) and active-site mutations oftentimes impair protein stability ( Khersonsky et al. 2018 ), we classified DMS variants into sensitizing- and resistance-associated mutations. We filtered out non-tolerated mutations in DMSO ( Figure 5B ) and examined the spatial distribution of resistance and sensitizing mutations ( Figure 5C ). A clear distinction can be drawn between the two categories: resistance mutations concentrate at the binding region and sensitizing mutations spread over the full kinase domain. When evaluated separately, all affinity models showed little or no correlation with experimentally measured sensitizing mutations but achieved low to moderate agreement for resistance-associated mutations ( Figure 5D ). Nonetheless, the performance of the models could be significantly improved (from absence of correlation to weakly correlated) when restricted to positions in the binding pocket for sensitizing mutations under certain inhibitor conditions ( Figure 5D ). One possible explanation for this result is that resistance mutations generally preserve the overall protein fold, and, more importantly the key amino acid(s), while perturbing local protein-ligand interactions, making them more compatible with the largely mutation-invariant structures used as model inputs. In contrast, sensitizing mutations may involve larger conformational or stability changes that are insufficiently represented by current structure prediction frameworks. These results suggest that the limited structural sensitivity of predicted mutant complexes constrains downstream affinity models in capturing the full spectrum of mutation-dependent functional effects. Overall, these observations suggest that current in silico affinity prediction methods are more effective at identifying mutations that locally perturb protein–ligand interactions within the binding pocket than mutations that induce broader conformational or stability changes across the protein.

Although global agreement between predicted affinity changes and experimental DMS measurements remained limited, multiple affinity models consistently converged on a subset of biologically meaningful resistance-associated hotspots. We therefore identified candidate affinity-sensitive residues by aggregating recurrent outlier predictions across models using a consensus-based ensemble approach ( Figure 6A ). We first examined the contributions of individual models to the recovery of documented resistance hotspots across inhibitor conditions ( Figure 6B ; Figure 6—figure supplement 1 ). For example, Boltz-2 predicted substantial affinity losses for mutations at G1163 and M1211, whereas PremPLI contributed strongly to predictions at D1228. Across models, several clinically established resistance hotspots, including G1163, Y1230, and M1211, were consistently recovered, whereas other documented sites, including L1157, F1134, and G1242, were rarely detected, if at all.

We next asked whether cross-model consensus could identify condition-specific resistance and sensitizing sites. We showed the five highest-ranked resistance- and sensitization-associated positions predicted for the type I inhibitor crizotinib and the type II inhibitor cabozantinib ( Figure 6C ). The highest-ranked positions were located within or near the ligand-binding pocket. In particular, mutations at the C-spine residue M1211 and solvent-front residue G1163 were predicted by multiple models, including GEMS, Boltz-2, FLOWR.root, EM-PLA, TankBind, and PremPLI, to substantially reduce crizotinib binding affinity. Likewise, the gatekeeper residue L1157 was repeatedly identified as a resistance-associated hotspot, consistent with its well-established role in regulating access to the hydrophobic back pocket ( Tiedt et al. 2011 ; Hart et al. 2019 ). For cabozantinib, mutations at the hinge residue Y1159 were predicted to weaken binding and mutations to M1160 within the kinase hinge region, likely reflecting its removal in hydrogen-bonding interactions with bound inhibitors ( Damghani et al. 2021 )( Figure 6D ), whereas the same position was less strongly prioritized under crizotinib, ranking ninth among resistance-associated sites ( Figure 6C ). This condition-dependent ranking suggests that the consensus approach can distinguish at least some inhibitor-specific resistance effects.

In contrast, sensitizing predictions showed considerably weaker agreement with experiment. Mutations at the P-loop residue G1085 were consistent with the experimentally measured ROSACE changes and were correctly classified as sensitizing to crizotinib, whereas most other highly ranked sensitizing predictions did not exhibit substantially greater loss of function than in the corresponding DMSO control. Similarly, under cabozantinib treatment, only mutations at the catalytic lysine K1110 among the highlighted sites were consistent with the experimentally observed sensitizing phenotype. Overall, consensus predictions were therefore substantially more robust for resistance-associated mutations than for sensitizing mutations, with the former repeatedly recovering experimentally supported binding-pocket hotspots while most predicted sensitizing sites showed limited agreement with the DMS measurements. Collectively, these analyses suggest that, despite limited global agreement with experimental fitness changes, independent affinity models can recover structurally and clinically relevant resistance hotspots within the MET kinase domain. Cross-model consensus may therefore provide a useful framework for prioritizing candidate resistance mutations for downstream experimental validation.


## Evaluation on diverse kinase-inhibitor DMS datasets yields similar performance

To test whether the lessons from the MET kinase benchmark extended to other deep mutational scanning datasets, we collected other DMS datasets from distinct kinases: SRC ( Chakraborty et al. 2024 ), CDKs ( Persky et al. 2020 ), EGFR ( Kim et al. 2024 ), and FGFRs ( Tangermann et al. 2025 ). While these datasets were measured with different assays, metrics, and data-processing ( Table 1 ), they all fundamentally report on how single amino acid substitutions affect the enrichment of their target when an inhibitor is present.

We followed the same strategy and metrics to assess the ability of different models to predict mutational effects and protein-inhibitor interactions as we used for the MET-inhibitor data. First, we evaluated zero-shot models: ESM2-3B ( Lin et al. 2023 ), SaProt ( Su et al. 2023 ), ProtLigand ( Kalifa et al. 2025 ), ThermoMPNN ( Dieckhaus et al. 2024 ), LASErMPNN ( Fry et al. 2025 ) and LigandMPNN (Justas Dauparas et al. 2025 ). In general, we observed similar performance degradation under non-control conditions as shown ( Figure 7A ). Moreover, among these DMS datasets, language models outperformed geometric models, which is consistent with the lessons from the MET dataset.

Next, we evaluated affinity predictions from two structure-based models, Boltz-2 affinity module ( Passaro et al. 2025 ) and FLOWR.root ( Cremer et al. 2025 ), which performed best of the models on the MET dataset. Similar to the the MET benchmark, their correlations to enrichment changes over conditions (d treatment - d control ) remained very small (spearman ρ less than 0.1) with Boltz-2 predicted mutant-inhibitor complex structures. We assessed their ability to recover experimentally documented resistance-associated mutations. From over 32 unique resistance sites (60 screenings in total under inhibitor conditions), Boltz-2 flagged 27 of them and FLOWR.root recovered 17 of them ( Figure 7B ), while the two models jointly identified 11 hotspots resistance positions and 25 sites are missed by both of them. In summary, these results demonstrate that the conclusions drawn from the MET benchmark generalize across diverse kinase systems: sequence-based protein language models consistently outperform geometric mutational effect predictors, whereas affinity prediction models remain constrained by mutation-invariant structural representations ( Feldman et al. 2026 ) despite explicitly modeling protein-ligand interactions.


## Discussion

We found that none of the evaluated deep learning models reliably predict mutation-dependent functional responses across inhibitor conditions in MET kinase ( Estevam et al. 2025 ). Although sequence- and MSA-augmented protein language models achieved the strongest overall performance, their predictions remained largely insensitive to ligand context. We attribute the strong performance of protein language models to evolutionary information encoded during large-scale pretraining, which provides informative priors for general mutational effects and likely contributes to their advantage over geometric models that are not directly exposed to comparable sequence variation ( Gordon et al. 2024 ; Hou et al. 2026 ; Cagiada et al. 2025 ). However, evolutionary sequence variation and the mutational space accessible through DMS are not equivalent. MSAs reflect substitutions sampled and filtered through evolutionary selection, whereas DMS can systematically interrogate substitutions irrespective of their occurrence in natural sequence space. Inhibitor-conditioned DMS introduces an additional dimension by measuring how the functional consequence of the same mutation changes across chemical environments. Thus, even a rich evolutionary representation of mutational tolerance does not directly encode the mutation-by-ligand interactions required to reconstruct inhibitor-specific fitness landscapes. Consistent with this distinction, pLM predictions remained highly correlated across inhibitor conditions despite experimentally distinct resistance and sensitization landscapes. Models explicitly developed for drug-target affinity prediction showed a complementary limitation, capturing aspects of ligand binding while remaining poorly correlated with mutation-dependent functional responses. Together, these results suggest that current models capture either general mutational constraints or protein-ligand recognition, but not both.

Although structure-aware protein language models can implicitly incorporate spatial information about the inhibitor, they did not consistently improve ligand-conditioned mutational effect prediction over sequence-based models. As an example, in the MET kinase system, type I and type II inhibitors induce only modest global conformational differences, resulting in highly similar structural representations across inhibitor conditions. Consequently, structure-derived embeddings and geometric tokens remain insufficiently discriminative when ligand-specific interactions are absent or only indirectly encoded. While many predicted structures are sufficiently accurate for many downstream structural biology applications at protein-level, the resulting mutation-invariant structural priors in state-of-the-art structure prediction models ( Feldman et al. 2026 ) may obscure the subtle local perturbations that govern inhibitor binding and drug resistance. Consistent with this hypothesis, ligand-aware geometric models, including LigandMPNN, LASErMPNN, Boltz-2, and FLOWR.root, more accurately recovered experimentally validated resistance-associated solvent-front, hinge-region, and gate-keeper mutations than protein-only models. Beyond sequence- and structure-derived representations, recent studies have begun to explore general-purpose large language models, as an additional source of biological and chemical knowledge ( Fallahpour et al. 2026 ; Anand and Goel 2026 ). Integrating such representations with molecular geometry may provide complementary contextual information that is not readily captured by protein sequence or structure alone, although whether this improves mutation-conditioned protein–ligand prediction remains to be established.

Our results further indicate that incorporating ligand representations through multimodal fusion alone is insufficient to recover mutation-conditioned ligand responses. Although ProtLigand conditions structure-aware protein embeddings on molecular representations through cross-attention, its predictions remained highly correlated across inhibitor conditions and closely resembled those of its backbone structure-aware protein language model, SaProt. One possible explanation is that the masked-sequence reconstruction objective inherited from protein language modeling remains the dominant training signal, while ligand information acts primarily as an auxiliary conditioning signal. In contrast, ligand-aware geometric models explicitly propagate information through protein-ligand contacts. These models showed localized sensitivity only to mutations near the binding pocket. This limitation emerges because the overall training strategy for mutation effect prediction methods emphasize the protein component rather than the ligand contribution. In contrast, affinity prediction models treat these two components equally, but remain biased by ligand features dominating the splitting strategy than protein properties (J. Li et al. 2026 ). Together, these observations suggest that interaction-aware representations may not emerge simply by combining independently learned protein and ligand embeddings, and motivate training objectives that directly couple sequence variation with ligand-dependent outcomes. (L. Xie et al. 2026 ; J. Yu et al. 2026 ) More broadly, mutation effect prediction and mutation-conditioned ligand response prediction should be considered related but distinct learning problems: evolutionary constraints provide strong priors for the former, whereas the latter additionally requires resolving how a specific mutation perturbs molecular interactions within a particular ligand environment.

Importantly, the limitations observed for MET were not specific to a single kinase system. Extending the benchmark to 13 kinase-inhibitor pairs spanning SRC, CDKs, EGFR, and FGFRs reproduced the same broad performance trends across mutational effect and affinity prediction models. At the same time, assembling this expanded benchmark exposed a second limitation: mutation-conditioned kinase datasets remain sparse relative to the large-scale datasets available for related protein fitness and drug–target affinity tasks ( Tsuboyama et al. 2023 ; Notin et al. 2023 ; Zhihai Liu et al. 2014 ; T. Liu et al. 2007 ). Critically, the limitation is not simply dataset size, but also the type and consistency of the available supervision. Protein function is measured using heterogeneous assays, selection schemes, and readouts, making quantitative measurements difficult to compare directly across studies ( Anand and Goel 2026 ). Existing resources such as ProteinGym and FLIP2 partly accommodate this heterogeneity by evaluating models within individual assays or tasks rather than assuming that experimental scores share a common scale ( Notin et al. 2023 ; Didi et al. 2026 ). While this provides a practical framework for benchmarking, it does not fully address the challenge of learning transferable genotype-phenotype relationships across experiments. This problem becomes more pronounced for mutation-conditioned drug response, where ligand identity and experimental context introduce an additional source of variation. Inhibitor-dependent changes in cellular fitness or enrichment can reflect altered protein-ligand interactions, but can also arise from changes in protein folding, trafficking, catalytic activity, or other determinants of cellular fitness. Without orthogonal measurements that constrain these effects, such as protein surface expression, it remains difficult to determine which molecular consequence of a mutation produces the observed drug-response phenotype. Current drug-target affinity datasets present the complementary problem: they predominantly contain measurements for wild-type proteins and therefore provide limited supervision for learning how sequence variation reshapes protein-ligand interactions.

An ideal mutation-conditioned training resource would therefore align sequence variation, ligand identity and concentration, direct biochemical measurements, and functional phenotypes under standardized experimental conditions. For kinase inhibitors, inhibitor-conditioned DMS experiments could be paired with measurements of protein abundance or surface expression, catalytic activity, direct binding affinity, and clinically validated resistance annotations ( Singh et al. 2025 ; Aldeghi et al. 2019 ; Saifudeen et al. 2026 ; Yang et al. 2023 ). Such orthogonal measurements would help distinguish mutations that directly perturb inhibitor binding from those that alter drug response indirectly through protein stability, expression, or activity, providing the context-aware supervision needed for more reliable prediction of mutation-dependent drug sensitivity and resistance. Meanwhile, synthetic data generated by structure, affinity, or molecular simulation models could help expand sparsely sampled regions of mutation–ligand space. However, such augmentation cannot by itself provide the missing biological supervision: synthetic affinity or structural labels do not necessarily capture downstream effects on protein stability, expression, catalytic activity, or cellular fitness, and may propagate biases already present in the generating models. Synthetic data may therefore be most informative when calibrated against matched experimental measurements rather than used as a replacement for them.

More broadly, our findings indicate that mutation-conditioned drug response represents a distinct challenge from either protein fitness or conventional drug-target affinity prediction. Current protein language models capture strong priors for general mutational effects, whereas ligand-aware and affinity models can identify aspects of protein-ligand recognition and local resistance mechanisms. However, neither capability alone is sufficient to predict how mutational effects change across ligand environments. Addressing this gap will require both models and experimental datasets that explicitly couple sequence variation with ligand-dependent molecular and functional responses. Together, these advances may provide a path toward more reliable prospective prediction of mutation-dependent drug sensitivity and resistance ( Urrego et al. 2026 ).

## Mutation Labeling

In this work, we labeled single-point mutations using two methods: 1) binding region mutations and 2) essential mutations. Binding region mutations correspond to mutations that are within 5 Angstroms to any heavy atoms of the ligand, which is a default cutoff used in many deep learning models for defining binding pockets. Essential mutations are variants whose fitness score changes drastically when treated with and without inhibitor molecules. We define mutations that are one standard deviation away from zero in difference scores,f treatment -f control , distribution as essential, in particular, negative difference values refer to sensitized mutation (to the corresponding inhibitor) and positive values indicate resistance mutation (to the corresponding inhibitor).

## Ensemble predictions of low- and high-affinity mutants to inhibitors

To categorize resistance and sensitizing mutations with affinity prediction models, we followed a similar strategy to determine resistance and sensitizing mutations with experimental ROSACE values. In particular, affinity prediction models were trained to predict different experimental observables, such as log 10 IC 50 and p K i = − log 10 K i . We therefore, negated values predicted in pK i which indicates higher values are now predicted to be weaker binder across all predictions made. For each per-inhibitor per-model distribution, the mutations lying at the left tail (i.e., two standard deviations away from the mean) are identified as sensitizing mutations to the corresponding inhibitor and, conversely, mutations are classified as resistance if they are at the right tail.

Next, all models predicted resistance and sensitizing mutations were aggregated. For a position, p , its score, Σ is defined in Equation 4 . where m is affinity model, n m ( p ) is the number of substitutions at position p flagged by model m , and the scaling parameter α is set to be 0.5. Under this definition, repeated calls from a single model contribute sublinearly, so that concordance across models outweighs repetition within one. Finally, the strong- and weak-binder position scores were ranked independent and the five highest scoring positions were presented and compared against documented and experimentally measured hotspots.
