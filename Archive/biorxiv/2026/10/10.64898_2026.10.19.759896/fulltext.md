## Abstract

Computational protein design workflows require the orchestration of multiple tools, data formats, and analysis steps that are often fragmented. Here, we introduce Chaperone, a lightweight and customizable framework that enables seamless integration of RFdiffusion3 (RFD3) [ 1 ] with downstream sequence design, structure prediction, and evaluation tools, including ProteinMPNN [ 2 ], AlphaFold3 (AF3) [ 3 ], and more than 50 other protein design tools. By combining a programmable agent-friendly interface with parallelized execution on high-performance computing systems, Chaperone as a harness enables rapid iteration and scalable exploration of complex protein design workflows. Across an evaluation of nine biomolecular design tasks, we find using Chaperone reduces total token usage relative to baseline agents by 86% and GPU costs by 64%, while increasing the overall computational design pass rate. We demonstrate Chaperone’s utility for large-scale design-space exploration through a two-week end-to-end small-molecule binder design campaign, yielding low nanomolar binders to cortisol zero shot. We further demonstrate its versatility across distinct design challenges, including the design of a symmetric 12-subunit self-assembly nanoparticle and a linker that mediates interactions between C2- and D3-symmetric protein complexes to form crystalline assemblies. Together, these results demonstrate how agent harnesses built around standardized tool interfaces can substantially improve the efficiency and success of complex, long-horizon agentic design workflows in biology. We make Chaperone freely available to the community on GitHub: https://github.com/RosettaCommons/chaperone .


## 1 Introduction

Protein design pipelines have evolved rapidly in the recent years [ 2 , 4 – 8 ]. The sprawling open-source ecosystem has spurred the development of a wide variety of deep learning-based generative methods [ 1 , 5 , 6 , 9 ], prediction oracles [ 3 , 10 – 12 ], as well as physics-based scoring methods [ 13 , 14 ]. This growing ecosystem, coupled with the increasing complexity of design tasks, often necessitates the integration of multiple such tools. Workflows are therefore often convoluted, requiring users to connect several independently developed tools into multi-step pipelines without standardized data formatting or APIs. Furthermore, users often rely on bespoke computational environments that require manual installation and scripting to execute these tools on their own systems. Collectively, these challenges create energy barriers for researchers seeking to explore novel design hypotheses through combinations of various tools. Several recent studies have demonstrated the importance of complex, multi-stage design strategies [ 15 – 19 ]. Widely adopted systems such as BindCraft [ 8 ] integrate multiple protein design tools into accessible, end-to-end pipelines. HalluDesign [ 16 ] and Protein Hunter [ 17 ] demonstrate the benefits of iterative design, improving computational outcomes through repeated cycles of generation, evaluation, and refinement. However, these systems are primarily built around predefined workflows, and incorporating alternative models, custom evaluation metrics, or new optimization strategies often requires modifying their underlying implementations.

We set out to address these challenges by developing a customizable and lightweight abstraction layer that integrates a broad range of protein design tools under one umbrella, with the goal of enabling seamless interoperability through a direct DataFrame-based framework. We hypothesized that such an interface would extend into a natural harness for AI agents to assist researchers in pursuing challenging protein design tasks, decreasing the barrier for exploration, and accelerating research beyond conventional timelines. We focused in particular on design workflows involving the powerful recently released RFD3 generative protein design program, widely used for enzyme, binder and conformational switch design.


## 2 The Chaperone framework

By interconnecting a broad set of design tools within a unified framework, Chaperone enables researchers to systematically explore complex design strategies, customize computational pipelines, and execute experiments in parallel across a diverse range of targets and tasks.

Previous work has addressed multi-tool orchestration challenges through several complementary approaches. BioPipelines [ 20 ] and ProteinDJ [ 21 ] facilitate multi-tool workflow construction and execution on high-performance computing systems, while ProtFlow [ 22 ] provides a DataFrame-based interface for managing candidate designs and their associated metrics. Proto [ 23 ] approaches biological design through programmable, constraint-based optimization, whereas ProteinMCP [ 24 ] and T-REX [ 25 ] demonstrate the potential of agent-driven tool orchestration and adaptive design campaigns. Building on these efforts, we sought to develop a maximally lightweight framework that simplifies the integration of heterogeneous protein design tools, motivated in particular by the broad range of design tasks enabled by RFdiffusion3 (RFD3). Applications ranging from enzyme scaffolding and small-molecule binder design to RNA-binding protein design, symmetric assemblies, and nanoparticles each require different combinations of computational tools, evaluation metrics, and design strategies. We therefore aimed to provide a common interface through which such workflows could be readily constructed, modified, and scaled without rewriting the underlying orchestration logic.


## Blocks and the shared DataFrame

We observed that most biomolecular design tools perform one of two operations: (i) generating new candidates from existing structures, or (ii) annotating existing candidates with additional metrics. Both can be represented through a common DataFrame schema, in which each row is one design and carries its structural history and metrics. We term each operation a block , a lightweight Python class that simply takes as input a DataFrame and returns an extended DataFrame ( Fig. 1 a ). A block appends its output structure to each row’s history and adds its metrics alongside those already present. When a tool produces several candidates from one input, the parent row is duplicated for each output. Each row holds an ordered list of structure records (the producing step, file path and residue-level annotations) and a JSON-encoded collection of metrics, stored in a structured columnar schema built on Polars [ 26 ]. Generation, prediction and scoring blocks all share this interface. This preserves design lineage and keeps a single source of truth throughout a campaign.


## Executing a block ( Fig. 1c )

Each block separates input preparation, execution and output compilation. The tool itself is treated as a black box, so its environment and input/output conventions stay unchanged. Preparation converts the input DataFrame into the files and parameters the tool expects; compilation collects the results and converts them back into the canonical DataFrame. For example, a ProteinMPNN [ 2 ] block prepares backbone inputs, runs sequence design, and attaches the generated sequences and metrics to their parent structures. The execution engine splits the input DataFrame into independent chunks and distributes them across jobs. Each chunk’s compiled output is merged with the original input DataFrame by tracing the lineage for each output to its source based on path handles. Chunks are concatenated when all jobs finish. Intermediate outputs are stored as Parquet files, and incomplete workflows can be resumed from previously completed steps or chunks. Local multiprocessing and HPC job submission run through a custom scheduler built around Submitit [ 27 ].


## Writing and scaling pipelines

A pipeline script can mix common registered blocks with arbitrary custom code to be executed during jobs, including custom operations ( Fig. 1 b ). The syntax for writing pipelines is simple and follows a common pattern; an execution engine receives the configuration of the block to run and executes it with a supplied dataframe (Algorithm 1).

Algorithm 1 Syntax for a basic RFD3-MPNN-AF3 enzyme design pipeline with a custom filtering step. Each call to engine.run applies one or more blocks to a dataframe with one row per design and returns it with new columns for the block outputs. Blocks are looked up by name, instantiated with their arguments, and passed to engine.run. RFD3 first generates backbones from a configuration file (four batches of 16). The dataframe is then filtered with ordinary indexing on the rfd3.insertion_rmsd metric (the custom filter, which can be any ordinary Python), and the surviving designs are passed to a single chained call that runs LigandMPNN sequence design (model_type=“ligand_mpnn”) followed by AF3 structure prediction.

Agent harness Given its programmatic nature, we find Chaperone is particularly well suited as an interface for agentic workflows. An agent harness refers to the set of native tools available to agents including compaction, and context management as well as toolsets, skills and MCPs. In Figure 1 b we extend Chaperone as a harness for agents, providing the Chaperone library as the primary tool-use interface. The harness provides an agent four layers of context for writing and managing protein design workflows:

The abstract scripting layer: tool-independent ways to run design pipelines, interfaces for compute, the DataFrame-to-DataFrame API schema described above.

Documentation of blocks: since every block operates on a standardized schema, the output of each tool can be understood from the code alone, making the construction of the workflow devisable before any runtime execution.

Examples of integrated workflows across design tasks spanning both design tasks as well as tool-use edge-cases such as branching, cycling and templating.

Workflow orchestration in the form of agent skills, for example: effectively monitoring cluster jobs and resources, recovering crashed or incomplete outputs, scripting, debugging inputs, visualisation, setup and scripting.

Additional features of Chaperone are integrated specifically for simplifying workflows with agents: Agents work particularly well inside closed loops with verifiable rewards. Test mode reduces sampling for supported blocks to support small end-to-end checks, ideally using a persistent local GPU for shorter queue times on HPC clusters. This allows agents to adopt a fast build-test loop for complex pipelines ( Fig 1 b , right); efficiently testing and debugging the blocks integrate end-to-end as a fast verifiable signal. The same script can then scale to a full campaign, changed only through command-line overrides.

Additionally, blocks can run as separate steps, with each step’s returned DataFrame passed to the next and results gathered between jobs mimicking usual workflows ( Fig. 1 d , left). Alternatively, several blocks can be bundled into one call ( Fig. 1 d , right): Each chunk runs the multiple processes inside a single job, significantly cutting the scheduling overhead for multi-step workflows as well as improving throughput in cases with straggling processes.


## 3 In silico evaluation

To evaluate the impact of the Chaperone harness on agent-assisted protein design, we developed a de novo protein design evaluation suite spanning a diverse set of tasks and targets. Rigorously evaluating agent performance in this setting is challenging because successful in silico design depends on two intertwined capabilities: (1) the ability to execute and orchestrate complex, multistep tool-calling workflows and (2) the ability to reason about protein structure and make informed design decisions. To evaluate both capabilities, we provided agents with task-specific design specifications and the necessary molecular inputs, allowing them 12 hours and access to GPUs to autonomously develop, execute, evaluate, and iteratively refine their design strategies. At the end of each run, agents were required to submit 96 sequence-diverse candidate designs, which were independently evaluated using held-out scoring pipelines implemented through Chaperone as a benchmarking framework. These pipelines regenerated structures from the submitted sequences and assessed the resulting designs against predefined structural, geometric, and energetic criteria derived from real-world protein design tasks. We compared agent performance with and without access to the Chaperone tool-use harness, measuring the fraction of submitted designs satisfying all required criteria alongside computational resource consumption and token usage.

For our evaluation, baseline agents without the harness were allowed web access to install whichever tools they considered most suitable for accomplishing the design brief, while agents with the Chaperone harness had internet access disabled, as the necessary tools were already available. Evaluations were run using Harbor Controller [ 28 ], with agents given access to a maximum of five B4000 GPUs through a lightweight SLURM scheduler shim to track hourly usage and cost. For evaluations conducted with the harness, we provided a git-state-pruned copy of the Chaperone repository (to prevent leakage through the repository’s history), with relevant overlapping examples removed and containers pre-installed. GPU usage was similarly tracked through an environment shim. This comparison measures the practical benefit of the harness for end-to-end design under the evaluation conditions, although it does not disentangle workflow orchestration from structural reasoning and experimental activity. Additionally, evaluations were conducted on real, shared cluster resources with priority queuing to better reflect realistic computational workflow environments.

Across nine autonomous evaluations encompassing diverse protein design objectives and computational tools, GPT-5.6-sol agents with the Chaperone harness operating at high reasoning effort consumed 86% fewer total tokens and 64% fewer GPU-hours relative to baseline agents operating without the harness. Despite this substantial reduction in computational expenditure, the mean computational design pass rate increased from 6% to 12% ( Fig. 1 f ). These findings indicate that improvements in computational efficiency need not come at the expense of design performance, and suggest that the burden of tool integration and workflow management may itself constitute a substantial bottleneck in autonomous protein design.

To further investigate how orchestration overhead scales with agent reasoning effort, we extended our evaluation across low, medium, and high reasoning settings for both configurations across the same nine tasks above. As expected, increasing reasoning effort was accompanied by greater token consumption, GPU utilization, and agent-issued actions in both settings. However, the magnitude of this increase differed between configurations. Baseline agents exhibited a median increase of 25 million tokens between successive reasoning-effort levels, whereas Chaperone-harnessed agents consumed fewer than 10 million tokens total - even at the highest reasoning setting ( Fig. 1 g ). This divergence suggests that additional reasoning capacity in baseline agents is increasingly consumed by the implementation and maintenance of computational workflows, rather than necessarily translating into more extensive exploration of design strategies. In contrast, Chaperone’s standardized interfaces allow agents to construct, modify, and evaluate increasingly complex workflows without repeatedly regenerating the supporting scaffold. We additionally observed a substantial reduction in repeated job-status queries among Chaperone-harnessed agents, consistent with the availability of standardized job submission, automatic chunking, parallel execution, and failure recovery mechanisms that reduce the need for continuous intervention during long-running computations. Collectively, these observations suggest that the benefits of increased agent reasoning effort are strongly dependent on the computational environment in which the agent operates, with orchestration overhead potentially limiting the extent to which additional reasoning translates into productive scientific exploration.


## 4 Experimental results


## 4.1 Small molecule binder design

Designing high-affinity small-molecule binders remains challenging, with no single computational strategy generalizing effectively across targets. Differences in ligand geometry, interaction networks, and energetics often require extensive exploration of generative models, sequence design methods, and evaluation criteria to identify a suitable workflow. Previous efforts targeting cortisol involved iterative computational design followed by experimental affinity maturation, with Lee et al. ultimately achieving a binding affinity of 68 nM [ 29 ]. We therefore sought to investigate whether Chaperone could accelerate this process by allowing researchers and AI agents to collaboratively explore diverse design hypotheses, evaluate promising strategies through small-scale experiments, and selectively scale the optimal workflow.

We applied this approach to cortisol binder design, beginning with a sweep of 20 protein center-of-mass (COM) placements across four binder lengths, yielding 80 RFD3 runs to explore alternative pocket geometries and ligand contact surface strategies. From these, the researcher selected 10 promising backbone configurations for further sequence optimization, evaluating six customized LigandMPNN [ 7 ] amino-acid composition biases designed to promote hydrophobic packing around the ligand. The top three configurations were then subjected to iterative structure-prediction and refinement cycles, comparing six prediction-oracle combinations and six filtering strategies. Chaperone enabled the agent to execute and monitor 104 small-scale computational runs under designer guidance, progressively narrowing the design space before selecting a final workflow for large-scale generation ( Fig. 2 b,c ).

This exploration went beyond conventional parameter optimization, allowing us to modify the customized pipeline blocks as new hypotheses emerged, without repeatedly implementing the infrastructure needed to connect individual tools. Within two weeks, we progressed from initial design hypotheses to sequences ready for experimental testing. Of 27 candidates tested by fluorescence polarization in the first experimental round, 15 yielded supported affinity fits without quality-control flags, nine yielded flagged fits, and three showed no clear response. The best binder had an affinity of 1.32 nM without any affinity maturation. This result highlights Chaperone’s utility in making extensive methodological exploration practical within a short development cycle, allowing researchers to spend less effort managing computational workflows and more time investigating the design strategies most likely to succeed ( Fig. 2 d,e ).


## 4.2 Nanoparticle design

Designing de novo self-assembling protein nanoparticles has been historically challenging and often consists of many different steps. A recent approach described the design of de novo protein nanoparticles [ 30 ] used the following steps: (a) design de novo symmetric oligomers around a motif with RFdiffusion [ 5 ], (b) dock those oligomers into the desired geometry with RPXdock [ 31 ], (c) design the sequence of the entire nanoparticle with ProteinMPNN, and (d) use AlphaFold [ 10 ] and Rosetta [ 13 ] to score and select constructs. Here, we used a similar approach to design protein nanoparticles with Chaperone as a demanding test case for its ability to incorporate uncommon software, like RPXdock, into a standard pipeline ( Fig. 2 g ).

We aimed to design tetrahedral nanoparticles containing a trimeric coiled-coil motif. By providing the agent with a design outline for the approach previously described, it easily generated a small library of tetrahedral nanoparticles from which we could filter and select 15 designs for experimental validation. Of these, one successfully assembled into a protein nanoparticle, a success rate of 6.7% ( Fig. 2 h,i ). This result demonstrates the ability of Chaperone to translate an established design approach into a streamlined pipeline that produces experimentally validated proteins.


## 4.3 Protein fusion

Rigidity is a central requirement in crystal design, since the lattice only repeats if the components hold the orientation they were given. Designing a rigid linker ( Fig. 2 m ) that scaffolds two symmetric motifs and keeps their relative geometry fixed is therefore the step the rest of the crystal depends on, and the length that achieves it is not known in advance, although most tools require it to be fixed a priori.

We applied this approach to the fusion of C2- and D3-symmetric components, where the agent used RFD3 to build single-chain fusions from prepositioned motifs and customized filters to discard backbones that clash with symmetry mates or connect the motifs too weakly. The prepositioned motifs were generated by a separate rigid-body placement and scoring pipeline developed in our laboratory for de novo protein crystal design (manuscript in preparation), and are used here as fixed inputs. A base pipeline is devised based on the desired constraints ( Fig. 2 k ), and agents undertake pilot experiments to determine ideal length, the ideal ProteinMPNN variant, and whether to adopt the cycling strategy ( Fig. 2 l ). We then selected the ideal parameters and scaled backbone generation to ∼10x the pilot, diversifying the inputs across ∼4,500 placements instead of one. We tested seven lengths per placement, with the window centre taken from the pilot for 4,355 placements (97.9%) and from a recorded fallback for the remaining 94. Both rounds used the same pipeline, with the additional load automatically dispatched by the execution engine.

Designs were refined through templated structure prediction and sequence redesign cycles using CaGE [ 18 ], with specified residues fixed to preserve crystallization interfaces. Classifier-free guidance was annealed over four stages, and a cycled backbone was kept only if its fixed residues stayed within 1.0 Å backbone RMSD of the input. Designs were then evaluated with hexamer AlphaFold3 predictions and researcher-provided analysis scripts. This example demonstrates Chaperone’s ability to settle a design parameter in a disposable pilot and carry the same workflow into production.


## 5 Conclusions

In this work, we showed that generalized design across a range of tasks with freely available protein design tools can be standardised under simple principles. The design of blocks allows for a simple interface that allows rapid integration across a wide array of biomolecular tools. The harness design of Chaperone incorporates multiple optimizations to biomolecular design workflows such as incorporating verification loops for complex pipelines, bundled multi-step jobs with customised augmentation and manipulation of structures at scale. We demonstrate the harness improves agent tool use on real design workflows, owing to simplified orchestration, efficient context management, improved output formatting and job polling, as well as pre-built pipeline patterns. We found the workflows of harnessed agents are more monitorable, owing to their reduced output space, enabling reliable and manageable scaling to several agents or complex tasks.

We demonstrated design with Chaperone across multiple wet-lab validated tasks: We first demonstrated the design of high affinity (low nanomolar) cortisol binders with RFdiffusion3 as a generation oracle, scaled with customised hydrophobic sequence design tailored to the design task. Chaperone enables such a complex workflow to be fully tested and encapsulated by a few dozen lines of code, rather than thousands. We further demonstrated how non-standard design tasks—requiring tools external to the conventional generate-predict pipeline—are particularly well suited for the Chaperone framework: we demonstrate the successful design of a 12-subunit nanoparticle, using autonomous RPXDock parameter tuning. Finally, we demonstrate the task of linker design can be effectively carried out; by tuning key design parameters such as length and pipeline choices such as sequence fitting method. These workflows yielded observed crystals with brightfield microscopy.

We note that rigorously evaluating tool use is compute intensive; evaluations mix stochasticity within the tool calls themselves with the agents and tool use choices. We observed task-by-task variability, for example in protein binder design. As a result, we chose to more deeply evaluate GPT-5.6 Sol with the resources available. We leave a large-scale evaluation of agent tool use across model families and harnesses as directions for future work.

Overall, Chaperone supports a wide range of tasks and enables scientists to tackle ambitious long-horizon design problems with more reliable agents. Additionally, Chaperone can readily be used in a number of additional contexts not explored here; including retrospective analysis, model benchmarking and development. As tools increase in generation and prediction quality, we expect the necessity to effectively orchestrate and couple tools together and to orchestrate long-horizon agentic tasks to become increasingly prominent. To this end, we make Chaperone freely available to the community on GitHub: https://github.com/RosettaCommons/chaperone .

## S2#Wet lab validation of nanoparticles

DNA fragments for expression and screening of protein designs (IPDblocks) were generated from oligonucleotide pools using the IPDassembly process, similar to previous reports. Briefly, DNA sequences encoding designed proteins were bioinformatically fragmented, and each fragment was appended with BsaI restriction sites and design-specific PCR priming sites. These sequences were synthesized within a 350 nucleotide oligonucleotide pool at Twist Bioscience. Two nested PCR stages were conducted to dial-out design-specific fragments within each well of a microwell plate. DNA fragments composing each design were individually combined with LM1371 and assembled via a Golden Gate reaction [ 37 ]. Genes were then transformed into BL21 competent E. coli cells (NEB). Constructs were screened at 1 mL scale where cells were grown in 96-deep well format in autoinduction media [ 38 ] (Terrific Broth II media supplemented with 50×5052, 50×M salts, 20 mM MgSO 4 , and a trace metal mix) at 37 ◦ C for 24 hours at 1350 revolutions per minute (rpm) in a Heidolph shaker. Cells were then spun down at 4000 g for 30 minutes and resuspended in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris pH 8, 250 mM NaCl, 20 mM Imidazole, 200 mM L-Arginine) with the addition of bovine pancreatic DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich) and protease inhibitors (Thermo Scientific). Resuspended cells were then sonicated with a 24-prong sonicator. Lysed cells were centrifuged for 30 minutes at 4000 g and clarified lysate was subsequently poured over equilibrated Ni-NTA resin (QIAGEN) in a gravity column. The columns were washed with 10 column volumes of wash buffer (25 mM Tris pH 8, 250 mM NaCl, 20 mM Imidazole), then mixed with and incubated in elution buffer (25 mM Tris pH 8, 300 mM NaCl, 500 mM Imidazole) for 10 minutes prior to being eluted. Eluant was concentrated to 100 μ L and further purified using Superdex 200 5/150 columns on an HPLC (Agilent 1260 Infinity II LC System) and buffer exchanged into a sizing buffer (25 mM Tris pH 8, 500 mM NaCl, 100 mM L-Arginine) before selecting fractions for confirmed assembly via negative stain electron microscopy.

300-mesh carbon coated grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences) were glow discharged for 30 seconds prior to application of protein samples (3 μ L, 0.1 mg/mL) and blotting three times with 2% (w/v) uranyl formate. Micrographs were acquired using a 120 kV Talos L120C transmission electron microscope (Thermo Scientific) with a BM-Ceta camera.


## S3#Wet lab validation of protein fusion

Selected designs were expressed from a customized pET29b-derived vector with a C-terminal His-tag (LM627, Addgene 191551). Plasmids were transformed into chemically competent E. coli BL21(DE3) cells. Single transformants were used to inoculate 1 mL LB pre-cultures supplemented with 50 μ g/mL kanamycin, which were grown at 37 ◦ C for 16 h. Pre-cultures were used to inoculate expression cultures in autoinduction medium [ 38 ] (Terrific Broth II supplemented with 25× M salts, 25× 5052, 2 mM MgSO 4 , a trace metal mix and 50 μ g/mL kanamycin), distributed as 1 mL per well in 2 mL 96-deep-well plates, for a total of 4 mL per design. Expression cultures were grown at 37 ◦ C for 20 h without a temperature shift. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4,000 × g for 15 min at 16 ◦ C, and the pellets were frozen at −80 ◦ C overnight.

Lysis buffer was prepared by combining 5 mL BugBuster, approximately 100 mg lysozyme, 1 mL bovine pancreatic DNase I, 50 μ L of 1 M MgSO 4 and one protease inhibitor tablet, topped up to 50 mL with wash buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole pH 8). Frozen pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer at 100 μ L per 1 mL of expression culture and lysed by shaking at room temperature for 1 h; no sonication was used. The lysate was then incubated with Ni-NTA beads for 15 min at room temperature. The beads were washed once with wash buffer and once with high-salt wash buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8, 1 M NaCl, 20 mM imidazole pH 8), and bound protein was eluted in elution buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 400 mM imidazole pH 8).

Purified protein was transferred to a black 96-well glass-bottom plate with #1.5 high-performance cover glass (0.17 ± 0.005 mm; Cellvis, P96-1.5H-N) and imaged on an IN Cell Analyzer 2500 HS (Cytiva, formerly GE Healthcare) using bright-field illumination at 60× magnification.


## S4#Wet lab validation of cortisol binder

For binding experiments, Hydrocortisone (Sigma Aldrich H4001, synonym: cortisol) was used as the design target throughout the computation pipeline and as a competitor. TAMRA (5-Carboxytetramethylrhodamine, Thermofisher C6121) was used as a negative control. TAMRA-PEG3-cortisol [ 29 ] was a custom synthesized ligand used for fluorescence activated cell sorting and fluorescence polarization experiments.

Designed protein sequences were reverse-translated using DNAWorks [ 39 ] and codon-optimized for Saccharomyces cerevisiae , excluding common restriction enzyme recognition sites. Each coding sequence was divided into overlapping 5 ′ and 3 ′ fragments, and designs were grouped by length to minimize length-dependent amplification bias. The corresponding oligonucleotides were synthesized as a 300-nt oligonucleotide pool (Twist Bioscience). The 5 ′ and 3 ′ fragments were amplified separately by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using KAPA HiFi HotStart Uracil+ ReadyMix. Inner primer-derived adapter sequences were removed by sequential treatment with USER enzyme and the NEBNext End Repair Module, and corresponding fragments were assembled into full-length inserts by overlap-extension qPCR using KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix. The assembled insert pool (5 μ g) was combined with linearized pETCON3 vector (25 μ g) and introduced into electrocompetent Saccharomyces cerevisiae EBY100 cells following a previously published protocol [ 40 ]. Transformants were cultured in complete synthetic medium lacking tryptophan and uracil and supplemented with 2% glucose (CTUG) at 30 ◦ C for 48-72 h, until an OD 600 of 4-5 was reached. Surface display was subsequently induced in SGCAA medium at 30 ◦ C for 18 h.

All staining and ligand-binding incubations were performed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na 2 HPO 4 , 1.8 mM KH 2 PO 4 , pH 7.4) supplemented with 0.1% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (PBSF). Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) was performed on a Sony SH800S equipped with 488- and 561-nm lasers, using a 100- μ m sorting chip and PBS as the sheath fluid. An initial expression sort was performed to enrich the library for designs displaying detectable levels of the C-terminal c-Myc tag. 20 million induced cells were washed twice with 1 mL PBS, resuspended in PBSF containing FITC-conjugated anti-c-Myc antibody (Immunology Consultants Laboratory, CMYC-45F) at a 1:50 (v/v) dilution, and incubated for 30 min at 4 ◦ C. Following two washes with PBSF, c-Myc-positive cells were collected by FACS, expanded in CTUG medium to an OD 600 of 4-5, and re-induced in SGCAA medium for 18 h.

For binding enrichment, induced cells were stained with FITC-conjugated anti-c-Myc antibody as described above, washed twice with PBSF, and divided into experimental and control aliquots. Experimental aliquots were incubated with 1 μ M TAMRA-PEG3-cortisol, whereas control aliquots were incubated with 1 μ M TAMRA alone, both for 60 min at 4 ◦ C. Cells were subsequently washed twice with PBSF. From the TAMRA-PEG3-cortisol-treated population, FITC-positive cells exhibiting TAMRA fluorescence above the TAMRA-only control were collected, with TAMRA fluorescence measured in the PE detection channel. Two rounds of binding enrichment were performed. For library titrations, cells were incubated with TAMRA-PEG3-cortisol at seven concentrations: 0, 320 pM, 1.6 nM, 8 nM, 40 nM, 200 nM, and 1 μ M and sorted using the same gate across all concentrations. Binding volumes were scaled with ligand concentration to minimize ligand depletion.

Plasmid DNA was isolated from sorted yeast populations using the Zymoprep Yeast Plasmid Miniprep II kit (Zymo Research), and the design-encoding regions were amplified by PCR using indexed primers. Amplicon libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 1000 using 2 × 300-bp paired-end reads. Paired-end reads were assembled and matched to the reference design sequences using a custom analysis pipeline [ 41 ] yielding per-design read counts for each sorted population.

For titration sorts, concentration-dependent enrichment was used to estimate SC 50 as a proxy for apparent dissociation constants ( K D ) for binding following a previously published analysis procedure [ 41 ] . Designs with estimated apparent K D values ≤1 μ M were classified as candidate binders.

DNA fragments encoding candidate binders were ordered as Twist Gene Fragments (Twist Bioscience) with flanking sequences for Golden Gate cloning [ 37 ] using BsaI-HFv2 (New England Biolabs R3733L). Each fragment was individually assembled into the LM670 expression vector (Addgene plasmid 191552), and the assembly products were transformed into chemically competent Escherichia coli BL21 cells (Intact Genomics 1054-81). Each design was expressed in four parallel 1-mL cultures distributed across four 96-deep-well plates (Fisher Scientific PDW20CSIND). Cultures were grown in autoinduction medium [ 38 ] consisting of Terrific Broth II (Fisher Scientific MP113046052) supplemented with 1x 5052 solution and 1 mM MgSO 4 at 37 ◦ C for 4 h, followed by 20 ◦ C for 24 h, with shaking at 1,350 rpm on a Heidolph orbital shaker. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4,000 × g for 15 min, and pellets were stored at -20 ◦ C overnight. Pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (100 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, and 25 mM imidazole) supplemented with BugBuster 10x Protein Extraction Reagent diluted to 1x (MilliporeSigma 70921-5) bovine pancreatic DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich DN255G), and protease inhibitor tablet (Thermo Fisher A32965). Following resuspension, the four samples corresponding to each design were pooled. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 4,000 × g for 30 min. Each clarified lysate was applied to 100 μ L of pre-equilibrated Ni-NTA resin (QIAGEN 30250-A) in a 96-well plate with 25- μ m frits (Agilent 200953-100). The resin was washed with 10 column volumes of wash buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl, and 25 mM imidazole). Proteins were eluted by incubating the resin with elution buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl, and 250 mM imidazole) for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 500 x g for 2 min. Eluates were filtered through a 0.2- μ m 96-well filter plate (Cytiva 8586) and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) using a Superdex 75 Increase 5/150 GL column (Cytiva 29148722) on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II LC system. SEC was performed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na 2 HPO 4 , and 1.8 mM KH 2 PO 4 , pH 7.4).

Purified proteins were used directly to prepare dilution series in PBS. Stock concentrations were determined from absorbance measurements at 280 nm using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Fluorescence polarization (FP) assays were performed in 96-well plates (Corning 3686). Each well initially contained 50 μ L PBS, and 25 μ L purified protein stock was added to the first well of each dilution series. Following mixing, 25 μ L was transferred sequentially between wells to generate a 12-point, threefold serial dilution. Subsequently, 50 μ L of 2 nM TAMRA-PEG3-cortisol was added to each well, yielding a final assay volume of 100 μ L and a ligand concentration of 1 nM. The initial screen used one dilution series per protein; the four selected proteins were subsequently measured using three technical replicate dilution series.

FP was measured using an Agilent BioTek Synergy Neo2 plate reader with excitation at 530 nm (25 nm bandwidth) and emission at 590 nm (20 nm bandwidth) using a red filter cube (Agilent 1035062). Measurements were collected using top-read optics, a gain setting of 120 and a read height of 7.5 mm, with ten instrument measurements per data point. The recorded reader temperature was approximately 24 ◦ C. Parallel and perpendicular fluorescence intensities and polarization values in millipolarization units (mP) were exported for analysis.

For competition measurements of all candidate binders, 10 μ L of 1 mM unlabelled ligand was added to each well following the initial FP measurement. This increased the total volume to 110 μ L, yielding final concentrations of 90.9 μ M unlabelled ligand and 0.909 nM TAMRA-labelled ligand, with a corresponding dilution of protein concentrations by a factor of 100/110. The same wells were measured shortly after competitor addition using identical reader settings.
