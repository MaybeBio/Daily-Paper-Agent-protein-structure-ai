## Abstract

Rapid advances in protein structure design have not translated to intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs). This is largely due to our incomplete understanding of how IDR sequence encodes function. Here, we use stem cell differentiation as a model to map functional sequence features in the IDR of the transcription factor RUNX2. We show that RUNX2 requires its C-terminal IDR to bind chromatin and regulate gene expression during osteogenesis. Surprisingly, linear IDR motifs are dispensable, and instead features of the conformational ensemble predict function. We develop an algorithm for rational IDR design based on biophysical principles, and use it to generate functional IDRs de novo . These findings demonstrate the central role for TF IDRs in chromatin binding and extend generative protein design beyond the folded proteome.


## Introduction

Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have enabled prediction and design of folded proteins; however, these methods are blind to the ∼35% of the human proteome that lack a well-defined structure ( 1 – 3 ). While recent approaches facilitate IDR design against linear sequence features or predicted physico-chemical sequence parameters, the appropriate target for biologically functional IDR design is often ill-defined, as sequence-to-function models of IDRs have yet to be resolved and integrated ( 4 – 9 ).

IDRs encode biological function at different levels. First, short linear interaction motifs (SLiMs) ( 10 – 12 ), are precise sequences of 3-15 amino acids that can bind folded interactors via coordination of motif amino acids in structured binding pockets ( 13 ). However, recent work shows that chemically specific, multivalent interactions between IDRs, and binding partner surfaces can augment and even replace motif-based interactions ( 14 ). These “fuzzy” complexes explain how transient, distributed IDR-structure or IDR-IDR interactions can give rise to well-defined molecular interactions ( 15 – 20 ). Molecular grammars – non-random amino acid compositions and patterning – have been proposed to define the chemically specific interactions that mediate such transient interactions, but how sequence grammar encodes interaction specificity remains an open question ( 9 , 21 – 23 ). Finally, the biophysical features of IDR conformational ensembles, for instance their global dimensions (e.g. the radius of gyration) and local dynamics (e.g. transient helicity) also impact function ( 3 , 24 – 26 ). Each of these three regulatory layers imposes distinct constraints on IDR sequence space that can be distinguished by coupling rational sequence perturbation with functional assays ( 7 ).

Transcription factors (TFs) are disproportionately disordered; IDRs account for ∼55% of residues in human TFs (Fig. S1A) ( 27 ). Historically, TFs have been treated as modular units in which structured DNA-binding domains (DBDs) direct binding to specific DNA sequence motifs while flanking IDRs encode effector activity: the recruitment of co-factors that activate or repress nearby genes, or modify chromatin locally ( 28 , 29 ). This paradigm has guided large-scale, reductionist approaches to characterize either the DNA sequence motifs of isolated DBDs or the effector activity of isolated TF IDRs ( 16 , 28 , 30 – 36 ). While these approaches have been successful, mounting evidence suggests they are incomplete. First, TF IDRs can impact chromatin binding in cells: both IDR-IDR and IDR-DNA interactions have been proposed to guide DBDs to genomic binding sites ( 37 – 40 ). Second, effector activity screens that recruit IDRs to a reporter gene often do not generalize across promoters and cell types; some IDRs even flip between activation and repression depending on the context ( 32 , 34 , 36 , 41 ). While these studies have yielded models that predict general transcriptional activation domains found in IDRs from viral or stress TFs, we noticed that they miss activity in many TFs that regulate cell differentiation during vertebrate development (Fig. S1B-C). Indeed, several key TFs that direct skeletal development, including RUNX2, the master regulator of osteogenesis, were not active in any recent reporter screens despite previous reports of effector function in the literature (Fig. S1D-E) ( 29 ). These observations suggest that for certain transcriptional programs, cellular context may be an essential determinant of TF function.

Cell differentiation involves orchestrating the expression of many genes, often in both directions, within a dynamic cellular context ( 42 , 43 ). These complex biological requirements mean that TF IDR functions evolve under a large network of constraints unlikely to be entirely captured by studying a single reporter gene in a foreign cellular context. We therefore sought to investigate TF function within its native context.

Here we developed a platform to measure integrated IDR function in a robust and quantitative differentiation phenotype, using osteoblast differentiation from mesenchymal stem cells driven by the TF RUNX2 as a model. We show that the RUNX2 C-terminal IDR (CIDR) is required for differentiation and mediates RUNX2 association with chromatin. Despite high evolutionary conservation, we found that linear IDR motifs are dispensable for CIDR function. Using molecular dynamics simulations, we identified features of the CIDR conformational ensemble predicting its potential to drive differentiation and used them to design de novo RUNX2 IDRs that complement WT activity.

Together, these findings demonstrate that specific biological function can be encoded in IDRs via rational design and provide a roadmap to probe IDR sequence space unexplored by nature.


## Results


## The RUNX2 CIDR is required for osteogenesis

RUNX2-regulated osteogenesis offers an attractive model to study TF IDR functions. First, RUNX2 loss-of-function leads to a dramatic phenotype: mice lacking RUNX2 fail to mineralize the skeleton, while in humans, RUNX2 mutations cause the congenital bone disease cleidocranial dysplasia (CCD) ( 44 – 46 ). Additionally, osteoblast differentiation from human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSC) is a well-characterized system that produces a robust and quantifiable mineralization phenotype in vitro ( Fig. 1A ) ( 47 , 48 ). Finally, RUNX2 contains a large, poorly characterized C-terminal IDR (CIDR), the truncation of which causes CCD ( Fig. 1B ) ( 49 ).

We developed an osteogenesis rescue assay to measure the activity of RUNX2 CIDR variants. We first generated RUNX2 knockout (KO) hMSC clones that could not differentiate into osteoblasts (Fig. S2A-E). We then generated RUNX2 rescue lines stably expressing inducible RUNX2 (iRUNX2) variants by piggybac integration of the transgenes into the RUNX2 KO clones ( Fig. 1C ). We induced RUNX2 expression with doxycycline and osteoblast differentiation with an established cocktail (see methods), and quantified the mineralization phenotype on day 14 by staining cells with 800CW BoneTag (a calcium-chelating compound conjugated to near-infrared fluorescent dye) and reading out well fluorescence ( Fig. 1D ). Importantly, we fused a FLAG tag to each of the RUNX2 variants to confirm consistent expression levels and nuclear localization on day 4 of differentiation via immunofluorescence, and measured induction of the osteoblast marker ALPL by RT-qPCR. We first benchmarked the system with WT human RUNX2, a nonfunctional DBD mutant identified in CCD patients (R193G) ( 50 ), and a deletion of the CIDR (ΔCIDR) ( Fig. 1E ). While WT iRUNX2 rescue cells produced a robust mineralization phenotype, neither R193G nor ΔCIDR rescues were distinguishable from KO, despite correct expression and localization of the variant (Fig. S3A-C), indicating the RUNX2 CIDR is required for osteoblast differentiation.


## Effector domain IDRs from other TFs do not complement RUNX2 CIDR function

We first tested whether RUNX2 variants containing a strong transcriptional activator (VP16) ( 51 ), a transcriptional repressor (ZIM3) ( 32 ), or a bifunctional domain (ARGFX, shown to activate or repress depending on the promoter) ( 33 ) in place of the CIDR could rescue osteogenic activity. Interestingly, although both the VP16 and ARGFX variants activated the osteoblast marker gene ALPL similar to WT RUNX2 early in differentiation, all three variants failed to rescue the mineralization phenotype ( Fig. 1F , S4A-C). Only the ARGFX fusion showed measurable mineralization, but was inefficient compared to RUNX2 CIDR. These results support the idea that individual reporter assays might not fully capture TF IDR biological functions.

Because replacing the CIDR with unrelated effector domains did not rescue osteogenesis, we reasoned that CIDR activity may depend on cellular context, i.e., sets of co-regulators or a chromatin environment specific to hMSCs. hMSCs are multipotent and can also differentiate into adipocytes or chondrocytes, driven by CEBPB and SOX9, respectively ( 52 , 53 ). We reasoned that if IDRs taken from these hMSC-active TFs contained context-specific sequence features, they might rescue RUNX2 activity. However, IDRs from CEBPB and SOX9 failed to fully rescue osteogenesis when fused to RUNX2 in place of the CIDR, although the SOX9 IDR had mild activity ( Fig. 1F , S4A-C). These results suggest that distinct, program-specific functional determinants underlie CIDR function.

Finally, previous work has implicated the yeast TF MSN2 IDR in dictating genomic binding ( 38 ). Importantly, the molecular grammar underlying this binding appears to depend on distributed hydrophobic residues across the IDR ( 54 ). To investigate whether this conferred a generic chromatin-binding function, we tested it in our assay. However, substituting the RUNX2 CIDR for the MSN2 IDR did not rescue osteogenesis ( Fig. 1F , S4A-C).

Together, these results demonstrate that general effector activity, hMSC-specific activity, and DNA-binding are each insufficient to rescue the osteogenic activity of the RUNX2 CIDR. Thus the RUNX2 CIDR appears to encode a context-specific activity required for osteogenesis. We next sought to determine the specific sequence features underlying this activity.


## Degenerate local sequence features are sufficient for RUNX2 CIDR function

We suspected that the RUNX2 CIDR may encode a specific interaction in a short linear motif. In practice, these motifs mediate site-specific binding defined by intolerance to sequence scrambling, which would disrupt coordination of amino acids in a binding pocket, while avoiding the potential confounder of perturbing IDR composition ( Fig. 2A ) ( 14 ). To identify linear motifs within the RUNX2 CIDR ( Fig. 2B ), we divided the CIDR into 28 contiguous tiles of 8-11 amino acids and generated a series of RUNX2 variants in which each construct contained a scrambled version of one tile (Fig. S5A). To our surprise, all 28 variants successfully drove osteogenesis ( Fig. 2C , S5B-C), including constructs whose scrambled tiles overlapped three previously characterized motifs with proposed molecular functions ( 55 , 56 ). These results unambiguously demonstrate that CIDR function does not depend exclusively on a single linear motif, in contrast to prior models of RUNX2 function.

While individual tile scrambles rule out the essentiality of any one CIDR motif, there remains the possibility for motif redundancy, where loss of one motif is compensated by a different motif elsewhere in the IDR ( Fig. 2D ). Because testing all tile combinations was infeasible, we devised a “local scramble” strategy to test for motif redundancy ( Fig. 2E ). We concatenated all CIDR scrambled tiles into one sequence (LocScr1), generating a single sequence that lacks any contiguous wild-type subregion (Fig. S6A). To our surprise, this variant was functional ( Fig. 2F , S7A). To confirm this result, we generated three additional local scramble CIDR variants by shifting the tile boundaries and independently scrambling the tiles. We also generated “full scramble” variants in which we scrambled the entire CIDR sequence without preserving residue composition within local windows (Fig. S6A). Each local scramble variant was functional, although one had a partial loss of function, while full scramble variants were not ( Fig. 2F , S7A-C). Taken together, these results show that the RUNX2 CIDR encodes activity that – surprisingly – does not require its exact amino acid sequence order.

In general, we found that CIDR function is preserved as long as local amino acid composition is preserved. This implicates chemically specific interactions, as opposed to site-specific interactions, as playing a crucial role in RUNX2 CIDR function.


## Both repulsive and attractive domains are required for RUNX2 CIDR function

Because short linear motifs did not appear to account for RUNX2 CIDR function, we investigated the CIDR in terms of biophysical properties. Recent advances in coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations of IDRs ( 57 – 60 ) have revealed a rich, broad, sequence-encoded conformational landscape, enabling semi-quantitative insights into how IDR sequences dictate intra– and inter-molecular interactions ( 6 , 61 – 63 ). We performed molecular dynamics simulations of the RUNX2 CIDR and quantified inter-residue contacts in the conformational ensemble ( Fig. 3A , S8). We identified a “linker” / “patch” subdomain architecture in which four attractive patches (P1-4) were enriched for inter-residue contacts, while four repulsive linker regions (L1-4) were depleted of contacts ( Fig. 3B ). The patches were enriched for aromatic amino acids, which are known to drive inter-residue interactions in IDRs. To test the requirement of these linker and patch subdomains for RUNX2 CIDR function, we generated CIDR variants that neutralized the repulsive interactions in each linker with polar Glycine-Serine repeats of the same length (L1mut, L2mut, L3mut, L4mut), and variants that neutralized the attractive interactions in each patch by replacing aromatic residues with glycines (P1mut, P2mut, P3mut, P4mut). Interestingly, these variants showed a range of osteogenic potential. Among patches, P1mut and P2mut led cells to full differentiation, P4mut showed a partial loss of function, and P3mut was completely nonfunctional ( Fig. 3C , S9). While L1mut and L3mut rescued differentiation, L2mut and L4mut RUNX2 showed a strong loss of function ( Fig. 3D , S9).

Together, these results show that the RUNX2 CIDR contains both attractive and repulsive subdomains that are required for its function. As L2mut and P3mut led to severe differentiation disruption despite opposing effects on the conformational ensemble (neutralization of respectively a repulsive vs. an attractive subdomain), we chose to further characterize the molecular mechanisms of loss of CIDR function in these two mutants.


## RUNX2 CIDR is required to bind motif clusters in inaccessible chromatin

While TF IDRs have largely been assumed to recruit co-regulators to the DNA-bound TF, several recent studies have shown that TF IDRs impact chromatin binding in cells ( Fig. 4A ) ( 37 , 38 , 40 ). Single molecule tracking (SMT) directly measures the binding of TFs to chromatin in living cells by measuring the fraction of freely diffusing to near-stationary molecules in the nucleus ( Fig. 4B S10); it has the advantage over genomics approaches of capturing overall chromatin-TF association, i.e. both transient chromatin-scanning episodes and motif-binding events are captured ( 64 ). We generated hMSC lines expressing HaloTag fusions of RUNX2 WT, R193G (loss of function CCD mutation in the DBD), ΔCIDR, LocScr1, L2mut, and P3mut and performed SMT (Fig S10A-C). We also generated control lines expressing H2B– and NLS-HaloTag fusions as controls for chromatin-bound and free molecules. For both functional RUNX2 variants, WT and LocScr1, the slow-diffusing fraction was near the H2B control, indicating most molecules were chromatin-bound ( Fig. 4C ). Surprisingly, deleting the CIDR effectively abolished binding; this effect was even stronger than the R193G DBD mutant. L2mut and P3mut exhibited chromatin occupancy intermediate between those of RUNX2 WT and ΔCIDR. These results indicate that the RUNX2 CIDR is required for chromatin binding, and that perturbations to either repulsive linker or attractive patch subdomains impair this behavior.

We next performed RNAseq and ChIP-seq on differentiating cells expressing the same set of RUNX2 CIDR variants to determine whether genome-wide chromatin binding and gene regulation were impacted ( Fig. 4D ). Consistent with SMT data, the ΔCIDR variant lost the majority of ChIP-seq peaks, L2mut and P3mut showed a partial loss, and LocScr1 bound a similar number of sites relative to WT ( Fig. 4E ). The first component (PC1) from a principal components analysis (PCA) on differentially bound peaks was strongly correlated with mineralization phenotype ( Fig. 4F , Fig. S11A). This was also true of the PCA on differentially expressed genes ( Fig. 4G , S11B), which were enriched for genes involved in osteogenesis (Fig S11C-E), supporting a direct correlation between loss of chromatin association and failure to differentiate. De novo DNA motif discovery revealed that each variant bound the consensus RUNX2 motif, though with slight variations ( Fig. 4H ) ( 65 ). The RUNX2 DNA motif derived from in vitro SELEX experiments on the isolated DBD differs from the ChIP-seq motif in the underspecification of a central T ( 35 , 66 ). The ΔCIDR motif matched the SELEX-derived motif while other variants matched the ChIP-seq motif, suggesting that this slight motif variation is caused by the presence of CIDR rather than experimental context. Flanking residues were also more strongly specified in the binding-deficient CIDR variants, suggesting that the CIDR can facilitate access to weaker motifs.

As all CIDR variants bound the consensus RUNX2 motif, we asked whether other genomic features were associated with differential binding. We found mild enrichment for CEBPB and KLF5 motifs in peaks that gained binding by ΔCIDR, P3mut, and L2mut (Fig. S12A). However, the strongest signal in peaks that lost binding in variants was for RUNX2 motifs. This indicated that binding defects were unlikely to result from a lost interaction with a preferred sequence-specific TF partner. To determine whether chromatin state impacted CIDR-dependent binding, we analyzed DNase accessibility in undifferentiated hMSCs prior to the onset of RUNX2 activity ( 47 ). When we compiled DNase accessibility at WT RUNX2 ChIP-seq peaks along with the RUNX2 motif count under those peaks, we noticed two populations: high accessibility, low motif count vs. low accessibility, high motif count ( Fig. 4I ). ΔCIDR, L2mut, and P3mut lost binding at low accessibility, high motif count sites and gained binding at high accessibility, low motif sites ( Fig. 4J , Fig. S13A). LocScr1, on the other hand, bound more to low accessibility sites irrespective of motif count. A linear model showed motif count and accessibility were both predictive of differential ΔCIDR, L2mut, and P3mut binding, whereas only accessibility was predictive of differential LocScr1 binding (Fig. S13B).

Together, our data suggest a model in which the RUNX2 CIDR is required for RUNX2 to bind clustered motifs in closed chromatin to drive the osteogenic gene expression program.


## De novo design of functional RUNX2 CIDRs

Because our data demonstrate that linear IDR motifs are not required for RUNX2 CIDR activity, we wondered whether we could instead leverage sequence-encoded biophysical properties to design functional synthetic IDRs de novo . We compared the conformational ensembles obtained from molecular dynamics simulations on the scrambled, linker, and patch mutants to the WT CIDR ensemble and found that perturbations in interresidue distances in each variant were highly correlated with mineralization phenotype ( Fig. 5A-B , S14A-C). This suggested that the RUNX2 CIDR ensemble could provide a tractable design target to produce functional sequences.

IDR conformational ensembles are driven by non-covalent chemical interactions between amino acid side chains ( 3 , 67 – 71 ). The recently published tool FINCHES uses the non-bonded interaction strength parameters from molecular dynamics force fields to estimate chemical attractiveness between subdomains of arbitrary IDR sequences ( 8 ). These interaction maps (intermaps) provide a proxy for the biophysical ensemble that can be constructed in milliseconds. This computational efficiency enables using intermap similarity as a generative design target to approximate constraints on the conformational ensemble ( Fig. 5C ).

To design de novo IDRs with CIDR-like ensembles, we combined FINCHES with the IDR design tool, GOOSE ( 7 ). Using FINCHES intermaps as a design constraint within GOOSE’s SequenceOptimizer functionality, we can design synthetic IDRs to match a desired FINCHES intermap along with any number of additional constraints. Specifically, we generated de novo IDRs satisfying the following constraints: maximal disorder, minimal sequence identity to WT CIDR, and maximal correlation to the WT CIDR FINCHES intermap ( Fig. 5D ). We generated one batch of sequences with fixed amino acid composition (i.e. same amino acid proportions as CIDR) and one with free composition (i.e. arbitrary amino acids). We expressed six variants (three in each category) and confirmed they localized to the nucleus similarly to WT RUNX2.

Remarkably, two synthetic variants – one of fixed and one of free composition – were fully functional, matching the success rates reported for state-of-the-art structured binder design ( Fig. 5E ) ( 72 ). These results establish that de novo IDRs built using the conformational ensemble as a design blueprint can perform the work of a native effector domain and drive cell differentiation.


## Discussion

Here, we used stem cell differentiation as a model to map the functional sequence elements of a TF IDR. We showed that features of the RUNX2 CIDR conformational ensemble predict osteogenic activity, while linear sequence motifs are dispensable. We identified ensemble CIDR features required for binding to chromatin and generated synthetic IDR variants de novo using a generative design algorithm built on these biophysical principles.

While small linear motifs mediate IDR function in many contexts, reductionist assays have suggested that degenerate sequence features (enrichment for acidity and hydrophobicity) are sufficient for TF IDRs to activate a reporter gene ( 16 , 20 , 73 – 75 ). Although these classic activation domain sequence features are largely absent from the RUNX2 CIDR, we similarly find that – based on direct test of shuffle variants – site-specific binding motifs are dispensable for RUNX2 CIDR function, assayed here in its native molecular and cellular context. Our work thus establishes that despite 99% human-mouse CIDR conservation, de novo CIDR sequences designed to match the WT conformational ensemble are competent to orchestrate an entire differentiation program. We nevertheless note that, given we only probed in vitro differentiation and given RUNX2 has pleiotropic functions ( 76 – 78 ), we cannot exclude the possibility that the CIDR function might require linear motifs in other contexts. Moreover, we suggest that this potential ability to multiplex chemically specific (as reported here) and site-specific (motif-based) interactions may be key to enabling IDRs to engage in multiple orthogonal modes of molecular and cellular function.

Interestingly, all locally scrambled CIDR variants tested here were active, while several de novo designs were completely dead. This suggests that functional CIDR sequence features are captured by local amino acid composition, but not by the conformational ensemble alone. One likely explanation for this is that the coarse-grained models imperfectly capture IDR intra-molecular interactions and conformational biases. Another (non-mutually exclusive) possibility is that redundant, degenerate motifs are preserved at some frequency by local scramble designs, while they are unlikely to emerge in a synthetic sequence by chance. Finally, the conformational ensemble might confer a permissive 3D context for fuzzy interactions which rely on specific residues (thus composition-specific). This would explain why local scramble variants – which conserve both conformational ensemble and local composition – preserve function. On the other hand, some synthetic variants, while retaining the correct conformational ensemble, might lack key residues in their required context, and thus be defective. Distinguishing between these possibilities will require better biophysical models and high-throughput functional screens capable of identifying degenerate linear sequence features.

Our work adds to a growing body of evidence that IDRs are required for many TFs to effectively bind chromatin in cells ( 39 , 79 ). While the ΔCIDR RUNX2 variant could still bind its motif, it was only found in the most open chromatin regions. How the CIDR potentiates binding to less accessible chromatin is an open question; mechanisms ranging from direct DNA contacts to interactions with protein partners have been proposed for other TFs ( 39 ). Since CIDR favors RUNX2 association with multiple RUNX2 motifs, it is possible that the CIDR might mediate homotypic interactions between RUNX2 molecules, thus increasing RUNX2 avidity at motif clusters ( 80 ). Regulatory specificity may thus emerge from two matched codes: the physicochemical patterning of the CIDR (which sets what interactions are possible) and the motif architecture of the genome (which sets where those interactions can occur).

Decades of structural biology are built on the axiom that sequence encodes structure, and structure equals function ( 81 ). This vision has culminated in AI-accelerated protein structure design, making the de novo design of protein binders routine and possible, even in non-expert labs. Yet, since the mechanisms relating sequence to function are still being defined in the unstructured proteome ( 3 ), IDR design has lagged behind ( 82 ). Our ensemble-guided framework extends de novo protein engineering into the disordered proteome, yielding synthetic IDRs that support cell differentiation, and open the door to encoding new functions for stem cell reprogramming.

## Supplementary material


## Materials and methods


## Cell culture

ASC52telo (ATCC, SCRC-4000) immortalized human mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) were maintained in DMEM (Gibco, 11-965-092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (VWR, 97068-086) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122) at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. Cells were passaged using TrypLE Express Enzyme (Gibco, 12563011) and were routinely tested for mycoplasma.


## Osteoblast differentiation

MSCs were seeded 2 days before inducing differentiation at 1000 cells/mL. Osteoblast differentiation media (growth media with 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 10 mM sodium beta-glycerophosphate, 10 nM 1α, 25-Dihydroxy-Vitamin D 3 , and 10 nM dexemethasone) was prepared fresh before the start of each experiment. Doxycycline was added to osteoblast media at 1µg/mL for the first two days to induce RUNX2 expression in all experiments involving iRUNX2 rescue cell lines. Osteoblast media was refreshed every two days for fourteen days during differentiation.


## Generation of RUNX2 knockout lines

RUNX2 knockout cells were generated using a chemically modified crRNA targeting exon 6 of RUNX2 (protospacer 5′-GTATGAGAGTAGGTGTCCCG-3′; IDT Alt-R CRISPR-Cas9 crRNA custom synthesis) using the Alt-R CRISPR-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) system (Integrated DNA Technologies, IDT) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNPs were delivered to 100,000 cells using the P1 S nucleofector kit (Lonza, V4XP-1032) according to manufacturers instructions. Cells were recovered and expanded for one week and then cloned by limiting dilution in 96-well plates. Clones were verified by western blot and genomic PCR of the region surrounding the gRNA cut site (forward primer 5′-CCGAGACCAACAGAGTCAGTGAGTGCTCTCT-3′, reverse 5′-GTTTGCTCAAATATTGGGAGCTGCCGGAGTCAATAAT-3′) followed by sequencing (plasmidsaurus).


## Immunoblotting

WT and RUNX2 KO MSCs were lysed on day 4 of osteoblast differentiation in 200 uL RIPA buffer (Thermo, 89901) containing protease inhibitor tablets (Roche, 04693116001). Cells were scraped and lysate was collected and sonicated on low power at 4C for 3x pulses of 10 sec each. Lysates were then spun at 16000g for 5 min and supernatant was collected and stored at –80C. Lysates were denatured by adding NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (Invitrogen, NP0007) with NuPAGE Sample Reducing Agent (Invitrogen, NP0009) to 1x and incubating at 70 °C for 10 min. 20 uL of each sample was loaded onto a NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris gel (Invitrogen, NP0322BOX) in NuPAGE MOPS SDS Running Buffer (Invitrogen, NP0001) and run at 200V for 30 min. Gels were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using the TransBlot semi-dry system (BioRad, 1703940) on the mixed molecular weight setting. Membranes were blocked with 5% milk in TBST (20 mM tris-HCL, 150 mM NaC, 0.1% Tween) for 1 hr at room temp. Membranes were then stained with 1:1000 anti-RUNX2 (D1L7F, Cell Signaling, 12556T) and 1:1000 anti-TUB1A1 (Thermo, SAB4200776) in milk for 1 hr at room temp. Membranes were washed 3x in TBST and stained with 1:20,000 800CW goat anti-mouse and 1:20000 680RD goat anti-rabbit antibodies (Licor, 926-32210 and 926-68071) for 1 hr at room temp. Membranes were washed 3x in TBST and imaged on a Licor Clx imager in both 700 and 800 nm channels.


## Generation of iRUNX2 rescue lines

Plasmids containing RUNX2 variants under a doxycycline inducible protomotor and an upstream PGK promoter expression cassette containing rTTA and the puromycin resistance gene were delivered to MSCs by electroporation using a NEPA21 electrokinetic transfer system (Bulldog Bio). For all rescue experiments, RUNX2 was N-terminally FLAG tagged. For single molecule tracking experiments HaloTag was fused to the N-terminus with a glycine-serine linker. Cells were dissociated with TrypLE, washed 2x with 5 mL optimem, and 250,000 cells per sample were resuspended in 20 uL optimem. 1.5 ug of RUNX2 variant plasmid, 1 ug of piggybac transposase plasmid, and 1 uL of 10 mg/mL polyglutamic acid were added to each electroporation reaction. 20 µL of the reaction mixture was transferred to a 2mm electroporation cuvette (Bulldog bio, 12358-346) and electroporated using the settings 100V, 5 msec pulse length, 2 pulses, 10% decay rate, + polarity for the poring pulse and 20V, 50 msec pulse length, 50 msec pulse delay, 5 pulses, 40% decay rate, +/− polarity for the transfer pulse. Cells were resuspended in growth media and transferred to 6-well dishes to recover for 4 days. Puromycin was added at 0.75 µg/mL in growth media for 14 days to select for stable integrants.


## Mineralization assay

On day 14 of differentiation cells were washed 2x with PBS, and stained with 50 nM 800 CW BoneTag (Licor, 926-09375) in PBS for 10 min. Cells were washed 3x with 100 uL PBS and then imaged in 100 uL HBSS on a Licor CLx imager with focus set to 4 mm. BoneTag signal in each well was quantified using the in-cell-western western settings in the Licor software. For initial characterization of parental and KO RUNX2 clones FAM-ZOL dye was used to stain differentiated osteoblasts using the same protocol as described for BoneTag staining, and cells were imaged on a Cytation 5 multimodal plate imager using the 488 channel.


## Immunofluorescence

For all immunofluorescence experiments cells were seeded into #1.5 coverglass bottom 96-well plates (Cellvis, P961.5HN) as described for osteoblast differentiation. On day 4 of differentiation cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS. Cells were permeabilized with 0.5% triton in PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were then blocked in PBS containing 0.1% tween-20 and 1% bovine serum albumin (blocking solution) for 30 min at room temperature and stained with 1:1000 mouse anti-FLAG M2 (Sigma, F3165) for rescue experiments or rabbit anti-RUNX2 (D1L7F, Cell Signaling, 12556T) in blocking solution (1% BSA in PBST). Cells were washed 3x with PBST and stained with 1:5000 secondary antibody in blocking solution. Goat anti-mouse 647 (Thermo, A-21235) was used with anti-FLAG and goat anti-rabbit 647 (Thermo, A-21244) with anti-RUNX2. Cells were washed 3x with PBST and stained with 1 µg/mL Hoescht for 10 min in PBS. Cells were imaged in PBS on a Nikon Spinning disk confocal microscope using JOBS to automate multiwell plate acquisition.


## Single molecule tracking

HaloTag-RUNX2 fusion cell lines were seeded onto #1.5 coverglass bottom 96-well plates (Cellvis, P961.5HN) two days before imaging and induced with 1 ug/mL doxycycline one day before imaging. Cells were stained with 10 nM PA-JF549 dye (for single molecule tracking) and 100 nM JFX650 dye (to read out bulk RUNX2 concentration) in growth media for 20 min at 37C ( 83 , 84 ). Cells were washed 3x with growth media and intubated for an additional 15 min at 37C. Media was replaced with FluoroBrite (Gibco A1896701) imaging media with 10% FBS and pen/strep. SMT was performed on a custom built microscope with HILO illumination using lasers emitting at 405 nm, 561 nm, and 647 nm ( 85 ). Light was collected through a Nikon CFI SR HP Plan Apo 100x oil-immersion lens (NA=1.35) onto two Photometrics Prime 95B cameras. The microscope was controlled with Micro-Manager 2.0.3 ( 86 ). Cameras were read out in 12-bit with a 110 × 110 pixel region centred on the nucleus of each cell. Before each movie, one image of the 637 channel was taken to quantify RUNX2 expression level (20 mW laser power, 10 ms exposure). Each cell was then imaged continuously for 6,000 frames in the 561 channel (100 mW laser power, 10 ms exposure). The sample was illuminated continuously with the 405 nm laser during acquisition to photoactivate PA-JF-549. The 405 nm laser was passed through a neutral density filter and laser power was adjusted per sample to keep the number of molecules per frame low (1-5 mW). At least four cells per variant were imaged each day and three independent experiments were performed. Frames in the 561 channel were binned 2 × 2 by summing pixels, and spots were detected and linked with trackpy ( 87 ). Trajectories with fewer than 3 localizations were discarded. Trajectories from each cell were analysed with saspt to estimate the bound fraction and diffusion coefficient of free molecules ( 88 ).


## CHIPseq

Three iRUNX2 rescue lines per FLAG-tagged variant were seeded at 1000 cells/mL into 15 cm tissue culture treated plastic dishes 2 days prior to osteoblast differentiation. Cells were induced with 1µg/mL doxycycline in osteoblast media on day 0 as described above. On day 2 cells were switched to osteoblast media with dox. On day 4 cells were harvested for CHIPseq. 5 million cells per cell line were used as input to CHIPseq.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and library prep was performed essentially as described ( 89 ). Protease inhibitors (Roche, cOmplete; 25×) were added fresh to all lysis and sonication buffers immediately before use. Cell lines were expanded in growth media in 3 15 cm dishes per line. Around 5 million cells were collected per cell line. Cells were dissociated with TrypLE, pelleted at 300 rcf for 4 min, and resuspended in 10 mL PBS. Di(N-succinimidyl) glutarate (DSG) was added to 2mM and cells were incubated for 15 min. 35 mL of PBS was added and then 5 mL of cold freshly prepared fixation solution (50 mM HEPES, 100mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM EGTA, 11% formaldehyde) for a final concentration of 1% formaldehyde. Cells were incubated for 15 min at room temperature and the reaction was quenched with 2.5 mL of 2.5 M glycine. Cells were spun at 1100 rcf for 6 min at 4C, buffer was aspirated and the pellet was frozen at –80 for 30 min. Pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 0.5% IGEPAL, 0.25% Triton X-100), rocked for 10 min at 4C, and centrifuged at 2300 rcf for 5 min at 4C to pellet nuclei.

Supernatant was discarded and nuclei were resuspended in 2 mL sonication buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS). 0.45 g sonication beads per reaction were added to sonication tubes (Diagenode, C01020031) and washed with 600 uL PBS. 1 mL of lysate was added to each sonication tube. 50 uL of lysate was kept as an input fraction. Sonication was performed in a Diagenode Bioruptor Pico sonicator using 10 cycles of 30 sec ON and 30 sec OFF. The sonicated supernatant was removed from the beads and spun at 20,000g for 10 min at 4C and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube for immunoprecipitation.

50 µL of Protein G dynabeads (Thermo, 10003D) were used per sample. Beads were washed 3x with 1 mL PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin. Beads were resuspended in 250 µL blocking solution and 5 µg of mouse anti-FLAG M2 antibody (Sigma, F3165) were added. The antibody bead complex was rotated at 4C overnight, then washed 3x with 1 mL block solution and resuspended in 50 µL block solution. Beads were added to lysate and rotated at 4C overnight. Beads were collected on a magnet and washed once each with 1 mL Sonication Buffer, 1 mL high-salt Sonication Buffer (sonication buffer but 500 mM NaCl and 1 mM EGTA), 1 mL LiCl Buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, 250 mM LiCl, 0.5% IGEPAL, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate) and 1 mL TE (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA). Beads were resuspended in 210 µL Elution Buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS) and incubated at 95 °C for 5 min. 200 µL of supernatant was collected and incubated overnight at 65C to reverse crosslinks. Input samples were brought to 200 uL with elution buffer and incubated in parallel.

RNase A was added to 0.2 mg/mL and samples were incubated for 2h at 37C. CaCl 2 (10 mM final concentration) and proteinase K (0.2 mg/mL final concentration) were added and the samples were incubated at 55C for 30 min. 400 µL of phenol chloroform:isomyl alcohol were added and samples were mixed on a vortexer and spun for 5 min at 10000g at room temperature. 300 µL aqueous layer was transferred to a microfuge tube and NaCl (200 mM final concentration), 30 µg glycogen, and 880 µL of 100% ethanol were added. Samples were incubated at –80C for 1 hr, spun at 14000g for 15 min at 4C and pellets were washed with 500 uL ice cold 70% ethanol. Pellets were dried and resuspended in 70 µL water.

NEBNext UltraExpress DNA library prep kit (NEB, E3325) was used to prep DNA according to manufacturers specifications. Libraries were run on a high sensitivity DNA tape station chip (5067–5593) for QC and to measure fragment size distribution, and concentration was quantified using the qubit high sensitivity DNA assay kit (Thermo, Q33230). Libraries were sequenced on an illumina NovaSeq using a paired-end 2 × 151 bp kit and demultiplexed yielding 41.0–50.5 M read pairs per ChIP library.


## CHIPseq data analysis

CHIPseq data was analyzed according to ENCODE guidelines ( 90 ). Read pairs were aligned to the UCSC hg38 assembly with BWA-MEM ( 91 ). Alignments were sorted and filtered for PCR/optical duplicates and MAPQ>30 using samtools ( 92 ). Reads overlapping the ENCODE hg38 blacklist v2 (Amemiya et al., 2019) were then removed with bedtools ( 93 ). Library quality was assessed with deepTools and summarized with MultiQC ( 94 , 95 ). The three KO ChIP BAMs were merged into a single pooled-KO control. This pooled-KO BAM was the control for all peak calling and differential binding analyses. Peaks were called for each library with MACS2 v2.2.9.1 against the pooled-KO control ( 96 ). Replicate reproducibility was assessed with IDR v2.0.3 for every replicate pair within a variant (18 pairs). Input for each replicate was its top 150,000 narrowPeaks ranked by p-value. Peaks with IDR < 0.05 were kept ( 97 ). Differential binding was tested with Diffbind ( 98 ) using the DESeq2 method ( 99 ). A consensus peakset was built from the MACS2 narrowpeak files keeping peaks called in ≥ 2 libraries. Peaks were re-centred on summits into 401 bp windows. Each variant was compared with WT for four total contrasts. Principal components analysis on DiffBind normalized counts of differentially bound peaks was performed using the sklearn python package ( 100 ). RUNX2 motif discovery was performed on 500 randomly sampled peaks from each variant using MEME. Peaks were centered on the summit +/− 15 bp. DNase-seq from human bone-marrow MSCs immortalized with hTERT (hMSC-TERT4) was obtained from GSE113253 ( 47 ). Reads were trimmed with Trim Galore v0.6.6 and aligned with Bowtie2 v2.5.3 to GRCh38 ( 101 ). Alignments were filtered to MAPQ ≥ 30 and canonical chromosomes, and duplicates were removed with Picard v2.26.10. For each RUNX2 peak, accessibility was computed as the mean DNase RPKM over the 401 bp peak window. RUNX2 motif counts per window were generated from a genome-wide FIMO scan with the WT de novo motif. Genomic background was generated from randomly sampled windows of matching GC content as the merged peakset. Motif enrichment in differentially bound peaks was tested using a FIMO scan. A random sample of peaks outside of promoters (further than 500 bp from transcription start sites) were selected and paired with random genomic windows outside all RUNX2 peaks, blacklisted regions and promoters, matched on GC content and day-0 DNase accessibility by nearest-neighbour matching. Motif families were chosen from a screen of each peak set against its matched control with SEA (MEME suite) over the full JASPAR 2024 CORE vertebrates collection, then one representative matrix per family was rescanned with FIMO. Enrichment was quantified as the log2 odds ratio of motif presence in peaks vs matched background and tested with a two-sided Fisher’s exact test. P-values were adjusted by Benjamini–Hochberg across all motif–set comparisons, and FDR < 0.01 was considered significant. For each variant, the log2 fold change of each window vs WT (from DiffBind) was regressed by ordinary least squares on RUNX motif count and DNase accessibility. Models were fit with accessibility only, motifs only, and both. Each peak was then assigned to the nearest protein-coding TSS (GENCODE v45).


## RNAseq

Cells were seeded at 1000 cells/mL in tissue culture treated 6-well plastic dishes two days before osteoblast differentiation. Osteoblast differentiation was induced as described. On day 4, cells were lysed in 200µL trizol (Thermo, 15596026) and RNA was extracted using a directzol RNA miniprep kit (Zymo, R1054) according to manufacturers instructions. RNAseq libraries were prepared using the NEBNext UltraExpress RNA library prep kit with polyA magnetic bead selection according to manufacturers instructions. Libraries were run on a high sensitivity DNA tape station chip (5067–5593) for QC and to measure fragment size distribution, and concentration was quantified using the qubit high sensitivity DNA assay kit (Thermo, Q33230) Reads were sequenced on an illumina NovaSeq 2 x 151 bp kit and demultiplexed generating 17.4–26.3 M read pairs per RNAseq library.


## RNAseq data analysis

Reads were aligned to the human genome (hg38) using STAR v2.7.11b ( 102 ). Gene-level read counts were obtained with featureCounts (Subread v1.6.3) on exon features grouped by gene_id, using the UCSC hg38 ensGene annotation ( 103 ). Differential expression was analyzed with DESeq2 v1.46.0 (R v4.5.3), with all six conditions fit jointly (18 libraries; 3 clones × 6 variants) ( 99 ). Genes with ≥5 counts in at least 3 samples were retained. Pairwise contrasts were extracted with the Wald test and p-values adjusted by Benjamini–Hochberg ( 104 ). Differentially expressed genes were defined as adjusted p < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1. Principal components analysis was performed as described for CHIPseq using the differentially expressed gene set. RNA integrity of libraries was assessed from aligned reads with RSeQC read_distribution. The 5’UTR to 3’UTR tag density was used as a degradation score, which was z-scored across libraries and included as a continuous covariate in the DESeq2 model.


## Molecular dynamics simulations

RUNX2 CIDR simulations were run with the residue-level Mpipi force field ( 59 ) in LAMMPS ( 105 ). In this model each residue is one bead. Beads are joined by harmonic bonds (E = K(r − r₀)², K = 9.6 kcal mol⁻¹ Å⁻², r₀ = 3.81 Å). Non-bonded interactions are given by the Wang–Frenkel potential (ν = 1, μ = 2, pair-specific cutoff r_c = 3σ_ij) plus Debye–Hückel electrostatics (κ = 0.126 Å⁻¹, corresponding to 150 mM salt; relative dielectric 80; cutoff 35 Å). Each chain was placed in a cubic periodic box of 600 Å, started from a self-avoiding coil, energy-minimized and simulated at 300 K. The equations of motion were integrated with a 10 fs timestep under NVE integration with a Langevin thermostat (damping time 5 ps). Each replicate was run for 3.005 × 10⁸ steps. The first 5 × 10⁵ steps were treated as equilibration and not written out, leaving 3 µs of production per replicate. Coordinates were saved every 1 ns (3,001 frames). For every analysis, the first 10% of frames were discarded, leaving 2,701 frames per replicate. Replicates differed in their random seeds for the initial velocities and the thermostat. The wild-type CIDR (RUNX2 230–521, 292 residues) was simulated in two independent replicates. For each frame, all bead–bead distances were computed under the minimum-image convention (MDAnalysis 2.6.1) ( 106 , 107 ). Two residues were scored as in contact if their beads were within 8 Å. Contact probabilities P(i,j) were averaged over frames and then over replicates. To remove the generic dependence of contact probability on sequence separation, each residue’s excess contacts were defined as where B(s) is the mean contact probability over all residue pairs at separation s in the same ensemble. Positive values mark residues that make more contacts than average for their sequence separations, and negative values mark fewer. The track was smoothed with a centred three-residue moving average.

Wild type and the 15 variants were simulated with Mpipi under the conditions above (300 K, 600 Å box, 150 mM, 3 µs), each in three independent replicates. For each replicate, the mean inter-bead distance ⟨d_ij⟩ was computed over the retained frames, after unwrapping the chain across periodic boundaries. The per-replicate maps were then averaged. To remove the dependence of distance on sequence separation, each map was normalized by subtracting, from every pair, the mean distance of all pairs at the same separation |i−j| in that same map. Pairs with |i−j| < 3 were excluded. After normalization, negative values (Å) mark residue pairs that are closer than typical for their separation, and positive values mark pairs that are farther apart. Each variant’s similarity to wild type was taken as the Pearson correlation coefficient between its normalized map and the wild-type map over all pairs with |i−j| ≥ 3. Difference maps (variant − wild type) were computed from the normalized maps. Map similarity was compared with log₁₀ of mineralization normalized to the wild type measured on the same plate (n = 15 variants) by Pearson correlation. Significance was assessed with a two-sided permutation test: mineralization values were reassigned to variants at random N = 100,000 times, and p = (1 + k)/(N + 1), where k is the number of permutations with |r| at least as large as the observed |r|.


## Generative IDR design

De novo CIDRs were designed using GOOSE against the FINCHES mean-field interaction model ( 7 , 8 ). For a candidate sequence, a self-interaction (ε) matrix was computed with the FINCHES Mpipi frontend as the intermolecular IDR matrix of the sequence against itself, treating the whole sequence as disordered, and similarity to wild type was scored as the Pearson correlation between the candidate and wild-type matrices. All designs were 286 residues, matching the wild-type CIDR (RUNX2 236–521). The two design classes were produced by different procedures. Fixed composition designs were generated with the GOOSE sequence design framework (SequenceOptimizer) run in shuffle-only mode (just_shuffle, shuffle interval 1, five shuffles per step) ( 7 ). This strategy preserves exact amino acid composition and only changes residue order. The run was warm-started from a randomly shuffled WT CIDR sequence, and combined three objectives: MatchSequenceDisorder against wild type (non-exact, requiring the design to be at least as disordered as wild type, weight 20); MatchingResidues with a target of less than 114 positions, i.e. 40% positional identity to wild type (weight 1); and a custom property (SelfEpsilonByMatrix) matching the candidate’s Mpipi ε matrix to that of wild type, computed as the intermolecular IDR matrix of the sequence against itself with both disorder masks disabled (target value 0, weight 1.0, model mpipi). The optimizer was run for up to 5000 iterations (tolerance 0.01). Free composition designs were generated with GOOSE using the same objectives but without shuffle-only mode, thus not preserving amino acid composition.


## Transcription factor disorder and effector-domain predictions

IDRs in the TFome and proteome were identified with metapredict (defined as contiguous disordered stretches of at least 20 residues) ( 28 , 108 ). Four independent models were used for activation domain prediction on TF IDRs according to each models’ published configuration. PADDLE ( 20 ) was run in the published full configuration, and a TF was called if it carried at least five consecutive 53-residue tiles with Z > 4. ADpred ( 31 ) was called at a probability of at least 0.8 over at least five contiguous residues. TADA ( 109 ) was called on a run of at least two windows scoring above 0.5. Acidic exposure ( 73 ) was called on 39-residue tiles with a net charge between –13 and –8 and at least six tryptophan, phenylalanine or leucine residues. Gene Ontology enrichment was computed for the 885 TFs called by no predictor family, against a background of all 1,619 TFs scored by all four families. Annotations were taken from the human GOA annotation file and the go-basic ontology ( 110 ). Each term was tested by a two-sided Fisher’s exact test; terms annotating fewer than eight genes in the background were dropped for lack of power, and p-values were corrected across terms by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure ( 104 ), with q < 0.05 taken as significant. Measured effector-domain activity was taken from four published high-throughput screens, treated as eight arms: DelRosso et al. (2023) ( 33 ), minCMV for activation and pEF and PGK for repression, in K562; Tycko et al. (2020) ( 32 ), minCMV for activation and pEF for repression; Tycko et al. (2025) ( 34 ), CD2 for activation and CD43 for repression, in K562; and Alerasool et al. (2022) ( 111 ), a GAL4-UAS assay in HEK293, activation only. The authors’ own published calls were used from their supplementary tables. For the per-TF grid, literature effector-domain annotations were taken from the compendium of Soto et al. (2022), 924 domains across 594 TFs. Examples of TFs from different functional categories are shown.
