## Abstract

Membrane proteins drive vital cellular signaling and bioenergetics yet resolving their physical interaction interfaces remains hindered by the paucity of experimental complexes and the breakdown of sequence alignments across hydrophobic bilayers. Here, we present CHORUS, a sequence-only deep-learning framework that decodes interaction sites across membrane proteins without requiring 3D structures, multiple sequence alignments (MSAs), or predefined partner identities. By systematically synthesizing complementary latent representations across diverse protein language model (pLM) families and functional layer depths, CHORUS outperforms 18 established sequence- and structure-based methods across 4,655 homology-isolated test contexts (average precision: 0.643), demonstrating amplified predictive capacity within the lipid-embedded core (0.746). We show that CHORUS delineates quaternary contact boundaries across megadalton respiratory supercomplexes directly from sequences alone (0.984). We deliver the CHORUS Atlas ( https://chorus.atlas.sunnylab.org ), providing precomputed, residue-resolved interaction landscapes for 80,619 reviewed transmembrane proteins spanning 4,918 species and 33.7 million residues to empower interactome-scale discovery and allosteric therapeutic design.


## Introduction

Network edges in protein interactomes effectively chart cellular architecture 1 , yet they fall short of resolving mechanistic causality at the molecular scale 2 . Instead, insights into underlying mechanisms reside in the physical interfaces through which proteins recognize one another 3 , assemble into higher-order structures 4 , and propagate conformational change 5 . This distinction is particularly consequential for membrane proteins, which account for approximately one-quarter of human protein-coding genes 6 and perform essential functions as receptors 7 , ion channels 8 , active pumps 9 , solute carriers 10 , transporters 11 , and membrane-embedded enzymes 12 , coordinating a plethora of cellular activities, such as signal transduction 13 , electrical excitability 14 , nutrient transport 15 , and energy conversion 16 . Across these classes, interaction and function are fundamentally inseparable: HRD1 recruits SEL1L to organize ER-associated degradation 17 , 18 . NaDC3 couples transport to asymmetric dimeric states 19 . CaSR activation is coordinated within an obligate homodimer 20 , and Kv4 channels are functionally remodeled by KChIP and DPP6 auxiliary subunits 21 . Such examples illustrate that membrane-protein interfaces are not merely sites of physical contact; they are operative engines of biological regulation.

The pervasive reliance of protein function on molecular interactions raises the dual challenge of defining cellular interactors and deciphering how their interfaces are physically encoded. This objective mirrors the steady progression of interactomics toward higher spatial resolution. Landmark screening efforts such as HuRI first established binary proteome wiring 22 , whereas platforms such as BioPlex 23 and hu.MAP 24 extended this view to the organization of multiprotein complexes. Yet, because co-complex membership frequently masks indirect associations, subsequent efforts such as DirectContacts have sought to disambiguate the true direct contacts within these macromolecular machines 25 . In parallel, proteome-scale deep-learning predictions and structural atlases are now translating topological networks into three-dimensional models with residue-level fidelity 26 , 27 . Collectively, these advances mark a transition from merely cataloging interactions to resolving the structural principles that dictate macromolecular recognition. However, this structural transition faces a formidable bottleneck in membrane proteins. Their functional interfaces are distributed across chemically antithetical compartments (i.e., spanning hydrophobic bilayers 28 and flanking aqueous environments 29 ) and the vast majority remain intractable to experimental complex determination or partner-resolved modeling. Deciphering interaction propensity directly from an isolated polypeptide sequence therefore represents an urgent yet under-addressed computational imperative, bypassing the requirement for predefined binding partners or resolved 3D complexes.

Predicting protein-protein interaction (PPI) interfaces at residue resolution provides the critical bridge between topological networks and the biophysical determinants of protein assembly 30 . Over the past decade, this field has bifurcated into structural and sequence-based computational paradigms 31 , 32 . Structure-based approaches have advanced rapidly through geometric deep learning 33 . MaSIF-site pioneered the extraction of surface fingerprints 34 , while frameworks such as ScanNet 35 and PeSTo 36 parse local atomic geometries to identify binding patches. Yet, the limited structural coverage of membrane proteins (i.e., around 3-5% of high-resolution 3D structures in the PDB 37 , 38 ) renders sequence-based prediction an indispensable alternative. Early deep-learning frameworks, including DeepPPISP 39 , DELPHI 40 , and PIPENN 41 , demonstrated that integrating sequence-derived physicochemical and evolutionary features could effectively map interfaces without 3D coordinates. This paradigm has been further accelerated by protein language models (pLMs) 42 – 44 . Architectures such as ISPRED-SEQ 45 , Seq-InSite 46 , and PIPENN-EMB 47 now exploit pretrained representations to extract rich contextual constraints directly from primary sequences. For the membrane proteome, specialized tools including MBPred 48 and DeepTMInter 49 further enforce topological and trans-bilayer priors. Despite these advances, predicting protein interfaces in a partner-independent manner remains a grand challenge. A sequence-only, unpaired framework must identify binding-competent residues across hydrophobic bilayers and aqueous compartments without prior knowledge of the interacting partner or quaternary context. Overcoming this limitation provides an essential, template-free foundation for deciphering the molecular wiring of uncharacterized membrane proteins.

Here, we introduce CHORUS, an end-to-end, sequence-only deep-learning framework designed to decipher residue-level interaction interfaces across membrane proteins without requiring structural coordinates, multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) 50 , or cognate partner identities. Rather than relying on a single static model, CHORUS leverages an optimal ensemble that systematically synthesizes complementary latent representations extracted across different model families (ESM-2 51 , ESM-C 52 , and ProtT5 53 ) and layer depths. Through rigorous retrospective benchmarking on non-redundant targets (at least <30% sequence identity to the training set) and diverse external cohorts, CHORUS consistently outperforms existing sequence-based predictors across disparate membrane topologies, interaction partner classes, and protein families. Systematic probing reveals how hierarchical pLM layers encode distinct biophysical properties of interfaces, while downstream analyses demonstrate its capacity to decouple protein interfaces from ligand-binding sites. Crucially, when applied to a mammalian respiratory supercomplex, CHORUS accurately recovers critical contact hubs across multi-subunit membrane assemblies directly based on sequences alone. Finally, to translate this computational capability into a community-wide resource, we establish the CHORUS Atlas ( https://tmatlas.sunnylab.org ), which spans 4,918 eukaryotic and prokaryotic species across the tree of life. This searchable atlas delivers precomputed, residue-level interaction landscapes for all 80,619 reviewed α-helical transmembrane proteins deposited in UniProt 54 , encompassing systematic inferences across more than 33.7 million individual amino acid residues. By democratizing proteome-scale interface maps without requiring structural inputs, CHORUS provides a foundational platform to potentially accelerate mechanistic discovery, functional annotation, and targeted therapeutic intervention across uncharted membrane proteomes.


## Results


## Overview of the CHORUS framework for sequence-based prediction of interaction sites in membrane proteins

Characterizing protein-protein interaction (PPI) sites in membrane proteins is crucial for understanding membrane-associated signalling yet remains challenging due to the scarcity of atomic-resolution structures with interacting partners and the limited reliability of multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) in hydrophobic transmembrane domains. To circumvent these limitations, we formulated transmembrane interface prediction as a partner-agnostic, residue-level sequence task ( Fig. 1 ). Given only the amino-acid sequence x of a transmembrane protein, our resulting model, CHORUS, estimates p i = Pr( y i = 1| x ), the conditional probability that each residue i belongs to an interaction interface across both intramembrane and extramembrane regions ( Fig. 1a ). This formulation computationally decouples interface identification from explicit partner specification, i . e ., rather than predicting interaction sites conditional on a predefined interacting partner. The resulting sequence-based inference framework therefore requires neither partner information nor complex templates, structural models, or MSAs.

As seen in Fig. 1b , CHORUS feeds embeddings from three complementary protein language model (pLM) families, including ESM-2 51 , ESM-C 52 , and ProtT5, across three neural network architectures (also called heads or backbones) to capture rich evolutionary and structural information. The predicted probabilities are aggregated via an unweighted ensemble mean into a calibrated interface probability p i , which is then converted into binary predictions ( ŷ i ∈ {0,1}) using a validation-tuned decision threshold (τ val = 0.554).

Supervised residue-level interactions were derived directly from experimentally resolved multimeric complexes ( Fig. 1c ). For each target chain, heavy-atom Euclidean distance matrices 55 were computed against all interacting partners, as illustrated by chain L interacting with chains H, M, and C in an exemplified complex (PDB code: 1L9J). Residues were labeled as interacting ( y = 1) if their global minimum distance fell within 5Å . Parsing distinct experimental complex structures resolved at < 3.5Å yielded 40,876 chains ( Fig. 1c,d ). To remove redundancy between protein chains, we instituted a strict graph-partitioning protocol ( Fig. 1d ). A total of 11,482 chains were identified to be representative of those 40,876 chains following an all-against-all pairwise alignment, which excluded 309,912 edges at ≥ 30% sequence identity. Taking the connected components of this similarity graph in union with chains of intra-PDB co-assemblies resolved the dataset into 419 strictly disjoint topological clusters. Entire intact clusters were assigned as monolithic blocks to training (299 clusters, 30,675 chain instances, 7.11M residues), validation (65 clusters, 5,546 chain instances, 2.24M residues), and independent test partitions (55 clusters, 4,655 chain instances, 2.19M residues). This partitioning guarantees that no test chain shares ≥ 30% sequence identity or structural co-complex association with any training or validation protein, providing a leakage-controlled benchmark to evaluate model generalization.


## Orchestrating cross-pLM representation diversity into the CHORUS ensemble

Our framework is designed to predict probabilities of interacting residues from sequences alone by extracting frozen embeddings from pretrained protein language models (pLMs) and routing them through downstream neural network architectures with diverse inductive biases. However, internal layers across distinct pLM families encode different levels of informative biological features, ranging from local physicochemical motifs and secondary structure propensities in early-to-intermediate layers to global tertiary contacts and functional site constraints in deeper layers. Consequently, maximizing interaction prediction requires jointly optimizing three axes, including i ) cross-family pLM diversity, which leverages distinct pretraining paradigms and model families (ESM2, ESMC, and ProtT5), ii ) intra-model representation depth, which probes layer-specific biological knowledge by sampling across multiple internal depths within each pLM (19 sampled representations in total), and iii ) downstream inductive bias, which pairs these representations with three distinct residue-level prediction backbones, including gated Conv-BiGRU (GCB), Conv-Transformer (CT), and gated Transformer-BiGRU (GTB). Taking the Cartesian product of these dimensions generated a candidate pool of 57 standalone predictors ( Fig. 2a-c and Supplementary Table 1 ). Model checkpoints for each candidate were tuned and selected strictly based on the validation split. To prevent sequence length from biasing optimization, average precision (AP) was computed independently within each target-chain context and equally averaged across contexts to yield a chain-macro-AP, wherein each protein chain contributed uniform weight. For each of the 57 candidate architectures, the checkpoint achieving the highest validation chain-macro-AP value was isolated for subsequent ensemble curation.

To preserve foundational representation diversity, constituent model selection was conducted within, rather than across, pLM families. Retaining the top two predictors per family yielded a six-model composite ensemble spanning all three pLMs and downstream architectures, namely, ESM2 layer 30 plugged into GTB (val AP: 0.5701) and CT (0.5680), ESMC layer 30 plugged into GTB (0.6142) and GCB (0.6095), and ProtT5 layer 24 plugged into GTB (0.5935) alongside layer 18 with GCB (0.5852). An unweighted arithmetic mean of these six prediction streams achieved a test chain-macro-AP value of 0.6434 ( Fig. 2a . and Supplementary Table 1 ). Paired cluster-bootstrap intervals confirmed this ensemble significantly outperformed baseline controls constructed by varying training seed (0.6210), coordinate basis (0.6178), representation depth (0.5881), uniform stream duplication (0.5752), or downstream parameter scaling (0.5567).

The six retained individual models were combined as the ensemble via an unweighted arithmetic mean to generate the final residue-level interaction score ( Fig. 2d ). Crucially, neither membrane-specific biological probes nor downstream interpretability analyses entered this selection pipeline: ensemble composition was governed exclusively by validation chain-macro-AP values. Downstream biological probing analyses ( Fig. 2b ) served to characterize what functional signals were linearly recoverable from distinct frozen representations, rather than to rank or filter ensemble constituents. On the independent test partition, the released six-model ensemble achieved a chain-macro-AP value of 0.6434 ( Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 1 ). This substantially exceeded six-model baseline controls constructed by varying training initialization (0.6210), feature coordinate basis (0.6178), ESM2 layer depth (0.5881), or uniform prediction-stream replication (0.5752), and outperformed a scaled single predictor with expanded downstream trainable parameter capacity (0.5567). Paired cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals for the performance difference between CHORUS and each control excluded zero, demonstrating that cross-family ensembling yields orthogonal predictive value that cannot be replicated by stochastic seed variation, intra-family depth sampling, or downstream parameter scaling.

We performed constituent model selection within pLM families to preserve embedding diversity across distinct pretraining paradigms. The 57 candidate predictors were ranked independently within ESM2, ESMC, and ProtT5 according to their validation chain-macro-AP values, and the two top-ranking models from each family were selected ( Fig. 1c , Supplementary Fig. 2 ). This stratified procedure identified ESM2 layer 30 paired with GTB (validation AP: 0.5701) and CT (0.5680); ESMC layer 30 with GTB (0.6142) and GCB (0.6095); and ProtT5 layer 24 with GTB (0.5935) alongside layer 18 with GCB (0.5852). This produced a composite six-model ensemble spanning all three pLM families and all three downstream architectures ( Supplementary Table 2-9 ). The complete validation trajectories of all 57 candidates, including their selected checkpoints and the six retained base predictors, are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 , whereas Supplementary Fig. 1 documents the optimization and validation trajectories of the six released predictors.


## Performance evaluation on the independent test set

We first benchmarked CHORUS against a total of 18 established protein interaction-site predictors, which encompass sequence-based methods, including DeepTMInter, DeepPPISP, EnsemPPIS 56 , PITHIA 57 , Seq-InSite 46 , PIPENN-EMB 47 , and ALLSites 58 , and structure-based methods that incorporate target-chain geometry, including ScanNet 35 , GPSite 59 , PeSTo 36 , GraphPPIS 60 , EquiPPIS 61 , Gated-GPS 62 , RGN 63 , Spatom 64 , DeepProSite 65 , MaSIF-site 34 , and EGRET 66 . Note that ScanNet was evaluated both with and without MSA features, while GraphPPIS was assessed with its fast and evolutionary-profile configurations (see Supplementary Tables 10 and 11 for full implementation details and input requirements). The entire benchmark was entirely evaluated on our orchestrated independent test set that comprises 4,655 chain contexts across 55 sequence-similarity clusters, with no sequence pair between partitions exceeding 30% sequence identity. All predictions were benchmarked against a standardized 5Å inter-residue contact threshold. Given the inherent class imbalance (interacting residues are far rarer than non-interacting residues), average precision (AP), a non-interpolated measure of the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), served as the primary evaluation metric 67 . We computed AP independently for each eligible target-chain context (excluding six contexts lacking positive residues) and calculated their unweighted mean. We refer to this equal-context average as chain-macro-AP values, where each context contributes equally irrespective of sequence length.

For the deployment-aligned main ranking, structure-based predictors used monomer structures predicted from the target sequence by ESMFold or ColabFold, according to the input modes available for each method. When both predicted-structure sources were available, the source with higher test chain-macro-AP values was displayed in Fig. 3a . Structure-source sensitivity is examined separately. Sequence-length limits restricted the coverage of DeepProSite and EGRET, while incomplete surface preparation or residue mapping restricted MaSIF-site. Their available scores are reported with context and residue coverage, but the main ranking includes only methods that provided predictions for the complete benchmark. To reflect deployment-realistic conditions, structure-based predictors operated on predicted monomer conformations generated by ESMFold or ColabFold, in accordance with the supported inputs of each tool. Where both predicted structural sources were applicable, the model configuration yielding the higher test chain-macro-AP values was selected for the primary comparison ( Fig. 3a , structural sensitivity is further analyzed). Although context- and residue-level scores for DeepProSite, EGRET (constrained by sequence length limits), and MaSIF-site (limited by surface mesh generation and residue mapping failures) are reported for completeness, our primary ranking strictly includes methods providing complete benchmark coverage.

Overall, CHORUS outperformed all comparators, achieving a chain-macro-AP value of 0.643, a 0.030 absolute improvement over the best-performing complete-coverage baseline, ScanNet-MSA (0.613, Fig. 3a ), and substantially outpacing leading structure-guided architectures including GPSite (0.583), PeSTo (0.581), and sequence-only methods (e.g., Seq-InSite at 0.556 and ESMC-L30 at 0.555). When pooling all 2.18 million residues, CHORUS reinforced its discriminative superiority, achieving an amino-acid AP (AA AP) of 0.667 and an AUROC of 0.861, compared with 0.612 and 0.843 for ScanNet-MSA ( Fig. 3a and 3d ). For downstream biological translation (such as targeted mutagenesis or epitope prioritization), accuracy within the highest-confidence prediction window is paramount. Crucially, evaluation of mean per-context precision among top-scoring candidates ( P @ k ) revealed that CHORUS uniquely suppressed false positives at the interface apex ( Fig. 3b ). CHORUS achieved highest precision among the top 5 predicted residues ( P @5 = 0.826), indicating that more than four in five top predictions correspond to true physical contacts. As the selection depth expanded to k = 10 and k = 20, CHORUS exhibited strong resistance to precision decay, maintaining 0.808 at P @10 and 0.793 at P @20. In stark contrast, competing methods experienced sharp degradation, with ScanNet-MSA decaying from 0.821 at P @5 to 0.743 at P @20 while GPSite and PeSTo plummeting below 0.710 (0.699 and 0.710 at P @20, respectively) and all other compared methods falling under 0.685.

As seen Fig. 3a and 3b , at validation-tuned operating thresholds, CHORUS demonstrated exceptional classification balance over pooled residues, achieving an F1 of 0.620 and the highest Matthews correlation coefficient on the benchmark (MCC = 0.509). Notably, whereas competing methods achieved apparent sensitivity by heavily sacrificing specificity, e.g., GraphPPIS-slow and PITHIA driving recall to 0.825-1.000 at the cost of precision dropping to 0.230-0.349, CHORUS balanced high precision (0.632) with robust recall (0.608). Furthermore, probability calibration assessed via the class-balanced (CB) Brier score confirmed that CHORUS produced the most reliable posterior probability estimates under class imbalance (CB Brier = 0.167 for CHORUS vs. 0.189 for ScanNet-MSA, 0.234 for PeSTo, and 0.291 for GPSite).

This quantitative advantage translated directly into spatially coherent interface delineation on a representative benchmark target ( Fig. 3c ), the Cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of a dimer of the undecameric human CALHM2 (PDB code: 6VAI, chain F), which comprises 109 true interacting residues out of 267 total residues. CHORUS achieved an AP of 0.781 (decision threshold τ = 0.554), faithfully mapping the continuous interaction patch with minimal spurious background activation. By contrast, competing tools failed to fully capture interface geometry or suffered widespread non-contact false positives, yielding lower performance, with an AP value of 0.685 for ScanNet-MSA, 0.646 for PeSTo, and 0.465, for GPSite). Across all thresholds, precision-recall and ROC curves collectively demonstrated that CHORUS forms the upper envelope over existing methods ( Fig. 3d ).


## Performance evaluation on external datasets

To rigorously assess model generalizability beyond internal splits, we first evaluated CHORUS on three established benchmarks specialized for transmembrane proteins: CompData101 (101 chains) 68 , TestData36 (36 chains) 48 , and IndepData30 (30 chains) 49 . To prevent data leakage from sequence homology, we retrained the six-model ensemble on data filtered at < 30% identity against the external sets (14,625 chains for training and 4,112 chains for validation). Evaluated across all 167 non-redundant external targets, our ensemble model retained robust predictive ability, with chain-macro-AP values of 0.770, 0.741, and 0.817 on CompData101, IndepData30, and TestData36, respectively ( Supplementary Table 12 ). Furthermore, pooled-residue discrimination proved robust, with AUROC values consistently reaching 0.803, 0.821, and 0.900 across the respective cohorts (pooled AP: 0.752, 0.744, and 0.844). These results show that CHORUS captures true interface physics rather than relying on sequence homology. For reference, performance using original released weights across the full external cohorts is cataloged in Supplementary Table 13 .


## Analysis with respect to pLM embedding layers, ensemble complementarity, and prediction sensitivity

Systematic evaluation across 57 model configurations revealed that the most informative representation for transmembrane contact prediction was rarely the terminal layer of a pLM ( Fig. 4 ). Averaged across three prediction heads, independent-test average precision (AP) peaked at intermediate-to-late representations: layer 30 for ESM2 and ESMC, and layer 18 for ProtT5. Notably, relative head rankings also shifted across representations, demonstrating the necessity of jointly optimizing the family-layer-head space rather than treating representations uniformly.

We next assessed whether the performance of the final CHORUS ensemble could be matched by simpler ensemble strategies or enlarged single models. Six-member controls constructed by varying random seeds, transforming feature bases, combining distinct ESM2 layers, or averaging identical outputs achieved chain-macro-AP values of 0.621, 0.618, 0.588, and 0.575, respectively, all significantly trailing CHORUS (0.643; Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 1 ). Scaling up a single prediction network on ESM2 layer-30 features likewise underperformed (AP = 0.557), despite using more trainable parameters than the entire ensemble combined (27.69M vs. 24.38M). Resampling test clusters confirmed that all paired improvements by CHORUS excluded zero at the 95% confidence level. Leave-one-out and pairwise ablations further corroborated the complementary synergy among distinct pLM families and heads. To investigate whether ensemble size could be reduced without sacrificing calibration, we screened an expanded pool of 63 predictors (incorporating multi-layer ProtT5 embeddings). Greedy selection guided by individual accuracy and inter-model synergy identified four-and five-member ensembles that achieved lower Brier error than CHORUS while maintaining statistically indistinguishable AP values.

To interpret the layer-dependent performance trajectory, we probed the biological properties encoded in frozen embeddings via regularized linear models ( Fig. 4b ). Across three training seeds, structural features exhibited distinct stratification across representation depth: membrane localization (interior vs. flanking) was captured in early contextual layers, local contact density emerged primarily in deeper layers, and evolutionary conservation remained weakly accessible throughout. Ridge regression predicting layer-wise AP gains demonstrated that biological probe scores ( R 2 = 0.789) and relative layer depth ( R 2 = 0.719) strongly tracked downstream utility, reaching R 2 = 0.821 in combination. While this coupling reflects the shared evolution of structural awareness across layers, paired bootstrap tests indicated that neither depth nor biological scores alone fully accounted for performance gains beyond the other. Because raw AP varies with positive contact frequency, we evaluated the sensitivity of predictions to sequence length and contact prevalence ( Fig. 4c ). From the shortest to longest protein quartiles (Q1-Q4), positive residue prevalence fell from 0.403 to 0.156, dropping observed CHORUS AP from 0.677 to 0.542. Standardizing positive prevalence to the validation baseline (0.233) reversed this trend (adjusted AP: 0.555 to 0.655), revealing that ranking fidelity remains high or even improves in longer chains. Across a balanced panel of 45 predictors, deeper layers consistently maintained higher adjusted AP across all length regimes.

Finally, benchmarking against structure-dependent methods highlighted divergent vulnerabilities to predicted coordinate quality ( Fig. 4d ). Methods requiring explicit 3D conformations suffered notable performance drops when moving from experimental coordinates to ESMFold or ColabFold models (e.g., Gated-GPS dropped from 0.630 to 0.475 and 0.446, respectively), whereas others exhibited modest degradation (e.g., GraphPPIS-slow: 0.485 to 0.477 and 0.484). Several structure-based tools also suffered from incomplete target coverage (e.g., EGRET context coverage: 57.3%). In contrast, CHORUS operates directly on primary sequences, ensuring full coverage and eliminating downstream reliance on structural preprocessing.


## Performance across membrane regions and biological contexts

To delineate the structural and evolutionary determinants of contact prediction, we systematically deconvolved the performance of CHORUS (implemented as the six-member cross-pLM ensemble, Cross-pLM-6) across sub-cellular microenvironments, interaction partners, topological architectures, and evolutionary novelty ( Fig. 5 ). To prevent over-represented structural families from biasing evaluations, metrics across biological subgroups were macro-averaged within homology clusters (30% sequence identity, SI30) and reported as median cluster average precision (mAP) alongside interquartile ranges (IQR).

We first assessed whether spatial partitioning within the lipid bilayer dictates predictive accuracy ( Fig. 5a-c ). When benchmarked against ScanNet-MSA, CHORUS exhibited distinct spatial compartmentalization. While both models delivered comparable precision in aqueous environments (cytoplasmic mAP: 0.626 vs. 0.621; extracellular mAP: 0.632 vs. 0.637), CHORUS established a decisive superiority within the intramembrane core, achieving a context-macro AP of 0.746 against ScanNet-MSA’s 0.645 (pooled AP: 0.754 vs. 0.623; AUROC: 0.889 vs. 0.814). This pronounced intramembrane advantage demonstrates that sequence-derived representations from diverse pLMs effectively capture the tightly packed, low-dielectric packing physics characteristic of transmembrane helices without requiring explicit 3D atomic coordinates.

Dissecting contacts by the molecular identity of the interacting partner revealed sharp performance disparities ( Fig. 5d ). For transmembrane protein interactions, CHORUS maintained consistently high precision across both homotypic interfaces (same-sequence partners: median AP = 0.603) and heterotypic complexes (different-sequence partners: median AP = 0.604) across 45 and 35 independent test clusters, respectively. In stark contrast, predictions degraded substantially for transient interfaces formed with soluble, non-TM proteins (median AP = 0.265 across 25 clusters). This divergence indicates that while lipid-embedded helix–helix packing motifs are robustly encoded in frozen pLM embeddings, transient and solvent-exposed interfaces governed by dynamic electrostatics and hydration shells remain substantially more challenging to infer purely from sequence.

Target topology and evolutionary novelty further constrained the operational envelope ( Fig. 5e-h ). For topological complexity, performance remained exceptionally robust for bitopic and oligotopic proteins containing 1 to 6 transmembrane helices (median cluster AP = 0.706–0.725; Fig. 5e ), but dropped sharply for polytopic architectures featuring ≥ 7 TM segments (median AP = 0.311 across 17 test clusters). Such polytopic bundles present intricate tertiary intramolecular contacts that often confound intermolecular interface prediction. For evolutionary generalization, while predictions generalized seamlessly across major kingdoms (Bacterial mAP = 0.627; Eukaryotic mAP = 0.594; Fig. 5f ) and between species seen or unseen during supervised training (0.627 vs. 0.607; Fig. 5g ), performance was strongly modulated by family-level homology ( Fig. 5h ). Targets whose SIFTS-Pfam domain families were fully represented in training reached a median AP of 0.749, dropping to 0.600 for partially represented families and 0.540 for orphan families unseen during training (23 test clusters). Thus, while global taxonomic origin does not restrict model utility, structural family coverage remains a key governing factor.

Finally, we probed whether the learned contact rules could zero-shot transfer beyond transmembrane architectures to soluble protein complexes without architectural modification or retraining. The frozen ensemble generalized remarkably well to coiled-coil oligomers (AUROC = 0.943-0.960) and specific canonical inhibitor complexes (e.g., eglin C and stefin B, AUROC > 0.940). However, performance varied widely on loose enzyme-partner interfaces (AUROC = 0.469-0.869), and the fixed calibration threshold failed to balance precision–recall across soluble classes (e.g., yielding saturating false positives on tetrabrachion while calling zero positives on ribonuclease inhibitor). These findings substantiate that while geometric contact features learned from membrane environments generalize to specific tightly packed, regularized soluble interfaces, target-specific probability recalibration will be essential for universal interactome-scale deployment.


## CHORUS-predicted interaction sites do not preferentially overlap annotated protein–ligand binding sites

To determine whether CHORUS recovers a generic binding-site signal rather than features associated with protein-protein recognition, we mapped experimentally curated small-molecule and ion-binding sites from BioLiP onto the atlas of interaction sites in membrane proteins. BioLiP/SIFTS mapping covered 2,994 unique sequences; the primary association analysis retained 2,971 sequences containing 100,003 annotated protein-ligand-binding (PLB) residues (see Methods). We then compared the observed overlap between CHORUS-predicted interacting residues and PLB residues with that expected under a sequence-specific model ( Supplementary Fig. 1a-c , see Methods for details). Across the atlas, 19.1% of PLB residues fell within predicted PPIS, closely matching the expected overlap (observed-to-expected ratio, O/E = 0.979). This relationship was maintained across cytoplasmic (0.967), extracellular/luminal (1.019) and transmembrane (0.983) regions, whereas intramembrane sites showed only a modest positive shift (1.094) ( Supplementary Fig. 1c ). Sequence-level observed-minus-expected differences were likewise centered close to zero ( Supplementary Fig. 1d ). The comparison samples only ligand contacts captured in experimentally resolved structures and therefore represents a restricted view of the accessible ligand-binding landscape. Indeed, residues lacking BioLiP annotations constitute an annotation background rather than a verified non-binding class. Thus, the analysis establishes that currently observed PLB sites do not systematically coincide with CHORUS predictions, while leaving open the capacity of individual PPI interfaces to accommodate or be modulated by small molecules.

We next assessed whether this relationship was consistent across individual proteins and evolutionary lineages by quantifying differential annotated PLB site occupancy (see Methods for details about its definition) within sequences, defined as the difference in PLB-site occupancy between our predicted interacting and non-interacting residues. Values near zero therefore indicate comparable PLB-site occupancy in the two residue classes. Differential PLB-site occupancy remained centred close to zero across major taxonomic strata ( Supplementary Fig. 1e ) and representative species ( Supplementary Fig. 1f ), with no consistent directional shift towards either predicted PPI or non-PPI regions. Together, these analyses show that CHORUS-predicted interfaces do not preferentially recapitulate known PLB sites, providing a cross-functional assessment of model specificity and indicating that CHORUS may capture residue-level information complementary to established ligand-binding annotations.


## Association with FunFam conservation

To determine whether CHORUS-predicted interaction sites preferentially occupy evolutionarily constrained positions, we mapped predictions onto CATH FunFam conservation profiles. The high-confidence analysis encompassed 2,739,713 Scorecons-mapped residues from 9,529 exact sequences distributed across 511 FunFams. Residues with Scorecons ≥0.7 were designated as highly conserved according to the established FunFam criterion 69 , and their overlap with predicted PPI sites was compared with the corresponding expectation, which thus account for both the prevalence of conserved residues and the fraction of residues called as PPIS within each sequence ( Fig. 6a-c ).

Across all mapped sequences, 23.0% of highly conserved residues coincided with predicted PPI sites, corresponding to an observed-to-expected ratio of 1.014 and indicating only limited overall enrichment. The association varied across membrane topologies. The strongest enrichment was observed in cytoplasmic regions, with an observed-to-expected ratio of 1.127, whereas extracellular, transmembrane, and intramembrane regions showed ratios of 1.013, 0.980 and 1.009, respectively ( Fig. 6b,c ). Thus, enrichment of highly conserved residues at predicted PPI sites was modest overall and most evident in cytoplasmic regions. We therefore complemented this threshold-based analysis by treating Scorecons as a continuous measure of evolutionary conservation. Within each exact sequence, mean Scorecons was compared between predicted PPI sites and non-PPI-site residues ( Fig. 6d ). Predicted PPI sites showed higher mean conservation overall and within cytoplasmic and transmembrane regions, whereas no clear difference was observed in extracellular regions. Notably, the positive conservation shift within transmembrane regions was detectable in the continuous analysis despite the absence of enrichment under the binary Scorecons ≥ 0.7 criterion, indicating that the association with evolutionary constraint was not restricted to the most highly conserved residues.

The magnitude of these within-sequence conservation differences varied across taxonomic groups ( Fig. 6e ) and representative species ( Fig. 6f ) and was generally attenuated after leave-one-out FunFam centering, suggesting that part of the apparent taxonomic variation reflected differences in the FunFam composition represented across taxa. Collectively, these analyses indicate a modest but reproducible association between predicted PPI sites and evolutionary conservation, with the clearest signal observed in cytoplasmic regions and additional evidence of elevated conservation within transmembrane PPI sites.


## Contact prediction across a large respiratory supercomplex

Mitochondrial energy transduction relies on massive membrane-embedded supramolecular machineries, wherein individual respiratory complexes assemble into higher-order supercomplexes (respirasomes) to optimize electron flux. Cryo-electron microscopy structures of the porcine respirasome resolve distinct architectures of complexes I, III, and IV, providing a stringent benchmark to evaluate whether sequence-based predictions can resolve densely packed, multi-subunit quaternary interfaces at the megadalton scale ( Fig. 7 ). We examined the circular I 2 III 2 IV 2 supercomplex assembly (PDB 8UGN) as the primary biological case study ( Fig. 7a ). This massive 140-chain assembly encompasses 38 unique membrane-protein entities represented by 78 physical chain copies. CHORUS evaluated each chain sequence independently, without access to spatial coordinates or quaternary assembly context.

Across 12,337 observed residues in these target chains, CHORUS accurately delineated inter-subunit contact boundaries, achieving an entity-macro average precision (AP) of 0.984, an AUROC of 0.970, and an MCC of 0.817 at the 5 Å contact threshold ( Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 14 ). Restricting evaluation to the 27 entities with annotated transmembrane segments yielded consistent precision (AP = 0.989, AUROC = 0.974). Visual inspection of surface probability projections across the 78 target copies confirmed exceptional spatial fidelity ( Fig. 7a,d ). These results demonstrate that CHORUS accurately resolves the architecture of native macromolecular supercomplexes directly from primary sequence, providing an effective framework for annotating uncharacterized multi-subunit membrane assemblies at structural resolution.


## Pan-proteomic landscape of transmembrane interactions

To bridge the gap between sparse structural complexes and the vast expanse of uncharacterized membrane interactomes, we scaled CHORUS to the global proteome, generating a comprehensive interaction atlas across reviewed UniProt transmembrane proteins ( Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 15 ). After deduplicating identical primary sequences, the resulting repository spans 80,619 curated protein records corresponding to 70,247 non-redundant sequences, 4,918 species across all domains of life, and over 33.7 million residues ( Fig. 8a ).

Quantitative mapping across the taxonomic cladogram uncovered substantial historical sampling asymmetries across evolutionary lineages ( Fig. 8a,b ). While Metazoa exhibited the highest taxonomic diversity (comprising 48.9% of sampled species), they accounted for only 31.5% of total records. Conversely, Bacteria demonstrated marked database enrichment, contributing 38.8% of all protein records despite representing just 18.7% of species (log 2 sampling ratio = +1.05; dominated by model organisms such as E. coli, n = 3,634). Viridiplantae (plants) and Fungi contributed a balanced 21.7% of records combined. Overall, the species record-count distribution was intensely skewed (Gini coefficient = 0.865), with 51.6% of species represented by singletons and the top 100 species capturing 61.0% of all cataloged transmembrane proteins.

Across this proteome-wide corpus, applying the validation-calibrated threshold ( p i ≥ 0.554) yielded a rich prediction landscape stratified by sequence length and inference regime ( Fig. 8c ). Standard exact inference encompassed 99.21% of sequences ( n = 69,694), spanning sequence lengths up to 1,024 residues with predicted interface densities predominantly concentrated between 15% and 40%. For long and ultra-long polypeptides, we established tiled windowing (0.52%, n = 368) and length out-of-distribution (length-OOD, 0.26%, n = 185) computational regimes, safeguarding inference consistency up to exceptional multi-pass channels exceeding 10,000 amino acids.

To democratize community exploration, we packaged this pan-proteomic landscape into an open-access web resource ( CHORUS Atlas, Fig. 8d ). The interface seamlessly integrates cross-species accession indexing, per-residue interaction probability profiles, and automatic topological alignment onto experimental structures or homology models (e.g., PDB 8V00 for olfactory receptor coreceptors).


## Discussion

CHORUS demonstrates that ensembling diverse protein language model (pLM) representations enables high-fidelity contact prediction for α -helical transmembrane proteins directly from sequence, bypassing dependencies on structural coordinates, MSAs, or nominated partners. Systematic representation analyses reveal that structural topology and contact signals peak at intermediate-to-late pLM layers rather than terminal representations, powering state-of-the-art ranking precision particularly within the lipid bilayer.

Pan-proteomic deployment yields two foundational biological insights. First, predicted interfaces are spatially orthogonal to BioLiP small-molecule binding sites (observed/expected ≈ 1.0), demonstrating that CHORUS decodes bona fide quaternary contact physics rather than generic pocket concavity, while highlighting these interfaces as an expansive, unexploited frontier for allosteric drug discovery. Second, cross-referencing with CATH Functional Families reveals an environmental dichotomy: whereas cytoplasmic interfaces are governed by rigid sequence conservation, intramembrane interfaces exhibit pronounced sequence plasticity. This divergence explains why alignment-based conservation metrics falter in lipid bilayers and confirms why contextual pLM embeddings are essential to resolve degenerate hydrophobic packing.

Key limitations outline clear future directions. The 5Å proximity threshold serves as a geometric proxy rather than thermodynamic affinity, and partner-agnostic inference cannot capture dynamic conformational transitions or lipidic environmental shifts. Furthermore, accuracy declines on polytopic architectures (≥ 7 TM segments) and transient non-TM partners, while querying three distinct pLMs carries substantial computational overhead. Compressing multi-pLM ensembles via knowledge distillation and developing partner-conditioned representations will be vital next steps for scalable interactome discovery.


## Methods


## Sequence-only prediction across a family-layer-backbone candidate space

To capture local biochemical surroundings and long-range structural dependencies from frozen pLM representations, we engineered three hybrid backbone architectures for residue-level interface prediction, including Gated Conv-BiGRU (GCB), Conv-Transformer (CT), and Gated Transformer-BiGRU (GTB) ( Fig. 2a and 2b ). Across all architectures, parallel multi-scale 1D convolutions extract localized motif patterns, which are integrated with contextual representations derived from multi-head self-attention or bidirectional recurrent units. In GCB and GTB, dynamic gating mechanisms adaptively balance local convolutional signals against sequential contexts. GCB incorporates ten auxiliary residue-level biophysical priors, including amino-acid (AA) index 70 , sequence length, noncanonical residue indicator, selenocysteine (U) indicator, polarity, hydrophobicity, charge, hydropathy, aromaticity, and relative position, while CT and GTB operate solely on foundation embeddings .

Different pLM families and transformer layers capture distinct hierarchies of protein structures, from local secondary-structure motifs to global fold topologies. To identify optimal representational depths, we systematically sampled 19 layers from pLMs, with 7 for ESM-2 (650M), 7 for ESM-C (600M), and 5 for ProtT5-XL-U50. Pairing each layer with the three candidate backbones led to a comprehensive pool of 57 prediction architectures ( Fig. 2a,c ). Candidates within each pLM family were ranked by chain-macro average precision (AP) values across proteins from the validation dataset. Rather than relying on a single architecture, two top-performing models per pLM family were selected to construct the final ensemble model, dubbed Cross-pLM-6 ( Fig. 2c,d ).

The resultant ensemble model strategically traverses structural depths and architectural archetypes, including two configurations utilizing ESM-2 layer 30 (coupled with GTB and CT), two exploiting ESM-C layer 30 (coupled with GTB and GCB), and the remaining two deploying intermediate representations from ProtT5 layers 18 and 24 (paired with GTB and GCB, respectively, Fig. 2d ). Finally, individual residue interaction probabilities are pooled via unweighted averaging across the six complementary predictors. Binary interface calls were assigned using a decision threshold determined exclusively on the validation set and subsequently fixed for all test evaluations (τ val = 0.554). This design allows the final classification of interfacial residues to benefit from complementary representational perspectives, thereby reducing single-model bias.


## Dataset construction and structural quality control

To develop and evaluate CHORUS, transmembrane protein complexes were retrieved from the protein data bank of transmembrane proteins (PDBTM) 71 , which provides an exhaustive cohort of annotations of protein locations (e.g., transmembrane segments). We restricted the dataset to multimeric assemblies with at least one interacting partner, which yielded an initial pool of 45,750 chains across 11,469 entries.

To resolve discrepancies with unresolved regions in canonical records, structure-derived sequences were aligned against canonical PDBTM sequences using the Python built-in class, difflib.SequenceMatcher, with heuristics disabled. Chains were excluded if structural sequence coverage fell below 70% of the canonical record, or if sequence identity was < 99% with more than 3 mismatches. Entries with empty sequences, index corruptions, or coordinate-record inconsistencies were discarded. This filter eliminated nine chains, ensuring strict one-to-one mapping between sequence positions and coordinate-derived labels. In total, the final dataset comprises 40,876 chains across 8,363 entries, encompassing 11,482 distinct structure-derived sequences and 11,537,440 residue-level contact labels. Based on PDBTM classifications, these correspond to 40,849 α-helical and 27 β-strand target chains.


## Definition of interacting residues

Interacting residues between protein chains were identified using spatial proximity between their respective heavy atoms, following distance-based criteria established for interaction interfaces in membrane proteins 48 , 49 . For each multimeric complex, the target chain was evaluated against all co-assembled partner chains, which were not restricted to transmembrane proteins. All hydrogen atoms, crystallographic waters, and heteroatoms (HETATM records) were removed. For each target residue at sequence position i , the minimum Euclidean distance across all heavy-atom pairs belonging to any partner chain was denoted as d i . In accordance with conventional interface definitions (4-6Å), a residue was designated as an interacting residue ( y i = 1) if it fell within 5Å of any partner chain, and non-interacting ( y i = 0) otherwise:

Residues lacking resolved coordinates in the structure were excluded from the analysis rather than treated as non-interacting. Crucially, designating a residue as interacting denotes geometric contact within a specific structural snapshot rather than a direct thermodynamic contribution to binding affinity, whereas a non-interacting assignment reflects the absence of contact within that observed conformation. This criterion was applied uniformly across training, validation, and test sets.

To avoid sampling bias and further ensure a fair comparison for evaluating generalization ability, we eliminated redundancy between proteins both within and across datasets. ## External datasets and evaluation

CHORUS was evaluated externally in two settings: i ) direct inference using the released six-member ensemble and ii ) a separate retraining experiment following sequence-similarity exclusion. We collected 3 datasets specialized for membrane proteins, including CompData (101 chains) 68 , TestData (36 chains) 48 , and IndepData (30 chains) 49 , alongside 15 general-protein datasets or docking views from literature 34 , 35 , 57 , 74 – 80 ( Supplementary Table 10 ). Each external benchmark retained its native interaction criteria and residue masks. For membrane protein targets, native 6Å heavy-atom criteria were maintained. For unbound docking targets, bound interfaces (≤ 6Å) were mapped via sequence alignment. For proteins used in MaSIF, residues were defined as interacting if their interface areas exceeded 5Å 2 and > 4% of total surface area (14,227 evaluated, 2,720 masked residues).

Predictions were generated by unweighted probability averaging across the six ensemble backbones and scored by chain-macro and pooled AP/AUROC values. Overlapping cohorts were evaluated individually. ## Helix connectivity

Numerous transmembrane proteins adopt conformations in which transmembrane helices are embedded within densely packed multi-helix bundles 81 . Local packing around transmembrane helices serves as a primary structural constraint shaping their tertiary structures 82 . We used connectivity between helices as a structural probe to determine whether 3D packing environments have been encoded by frozen pLM representations rather than merely local physicochemical properties.

The helix-neighbor connectivity was defined as a helix-level descriptor of intrachain transmembrane packing. Within a target chain, each transmembrane helix annotated in PDBTM was represented as a node in a graph where an edge can be represented as two helices being in contact. Residues belonging to distinct helices were considered to be in contact when their minimum heavy-atom distance was ≤5.5 Å as reported by Fuchs et al. 55 . A helix-helix edge was retained only when at least three residues from each of the two helices participated in such inter-helical contacts. This requirement suppresses edges arising from isolated atomic proximities, and, instead, captures sustained helix-helix packing. Helix connectivity was quantified by node degree and grouped into three packing states: isolated (0), paired (1), or bundled (≥ 2 neighboring helices). Residue embeddings were mean-pooled across each helix to yield a single representation, which was then evaluated for its predictive capacity over these connectivity classes. This task, therefore, tested whether frozen sequence-derived representations encoded higher-order transmembrane architecture sufficient to distinguish relatively isolated helices from helices participating in pairwise or multi-helix packing arrangements.

## Predictive architecture

CHORUS predicts residue-level interactions directly from amino-acid sequences without structural inputs or evolutionary alignments. Feature vectors were extracted from frozen pLMs, including ESM-2 51 , ESM-C 52 , and ProtT5 53 [12, 19, 20], sampling 19 distinct hidden layers from pre-Transformer inputs (Layer 0) to final layers. Non-canonical symbols (U, Z, O, B) were unified as X, and sequence delimiters were stripped. Protein sequences exceeding maximum context lengths were processed via sliding windows (window size: 1,022 residues yielding 128-residue overlaps for ESM-2/ESM-C, 1,024 yielding a 128-residue overlap for ProtT5), followed by linear averaging across intersecting segments. The pLMs and chosen layers are summarized in Table 1 .

We also calculated ten features directly from the input sequence, including a numerical amino-acid identity code, labels identifying noncanonical or unknown residues and selenocysteine (U), hydropathy, a coarse charge value, labels for aromatic, polar and hydrophobic residues, sequence length, and normalized residue position. The charge property was encoded as +1 for

K, R and H , -1 for D and E , and 0, otherwise. For residue position i in a sequence of length L , normalized position was , ranging from 0 at the first residue to 1 at the last. The GCB backbone received these ten features in addition to the pLM embeddings, while the CT and GTB backbones received pLM embeddings alone. All ten additional features were derived from sequence without structural or alignment information. Evaluating all combinations of the 19 representation layers and three backbones established a structured space of 57 candidate predictors (19 × 3).

Extracted representations were mapped to interaction probabilities using additional neural networks . To systematically evaluate the representational capacity across pLM families and layers, we designed three complementary backbones of neural networks as follows.

Gated Conv-BiGRU (GCB) : Parallel 1D convolutions (kernel sizes k ∈ {3,9}) capture sequentially contextual features. Concatenating the projected input with both convolutional representations feeds a gating sub-network that computes learned interpolation weights between 0 and 1, which blends local convolutions with the original projection. A single bidirectional GRU (BiGRU) 85 processes this blended sequence, and its hidden states are concatenated with the gated features before classification.

Conv-Transformer (CT) : Parallel convolutions ( k ∈ {3,7,11}) capture multi-scale local neighborhoods, which are concatenated with the projected features and linearly compressed to the backbone width. Two Transformer encoder layers 51 model long-range residue dependencies. The classifier receives the concatenation of the Transformer output and the initial projection, preserving both localized and global context.

Gated Transformer-BiGRU (GTB) : Two initial Transformer encoder layers process the projected features, followed by parallel convolutions ( k ∈ {3,9}) that inform a gating mechanism. This gate balances the Transformer output against the input projection. A single BiGRU processes the resulting mixture, with final classification performed on the concatenated BiGRU and mixed features.

Input features for each residue were standardized via layer normalization 86 , projected to the backbone hidden dimension ( Table 2 ), and activated by Gaussian error linear units (GELUs) 87 . For GCB, the ten engineered sequence features were concatenated with the pLM embedding prior to normalization. A linear projection produced an unconstrained logit z i for residue i , mapped to an interaction probability via the standard sigmoid function (1 + exp(− z i )) −1 . Backbones were optimized jointly while pLM weights remained frozen.

## Optimization and checkpoint selection

Trainable parameters were optimized using AdamW 90 , with a weight decay of 10 −4 , a constant learning rate, a dropout proportion of 0.15, and gradient norm clipping of 5. Protein chains used for training were dynamically batched and sorted by length (maximum 12 contexts per batch), which restricted total padded tokens to ≤ 14,000 during training and ≤ 18,000 during validation. Zero-padded positions were masked out of attention operations and excluded from all loss and metric evaluations. Training proceeded for up to 16 epochs with an initial random seed of 0. Model checkpoints were monitored after each epoch using the chain-macro-AP metric on the validation split, and training was terminated after four consecutive epochs without performance gain. The checkpoint achieving peak validation chain-macro-AP value was retained for ensemble integration and threshold selection.

## Comparison of CHORUS with established methods

To benchmark CHORUS against established interface predictors, we evaluated published methods on the independent test partition (4,655 chain contexts, 2,186,751 residues) using unified reference interaction criteria. For structure-dependent predictors, 3D structures were predicted de novo from their sequences using ESMFold 51 (v1, chunk size 64) or ColabFold 74 (AlphaFold2-ptm). ## Representation ablations, ensemble controls, and error independence

To systematically investigate the determinants of model performance, we constructed three schemes for ablation studies as shown below.

Exploratory architecture screening : Initial tests on sets of ∼1,400 training and ∼300 validation chains were conducted for choosing three backbones from linear projections, MLPs, residual/dilated CNNs, U-Nets, BiGRUs, and Transformers.

Ensemble diversity controls : CHORUS was benchmarked against five controlled six-member variants: i ) variation of random seeds from 0 to 5 using ESM-2 layer 30, ii ) coordinate permutations, iii ) within-family depths (ESM-2 layers 0, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30), iv ) identical duplicates, and v ) a single-model baseline scaled to match the total number of parameters of our ensemble model (27.7M parameters vs. ∼24.4M across CHORUS backbones).

Synergy and prediction decorrelation : To assess the complementarity of individual models, we evaluated all 15 pairwise model combinations and six leave-one-out ensembles. Pairwise Pearson correlations between individual model predictions were computed, relating lower correlation to performance gains over the reference ensemble.

To validate macromolecular interface prediction on massive native assemblies, CHORUS was further benchmarked on large-scale respiratory complexes, including four porcine supercomplex cryo-EM structures comprising complexes I, III, and IV 96 (PDB codes: 8UGH, 8UGJ, 8UGN, 8UGR) and two human complex-IV respirasome assembly intermediates 97 (PDB codes: 9I6F, 9I7U). Transmembrane subunits were verified by mapping UniProt topological annotations (≥ 80% sequence identity, ≥ 70% helix coverage). Predictions were executed per full-length sequence in single-precision floating-point 32 on CPU without sequence padding or structural features. Interaction sites were determined by an inter-chain heavy-atom distance cutoff ≤ 5Å across both intra-complex and inter-complex interfaces. Performance was quantified across all resolved residues and annotated TM regions.


## Generalization to soluble protein interfaces

Model transferability outside the membrane domain was benchmarked across 19 non-membrane assemblies, including coiled-coil oligomers (cortexillin I, COMP, tetrabrachion) and enzyme-inhibitor complexes (eglin C, stefin B, Im9, ribonuclease inhibitor) ( Supplementary Tables 16-19 ). ## FunFam mapping and conservation analysis

Functional domains across the 70,247 unique transmembrane protein sequences were assigned by scanning against the CATH (version 4.3.0) 102 Functional Family (FunFam) hidden Markov model (HMM) library using HMMER (version 3.3.2) 103 hmmsearch --cut_tc --notextw. Candidate matches were resolved into non-overlapping domain assignments using cath-resolve-hits v0.16.10 104 , requiring a minimum HMM coverage of 80% and a minimum bit score of 25, with a long-domain preference of 2.0. Each query was indexed by the digest of its complete uppercase sequence. Ambiguous residue identifiers were replaced with X for the HMM search only, while the original residue identifiers were retained in the source-coordinate output.

For each validated domain, query residues aligned to HMM match states were mapped through the profile MAP architecture onto standardized FunFam multiple sequence alignment coordinates. Insertions, deletions, and residues flanking the aligned domain boundaries were treated as unobserved conservation states rather than assigned zero-conservation penalties. In the presence of tandem homologous domains within a single polypeptide, positional observations across domain copies were first sequence-wise averaged internally. The global assignment identified 56,221 structural domains across 30,799 unique sequences, spanning 9,564 distinct FunFams and 10,180,315 domain-mapped residues of which 10,018,715 positions aligned directly to consensus HMM match states.

Residue conservation was quantified using the PET91 Scorecons algorithm 105 . Homologous sequences within each FunFam alignment were realigned to their corresponding profile HMMs using hmmalign. Residue conservation was computed across consensus match columns parameterized by the pinned PET91mod.mat2 substitution matrix. In this framework, higher scores denote heightened evolutionary constraints at the corresponding functional consensus position. Continuous Scorecons served as the primary variable, with a predefined threshold of ≥ 0.7 denoting highly conserved functional positions in accordance with established CATH criteria 106 . To guarantee statistical stability and eliminate artifacts arising from shallow alignments, conservation evaluations were restricted to checksum-verified CATH alignments exhibiting a Diversity of Positional Score (DOPS) ≥ 70, a minimum representation of three exact sequences in the current dataset and validated HMM match-state coordinates. No conservation metrics were imputed for uncharacterized families. From the initial cache of 621 official alignments, 580 occurred within the assigned domain cohort. Excluding 69 alignments lacking sufficient sequence support yielded a final high-confidence benchmarking corpus of 9,529 unique sequences, 12,093 resolved domains, and 511 FunFams spanning 114 homologous superfamilies, comprising 2,739,713 Scorecons-annotated residues.

Enrichment of predicted quaternary interfaces within highly conserved positions (Scorecons ≥ 0.7) was tested against the fixed-margin hypergeometric null model, substituting verified ligand-binding sites with highly conserved residue annotations. Multi-compartment stratifications across cytoplasmic (2,878 sequences), extracellular/lumenal (1,802 sequences), transmembrane (7,374 sequences), and intramembrane microenvironments (319 sequences) utilized the identical UniProt topological hierarchy defined in the BioLiP benchmark, with corresponding high-conservation overlap cohorts comprising 3,445, 3,286, 8,349, and 434 sequences, respectively.

At the sub-domain and consensus-state levels, predicted probabilities and binary interaction classifications were averaged across tandem domain repeats and pooled across sequences occupying identical HMM match states. Within each FunFam, monotonic concordance between positional conservation and interface propensity was assessed via Spearman rank correlation across eligible states (supported by ≥ 3 exact sequences, ≥ 5 and ≥ 10 assessed as sensitivity controls), requiring at least ten scoreable states and non-zero variance. ## Statistical analysis

We strictly retained the hierarchical distinction between residues, chain contexts and sequence-similarity clusters across all evaluations. The primary benchmark metric was the unweighted mean AP values across eligible contexts. To quantify uncertainty, the resampling unit for bootstrap analyses was the intact sequence cluster, thereby preserving dependencies among its associated contexts and residue-level predictions. For paired method comparisons, identical bootstrap clusters were drawn across models to compute paired metric differences for each replicate. Confidence intervals (95% CIs) correspond to the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the empirical bootstrap distribution.

Specifically, ensemble controls, pairwise constituent comparisons and ablation analyses used 10,000 cluster bootstrap replicates (base random seed: 20260905, with contrast-specific offsets documented in the configuration repository), whereas length-stratified evaluations used 2,000 replicates. Candidate-selection benchmarks were performed over 10,000 replicates (seed: 20260822) with equal weighting assigned to cluster-level AP or Brier scores. For representation-performance regressions, validation clusters (supplying probe scores) and test clusters (supplying interaction-prediction scores) were resampled independently. Representation-level metrics were reconstructed and regressions refitted across 2,000 replicates (seed: 20260717). Reported confidence intervals were pointwise and unadjusted for various comparisons.

Biological subgroup distributions are reported as median cluster AP values with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and sample counts. The 57-candidate benchmark was evaluated using a single fitted model per candidate on a fixed partition of datasets. Variability across the three probe optimization seeds was quantified independently from resampling uncertainty of proteins and does not capture retraining variance of the interaction predictors.


## Software implementation and reproducibility

Data processing and analysis were implemented in Python, using TMKit 107 and PyPropel 108 for reusable sequence, structure and feature-processing operations, and Biopython 109 , NumPy 110 , Pandas 111 , and Scikit-learn 112 supported sequence alignment, numerical analysis, and model evaluation. Downstream prediction backbones and linear probes were implemented in PyTorch 113 . To extract frozen representations, we used fair-esm 2.0.0 (ESM2), Transformers 4.57.6 (ESM-C), and Transformers 5.13.0 with SentencePiece 0.2.1 (ProtT5) 114 . To account for dependency variations across workflows, software environments were recorded per experiment rather than assumed identical across all processing stages.

Model fitting and inference were conducted on NVIDIA A100 80-GB GPUs, whereas full-set evaluation across the development cohort was executed on an A800 GPU. Detailed hyperparameters, including numerical precision, batch size, random seeds, and contextual windowing, were specified in the corresponding sections above. To ensure full reproducibility, the accompanying repository documented the source configurations, model checkpoints, data splits, residue mappings, model outputs, and exact evaluation commands for each in silico testing round. Complete parameter settings and the external dataset registry were retained within this reproducibility bundle. Repository links and access details are specified in the Data and Code Availability statements upon release of this work for publication.
