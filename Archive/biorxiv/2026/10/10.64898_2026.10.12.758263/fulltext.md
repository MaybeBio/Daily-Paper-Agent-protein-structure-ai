## ABSTRACT

The design of antibody complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) is pivotal for therapeutic development, yet remains challenged by the complexity of the antibody–antigen interface. Existing methods, which largely rely on pairwise relationships, struggle to capture intrinsic multibody interactions and global structural constraints. To address this limitation, we propose the Relation-Aware Graph–Hypergraph Network (RA-GHN), a novel framework that systematically unifies graph-based pairwise modeling with biophysical hypergraphs to capture high-order co-operative effects. Specifically, we construct a heterogeneous hypergraph representation featuring three complementary hyperedge types: epitope–paratope interaction patches, spatial structural clusters, and high-degree hub structures, enabling a comprehensive encoding of interface physics. Architecturally, RA-GHN adopts a dual-pathway design that synergistically integrates a Relation-Aware Equivariant Graph Network (RA-EGN) with an Equivariant Hypergraph Encoder , strictly preserving SE (3)-equivariance throughout the sequence–structure co-design process. Furthermore, to mitigate folding hallucination and sequence overfitting commonly observed in generative models, we introduce the Structure–Sequence Decoupling Index (SSDI), which employs a harmonic mean to non-linearly balance structural physical reliability with sequence novelty. Extensive experiments demonstrate that RA-GHN significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines across standard benchmarks and affinity optimization tasks, validating that the fusion of high-order geometric modeling with rigorous evaluation criteria enables the generation of physically feasible and functionally innovative antibodies.

Dual equivariant pathways synergistically guarantee local geometry and global shape.

## 1. Introduction

Antibodies, as proteins of the immune system, safeguard the host by selectively attaching to antigens, such as viruses and bacteria, providing protection [ 1 ]. The interaction between antibodies and antigens is primarily governed by specific regions within the antibodies called complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) [ 2 ]. As shown in Fig. 1 , it has six hypervariable-loop regions (H1, H2, H3, L1, L2, L3) that are used to recognize and bind an extremely diverse repertoire of antigens. Therefore, in order to create medicinal antibodies capable of effectively targeting specific antigens, designing CDRs is the most crucial step [ 3 , 4 ].

CDR diversity is achieved through two key processes: V(D)J gene recombination prior to antigen exposure and somatic hypermutation occurring in B cells after antigen exposure [ 5 ]. In biological experiments, the diversification of CDR regions is primarily achieved through techniques such as random mutagenesis, targeted mutagenesis, and chain shuffling. Subsequently, libraries obtained from these diversification methods are screened using display technologies such as yeast or phage display to evaluate their expression and binding characteristics [ 6 ]. However, a CDR sequence containing L amino acids has up to 20 L possible protein sequences, making it impractical to test all potential sequences experimentally, and the small solution space that satisfies the desired constraints of binding affinity, stability, and synthesizability [ 2 , 7 ]. Therefore, computer-aided antibody design can effectively address this challenge.

Traditional computational approaches rely on sampling protein sequences and structures from complex theoretical energy functions (physics-based) or statistical (knowledge-based) energies [ 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ]. However, these methods typically suffer from long computational times and are susceptible to being trapped in local optima. Furthermore, no force field comprehensively captures the intricate interactions inherent in biological molecules. In recent years, with the rapid advancement of deep generative models, several approaches have emerged for antibody design and protein design [ 2 , 6 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 ]. Compared to traditional algorithms, deep generative models can directly capture the higher-order interactions between amino acids in antibodies and antigens, resulting in more effective antibody generation [ 17 ]. However, there is still a significant gap that needs to be addressed in order for generative models to become practical for antibody design.

Early studies of antibody design focused mainly on the generation of CDR sequences without simultaneously considering their corresponding structural information[ 13 , 14 , 18 ]. Subsequently, Jin et al.[ 19 ] proposed the RefineGNN method, which achieves joint design (co-design) of CDR sequences and structures, providing a new perspective for the coordinated optimization of antibody structure and function. The subsequent work HSRN[ 20 ] integrates the design and docking of antigen-binding antibodies within a hierarchical refinement framework. Building upon this, DiffAb[ 2 ] utilizes diffusion models [ 21 ] to iteratively generate CDR sequences, C α coordinates, and orientation information. Similarly, AbDiffuser[ 22 ] incorporates family-specific priors into the diffusion process, significantly enhancing both generation efficiency and quality compared to DiffAb. To improve computational efficiency, MEAN[ 23 ] replaces autoregressive decoding with progressive full-shot decoding and incorporates light chain information as a conditional input for CDR generation. This approach was later extended to dyMEAN[ 24 ], enabling end-to-end full-atom antibody design. A recent study RAAD[ 25 ] models each antigen–antibody complex as an Attributed Heterogeneous Graph (AHG), defining multiple edge types to enrich residue sequence and structural context. It performs message passing for each relation separately and integrates the results into a final representation. By taking the entire antigen as input and modeling the dynamics of the antigen-antibody interaction as an optimization problem of the edge relationship, RAAD significantly improves the sequence–structure co-design of antibodies.

However, as the conditional contexts become increasingly complex, such models still face significant challenges in effectively capturing the intricate structural and sequential interactions between CDRs and their contexts. The fundamental limitation lies in their insufficient geometric modeling capability—existing methods rely solely on local residues or pairwise relationships, making it difficult to fully capture multi-body interactions and global structural dependencies in antibody molecules, thereby limiting the representation of complex geometric constraints and sequence–structure cooperative effects.

To address the limitations of existing methods in antibody sequence–structure co-design, we propose a novel model—Relation-Aware Graph–Hypergraph Networks (RA-GHN). Unlike traditional approaches that rely solely on local residues or pairwise relationships, RA-GHN leverages the strengths of both graphs and hypergraphs to simultaneously capture second-order and higher-order multi-residue interactions within antibody molecules. Through this joint modeling strategy, the model can not only capture local dependencies between sequence and structure but also explicitly represent global geometric constraints and complex spatial conformations, providing a more comprehensive reflection of the functional and structural cooperativity of antibodies.

During message passing, RA-GHN performs relation-aware message passing for different types of edge relations and integrates contextual information from multiple relations into a final representation enriched with higher-order structural dependencies. This approach effectively overcomes the shortcomings of traditional methods in modeling long-sequence CDRs, complex geometric constraints, and multi-residue cooperative effects, significantly enhancing the representation of antibody sequence–structure interactions and providing a solid foundation for high-precision antibody design and optimization.

Specifically, we decompose antibody CDR design into two core stages: (i) Graph–Hypergraph Geometric Modeling and (ii) Sequence–Structure Co-Learning. In the geometric modeling stage, RA-GHN leverages graph structures to capture second-order residue interactions and uses hypergraphs to explicitly represent higher-order multi-residue interactions, thereby comprehensively modeling the complex spatial geometry and global constraints within antibody molecules. It is important to note that here, “complex spatial geometry” does not refer to protein conformational dynamics, but rather to the static geometric relationships and interaction patterns among residues within the antibody and the antigen–antibody complex. This approach simultaneously captures local dependencies within the residues of the antigen–antibody complex as well as global interactions between different components, providing a solid geometric foundation for sequence–structure co-design. In the sequence–structure co-learning stage, we introduce a relation-aware message passing mechanism that propagates information for different types of edge relations separately and integrates the contextual information into the final representation, enabling complementary optimization of sequence and structure features. This strategy avoids the need for computationally expensive explicit iterative refinement while effectively enhancing the model’s ability to represent complex multi-residue interactions. We evaluated RA-GHN on three challenging tasks: sequence and structure modeling, antigen-binding CDR design, and binding affinity optimization. Experimental results demonstrate that our model outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods across all tasks, validating its effectiveness and utility in antibody sequence–structure co-design.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

We propose the Relation-Aware Graph-Hypergraph Network (RA-GHN), a novel framework that systematically unifies graph-based pairwise residue interactions with hypergraph-based high-order cooperative effects. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first SE (3)-equivariant architecture to seamlessly integrate these dual geometric modalities for the simultaneous optimization of antibody CDR sequences and structures.

We construct a biophysically grounded hypergraph representation comprising three complementary classes of hyperedges: epitope–paratope interaction patches, spatial structural clusters, and high-degree interaction hubs. This multi-view formulation enables the explicit modeling of complex multi-body dependencies and global geometric constraints that are critical for shaping the protein–protein interaction interface.

We engineer a dual-pathway fusion mechanism that processes structural information in parallel via a Relation-Aware Equivariant Graph Network (RA-EGN) and an Equivariant Hypergraph Encoder . This design synergistically balances local bond geometry with global shape consistency while strictly guaranteeing rotational and translational equivariance throughout the generation process.

We introduce the Structure–Sequence Decoupling Index (SSDI) to address the limitations of existing evaluation metrics, specifically the risks of “folding hallucination” and sequence overfitting. By employing a harmonic mean to nonlinearly couple structural reliability with sequence novelty, SSDI provides a robust and discriminative criterion for identifying antibody designs that are both physically feasible and functionally innovative.


## 2. Preliminary

As illustrated in Fig. 1 , antibodies are Y-shaped proteins composed of heavy and light chains, each containing a variable domain ( V H / V L ). These domains feature hypervariable Complementarity-Determining Regions (CDRs) flanked by framework regions. Since the heavy-chain CDRs (H1–H3) are the primary determinants of antigen-binding specificity and affinity, this study focuses specifically on their computational design and optimization.

To capture the multi-scale interactions inherent in antigen–antibody binding, we propose a Hybrid Topology comprising two complementary components: 1) a Pairwise Relational Graph ( G ) for modeling pairwise relations; 2) a Biophysical Hypergraph ( H )for capturing high-order cooperative effects.


## 2.1. Pairwise Relational Graph

We adopt the heterogeneous graph formulation from RAAD [ 25 ] to represent the antigen–antibody complex as a collection of pairwise residue interactions . The complex is modeled as G = ( V , ℰ), where nodes V represent residues endowed with geometric and semantic features, and edges ℰ capture multi-scale interactions between node pairs .

Input features are designed to be invariant to rigid body transformations. Node features aggregate residue identity, sinusoidal positional encodings, and local backbone geometry (including angles and coordinates). Edge features capture relative geometric information, specifically RBF-encoded distances and quaternion-based relative orientations. To systematically model the hierarchy of interactions, edges are categorized into eight relation types covering sequential connectivity, intra/inter-chain spatial proximity, and global context connections [ 25 ]. This rigorous graph construction serves as the foundation for our subsequent hypergraph evolution.


## 2.2. Biophysical Hypergraph

To model the intricate, high-order cooperative effects inherent in antigen–antibody complexes—which fundamentally extend beyond simple pairwise interactions—we introduce a biophysically grounded hypergraph representation H = ( V , ℰ hyper ). In traditional graph-based molecular representations, edges are restricted to connecting exactly two nodes, implicitly assuming that thermodynamic and geometric contributions are purely additive. However, at protein–protein interfaces, binding affinity and structural specificity are frequently dictated by synergistic many-body interactions, such as cooperative hydrogen bond networks, dense multi-residue hydrophobic packing, and coordinated electrostatic environments. Unlike standard graphs, a hyperedge e ∈ ℰ hyper can concurrently encompass an arbitrary number of residue nodes v ∈ ℰ. This mathematical property provides a natural and highly expressive formulation for the many-body cooperativity prevalent at these complex binding interfaces.

To systematically construct this high-order topology, we design a biophysics-aware hyperedge constructor, denoted as Φ hyper . Rather than relying solely on naive global distance cutoffs, Φ hyper generates semantically and physically meaningful hyperedges tailored to the fundamental biophysical principles of antigen–antibody recognition. Specifically, we constructed three types of complementary hyperedges, each targeting a different aspect of the structure-energy interaction:we formulate three complementary classes of hyperedges, each addressing a distinct aspect of the structural and energetic interplay:

Epitope–Paratope Interaction Patches: Captures cross-chain spatial packing and shape complementarity directly across the binding boundary, modeling how local groups of residues function as integrated units to cooperatively accommodate specific antigen features.

Spatial Structural Clusters: Encapsulates dense, intra-chain topological packing. This captures the spatial aggregation necessary to stabilize local folding units and preserve the anchoring architecture of highly flexible Complementarity-Determining Regions (CDRs).

High-Degree Interaction Hubs: Identifies topologically central energetic hotspots using a graph-theoretic approach, establishing star-topology structures that efficiently route geometric and feature signals from critical binding residues to their localized neighborhoods.

By seamlessly integrating these three complementary hyperedge types, our representation mathematically guarantees that the model perceives the antibody–antigen complex not merely as a collection of isolated atomic contacts, but as a tightly coupled, heterogeneous biochemical system. The detailed construction algorithms and formulations for each hyperedge class are elaborated below.

The high-affinity and highly specific recognition between antibodies and antigens is rarely governed by isolated, independent residue–residue contacts. Instead, it relies heavily on the synergistic formation of local surface patches composed of multiple spatially proximal residues acting in concert. These interaction patches exhibit a profound degree of multi-dimensional complementarity—encompassing topological geometric shape (e.g., knob-into-hole packing), electrostatic potential pairing, and optimal hydrophobic/hydrophilic matching. Consequently, they constitute the fundamental functional and energetic units of the antigen–antibody binding interface. Modeling these molecular interactions solely at the level of pairwise residue contacts fundamentally limits the representational capacity of the network, as it is often insufficient to capture the highly cooperative nature of interface complementarity, such as multi-centered hydrogen bond networks or distributed hydrophobic interfaces.

To explicitly model this patch-level cooperativity at the antigen–antibody interface, we introduce cross-chain interaction patches (epitope–paratope patches) and formally represent them as hyperedges. This hyperedge simultaneously aggregates the target antigen residues that are spatially proximal to u across the binding interface, along with its structurally adjacent antibody residues. In dense interfacial regions, an unrestricted neighborhood search could lead to excessive patch sizes, which implicitly introduces redundant structural noise and exacerbates the risk of over-smoothing during message passing. Through this biophysically motivated construction, the model intrinsically treats the local geometric configuration, steric constraints, and chemical microenvironment of the binding interface as a single integrated functional unit, fundamentally departing from the limitations of independent pairwise representations. This patch-level modeling strategy substantially enhances the network’s capacity to explicitly capture holistic interface complementarity, synergistic energetic effects, and unique local geometric fingerprints (e.g., structural motifs distinct to specific paratope– epitope pairings). Consequently, it provides a significantly richer and more biophysically meaningful structural basis for downstream high-order information propagation, ultimately improving the discrimination of high-affinity binders from structural decoys.

To rigorously maintain the stability of a protein’s tertiary structure, spatially proximal residues are driven by thermodynamic imperatives—such as the burial of hydrophobic side chains—to cooperatively pack into compact structural clusters. Such spatial structural clusters play a paramount role in protein folding: they severely restrict the conformational entropy of both the backbone and side chains, stabilize local folding units (e.g., α - helices or dense β-sheet cores), and preserve the overall topological organization of the macromolecule. In the context of antibodies, although the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) inherently exhibit pronounced structural flexibility to accommodate diverse antigens, their stable and functional conformations (i.e., canonical structures) remain critically dependent on the ordered aggregation and rigid structural scaffolding provided by surrounding framework residues in three-dimensional space.

To explicitly characterize these intra-chain spatial aggregation effects, we employ a geometry-based greedy clustering algorithm to identify dense spatial structural clusters within the proteins and formally model them as hyperedges. To rigorously circumvent cross-chain topological interference and preserve the distinct conformational identity of each molecule prior to complex formation, the algorithm is executed independently on each distinct protein segment (i.e., the antibody heavy chain, light chain, and the target antigen). In the thermodynamic landscape of antigen–antibody binding interfaces, the distribution of binding free energy is notoriously highly localized. A small subset of critical residues—often referred to as binding hotspots—functions as the primary energetic anchors, contributing disproportionately to the overall binding affinity while surrounded by energetically neutral peripheral residues (akin to the biophysical “O-ring” theory). These hotspot residues are not only strategically located at key spatial positions but also exhibit exceptionally high centrality within the network topology; the density of non-covalent interactions among their immediate neighbors significantly exceeds the interface average. To explicitly endow the neural network with the ability to focus its receptive field on these critical regions, we introduce a topology-driven hub hyperedge construction strategy. This approach structurally incorporates high-degree residues and their local microenvironments into unified, attention-demanding hyperedge representations.

Specifically, we first construct an underlying interface interaction graph G int = ( V int , ℰ int ), where the node set V int represents all identified interface residues, and the edge set ℰ int encodes basic pairwise residue–residue interactions defined by spatial proximity. For each node v ∈ ℰ int , we compute its topological degree deg( v ) to quantitatively assess its network connectivity and centrality within the interfacial environment. This mathematical construction imposes a strict star-topology structure: the central energetic hub radiates outward to its immediate structural scaffolding. This topology acts as an efficient “information highway,” seamlessly routing geometric and biochemical signals from the critical hub directly to its surrounding microenvironment during message passing.

By formally introducing these hub hyperedges, the model is mathematically compelled to distinguish between the core energetic engines of the interface (hub residues and their densely packed neighborhoods) and the structurally supportive but energetically peripheral regions. Within the paradigm of graph neural network-based information propagation, hub hyperedges effectively prevent critical hotspot signals from being diluted or “drowned out” by the majority of less important residues during global feature aggregation. Instead, they amplify the structural contribution of key residues across multiple deep network layers, significantly enhancing the model’s sensitivity and predictive accuracy for binding free energy estimation and hotspot identification. Furthermore, this topological approach is intrinsically orthogonal and naturally compatible with our geometry-based hyperedges (i.e., patch-based units and spatial structural clusters). Together, they coalesce into a unified, heterogeneous hypergraph architecture that simultaneously captures localized geometric constraints, chemical complementarity, and global topological centrality, thereby establishing an exceptionally rich and biophysically coherent representation for the high-order structural modeling of antigen–antibody interactions.

It mathematically guarantees that the generated conformations simultaneously satisfy rigorous local chemical validity (e.g., ideal bond lengths and absent steric clashes) and holistic interface compatibility.

## 4. Experiment setup


## 4.1. Experimental Design

Extensive experiments are conducted to systematically evaluate the proposed model from 4 aspects: (1) Sequence and structure modeling; (2) Antigen-binding CDR-H3 generation; (3) Antibody optimization tasks; (4) Ablation analysis of key model components.


## 4.2. Data Preparation

We systematically evaluate the Sequence and structure modeling performance of RA-GHN on the Structural Antibody Database (SAbDab)[ 3 ]. After excluding structures with missing light chains or antigens, the dataset comprises a total of 3,127 antigen–antibody complexes. To reduce data redundancy and mitigate bias arising from high sequence similarity, CDR sequences are first clustered using MMseqs2[ 26 ]. Antibodies with CDR sequence identity greater than 40%, measured by the sequence identity metric[ 27 ], are assigned to the same cluster[ 23 ]. Following preprocessing, 765, 1,093, and 1,659 clusters are obtained for CDR-H1, CDR-H2, and CDR-H3, respectively, which are then split into training, validation, and test sets using an 8:1:1 ratio.

To evaluate the Antigen-binding CDR-H3 generation performance of RA-GHN on antigen-related CDR-H3 design, we use the RAbD dataset[ 10 ] as an independent test benchmark, which contains 60 representative antibody– antigen complexes. Training data were curated from SAbDab with a strict data leakage prevention protocol, in which all antibodies whose CDR-H3 clusters overlapped with the RAbD test set were excluded. The remaining data were randomly split into training and validation sets at a 9:1 ratio.

One of the central goals of antibody design is to simultaneously enhance binding affinity and binding specificity. To systematically evaluate the performance of RA-GHN in antibody optimization, we first pre-train the antibody generation model F Θ on the SAbDab dataset, with the training and validation sets split at a ratio of 9:1. Subsequently, the pre-trained model is further fine-tuned on the SKEMPI V2.0 dataset[ 28 ], which contains 53 antigen–antibody complexes, to adapt it to the antibody optimization task.


## 4.3. Implementation detail

The following hyperparameters are determined using the AutoML toolkit NNI, with the corresponding search spaces defined as follows: the Adam optimizer is adopted with a learning rate lr = {0.0003, 0.001}, batch size B = {4, 8, 16}, and training epochs E train = 20; the embedding dimension for E type ( v i ) is set to F e = 32; the hidden dimension is selected from F = {128, 256}, and the number of network layers is L = {3, 4}.

For fair comparison, we select the model checkpoint with the lowest validation loss for evaluation during testing. Moreover, all baseline methods and our approach are implemented using the standard PyTorch 1.8.0 framework. Experiments are conducted on a high-performance computing server equipped with an AMD EPYC 7742 processor (64 cores and 128 threads, base frequency 1.5 GHz and maximum boost frequency 2.25 GHz) and four NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 80 GB memory each.


## 4.4. Baselines

We compare the proposed RA-GHN with a range of representative state-of-the-art methods, including LSTM[ 14 ], RefineGNN[ 19 ], HERN [ 20 ], DiffAb[ 2 ], AbDiffuser[ 22 ], MEAN[ 23 ], dyMEAN[ 24 ] RAAD[ 25 ], and ADesigner[ 29 ]. Since LSTM, RefineGNN, and HERN model only partial contexts of the antigen–antibody complex (with LSTM and RefineGNN using only the antibody heavy chain and HERN using only the antigen), we additionally compare three extended variants—C-LSTM, C-RefineGNN, and C-HERN—by providing the complete antigen–antibody complex as input, ensuring a fair and consistent comparison across methods.


## 5. Results and Discussion


## 5.1. Sequence and Structure Modeling

We adopt Amino Acid Recovery (AAR) and Root Mean Squared Deviation (RMSD) as evaluation metrics for sequence generation and structure prediction, respectively, and report the overall results based on 10-fold cross-validation in Table 1 .

As detailed in Table 1 , RA-GHN achieves a superior trade-off between sequence recovery and structural accuracy, demonstrating clear advantages across biologically critical metrics. Specifically, the model attains the lowest RMSD across all CDR loops; compared with the strongest baseline, RADD, it reduces structural error by 15.58%, 22.22%, and 6.81% on CDR-H1, CDR-H2, and CDR-H3, respectively. From a biophysical perspective, this pronounced reduction in RMSD implies that the predicted structures reside closer to the global energy minimum, effectively mitigating high-energy steric clashes associated with misfolded states. Such geometric precision is strongly correlated with antibody developability, significantly reducing the risk of aggregation and instability during downstream experimental characterization.

Crucially, RA-GHN validates its effectiveness on the CDR-H3 loop, the most hypervariable region and the primary determinant of antigen-binding specificity. While canonical CDR-H1 and CDR-H2 loops are structurally conserved and amenable to statistical memorization, CDR-H3 requires modeling complex, long-range dependencies. RA-GHN achieves both the highest AAR (38.15%) and the lowest RMSD (1.78 Å) on this challenging region, confirming that the Equivariant Hypergraph Encoder effectively captures multi-body interactions beyond simple distribution fitting. Although the model exhibits a marginal decrease in AAR on the conserved CDR-H1 and CDR-H2 loops (∼2%) compared to baselines, this is outweighed by a substantial reduction in structural error (∼20%). This result highlights a strategic prioritization of functional feasibility: RA-GHN effectively bridges the gap between sequence and structure, ensuring that generated antibodies are not merely native-like in sequence but are physically realizable and structurally stable.

Modeling the hypervariable CDR-H3 region presents a fundamental dichotomy: metrics emphasizing amino acid recovery (AAR) bias models toward conservative memorization of native sequences, whereas metrics relying solely on RMSD are prone to “folding hallucination”—a failure mode in which generated sequences yield deceptively low-RMSD structures despite lacking the biophysical properties required for stability. ## 5.4. Ablation Study

We systematically conduct an ablation study on the sequence and structure modeling task to evaluate the impact of incorporating hyperstructures and to analyze the efficacy of distinct hyperedge combinations. The experimental settings span a comprehensive spectrum, ranging from single-view modeling to multi-view fusion.

As illustrated in Fig. 5 , the ablation study elucidates the critical interplay between functional modeling and geometric constraints. The results indicate that explicit epitope modeling is the primary driver of sequence recovery: the Epitope-only setting achieves a remarkable AAR of 38.10%, confirming that paratope–epitope interactions provide the decisive signal for decoding binding specificity. Conversely, relying solely on geometric constraints (Spatial+Hub) leads to over-regularization; although this configuration minimizes RMSD (1.778 Å), it incurs a noticeable drop in AAR (37.12%). This observation suggests that purely geometric optimization tends to “over-smooth” sequence distributions, thereby discarding functionally essential residues that may be structurally non-ideal.

The Hybrid (RA-GHN) framework effectively resolves this tension. By fusing the semantic guidance of the epitope view with the physical precision of the spatial-hub views, it achieves the best overall performance (AAR: 38.15%, RMSD: 1.780 Å). This result demonstrates that our multi-view hypergraph does not merely trade off between competing objectives, but instead synergistically optimizes both biological function and physical structural integrity.

We conducted a sensitivity analysis on α, the weight of the coordinate loss, to determine the optimal balance between sequence generation and structure prediction ( Figure 6 ). Initially, increasing α from 0.1 to 0.2 yields a synergistic improvement, culminating in a peak AAR of 38.15% and a competitive RMSD of 1.778 Å at α = 0.2. This result suggests that moderate structural supervision acts as a beneficial regularizer, pruning biologically implausible conformations and thereby facilitating accurate sequence decoding.

However, a divergence emerges beyond α = 0.3. Although α = 0.7 achieves the lowest RMSD (1.760 Å), it incurs a significant drop in AAR (37.43%). This decline indicates structural over-regularization: excessive coordinate supervision drives the model toward rigid, “safe” mean-field conformations, suppressing the subtle, energetically driven sequence variations required for specific antigen binding. Consequently, we select α = 0.2 as the optimal configuration, prioritizing binding specificity (AAR) while maintaining robust structural precision.


## 6. Conclusion

In this work, we presented RA-GHN, a novel Relation-Aware Graph–Hypergraph Network designed to systematically address the challenges in antibody sequence–structure co-design. By fusing pairwise relation graphs with biophysical hypergraphs, RA-GHN explicitly models complex multi-body interactions and global geometric constraints at the antibody–antigen interface, effectively overcoming the limitations of conventional pairwise methods. The architecture employs a dual-pathway mechanism that coordinates the optimization of residue representations and three-dimensional coordinates while strictly ensuring SE (3)-equivariance. Furthermore, to mitigate the “folding hallucination” issue prevalent in generative antibody design, we introduce the Structure–Sequence Decoupling Index (SSDI). This metric balances physical reliability with sequence novelty, providing a rigorous and discriminative criterion for model assessment. Extensive experiments on SAbDab, RAbD, and SKEMPI demonstrate that RA-GHN significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines in CDR modeling, antigen-binding structure generation, and affinity optimization, confirming that incorporating high-order geometric constraints yields antibodies that are both physically feasible and functionally potent.

Despite these advancements, several avenues for further exploration remain. Future work will extend the current static modeling paradigm to account for conformational dynamics and induced-fit mechanisms. In addition, we plan to generalize the proposed graph–hypergraph architecture to broader protein design tasks, such as nanobody engineering, and to incorporate multi-objective constraints (e.g., solubility and immunogenicity). Ultimately, coupling this computational framework with high-throughput wet-lab screening will be essential for realizing a closed-loop design cycle that translates theoretical predictions into clinically viable therapeutics.
